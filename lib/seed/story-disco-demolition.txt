Due To Excessive Disco. Or, Who Let a DJ Blow Up Center Field?

## 1979
Okay. Quick context, for anybody who wasn't alive, or was alive and has chosen to forget.
In 1979, disco is everywhere. Saturday Night Fever came out two years earlier and basically turned the whole country into a dance floor with a mirror ball over it. Disco on the radio. Disco on TV. Disco versions of songs that had no business being disco.
And if you were a nineteen year old kid in Chicagoland who'd spent your paper route money on Led Zeppelin records, you felt like the world had moved on without asking you.
That kid had a guy. And that guy was on the radio.

## THE DJ
His name is Steve Dahl. And in early 1978 he comes to Chicago from Detroit radio, where he'd been doing well enough that a Chicago station, WDAI, offers to double his salary to come over and be their rock morning guy.
So he comes. He starts in February 1978. Things are going great.
And then, on Christmas Eve 1978, WDAI switches its format. From rock. To disco.
And they fire him. On Christmas Eve.
[FILL: Everybody who's ever been laid off by email, sit with that for a second. This man got fired on Christmas Eve so a radio station could play "Le Freak."]
So Steve Dahl lands across town at WLUP, The Loop, 97.9 on your dial. And he decides he's going to spend the rest of his life, or at least 1979, making the people who fired him sorry.
He starts calling his old station "Disco D.I.E." on the air. He mocks disco records every morning. He plays a disco song and then "blows it up" on the air with a sound effect, and his catchphrase for it is "blow 'em up real good," which he swiped from an SCTV sketch.
He does parody songs. The big one is "Do You Think I'm Disco?", which is Rod Stewart's "Da Ya Think I'm Sexy?" rewritten about a guy whose whole personality is the dance floor. And this is the part that gets me: it comes out as a real single, on a real label, and it actually charts. The B-side is called "Coho Lip Blues."
Because he also has an army. His listeners. He calls them the Insane Coho Lips, and the whole idea is they are at war with disco.
[RIFF: the name "Insane Coho Lips." Or use this:]
I've read about the Insane Coho Lips a bunch of times now and I still can't tell you exactly what it means. That's how you know it was 1979 and a radio guy came up with it at five in the morning.
So that's one half of this story. A guy with a microphone, a grudge, and a few hundred thousand people listening to him every morning.
Now here's the other half.

## THE VEECKS
[CLIP: Bill Veeck's wooden leg ashtray]
The White Sox in 1979 are owned by Bill Veeck. And you have to understand Bill Veeck, because he's one of the most interesting people who ever owned a baseball team, and also because if you understand him, everything that happens next makes sense.
Bill Veeck's dad was president of the Cubs. So he grows up in baseball, here in Chicago, and as a kid he's working at Wrigley Field.
In World War Two he's in the Marines, and in the South Pacific a gun recoils and crushes his leg. Over the years he has surgery after surgery on it, and the number you see over and over is thirty six, and eventually the leg comes off. So he's got a wooden leg.
And Bill Veeck, a heavy smoker, cuts a hole in his wooden leg. And uses it as an ashtray.
That's the guy. That's the owner. That's who's in charge of this building.
And as an owner, Veeck is the king of promotions. Back in 1951, when he owns the St. Louis Browns, he sends a guy named Eddie Gaedel up to bat. Eddie Gaedel is three foot seven. Wears number one eighth on his back. Crouches down. The strike zone is about the size of a cigarette pack. He walks on four pitches.
At Comiskey, Veeck puts in the exploding scoreboard: a White Sox guy hits a home run, and the scoreboard shoots off fireworks and plays sirens. He put names on the back of jerseys, which every team does now. He put the White Sox in shorts.
[RIFF: the White Sox in shorts. Or use this:]
Grown men. Major league ballplayers. Sliding into second base in shorts. I'm a Chicago guy, I'd defend this city in any bar in America, and I've got nothing for the shorts.
And here's one more Bill Veeck thing, because it pays off later. You know Harry Caray singing "Take Me Out to the Ball Game" in the seventh inning? That started on the South Side, before he was the Cubs guy. Harry used to sing it in the booth, to himself, and the fans under the booth sang along. And the story goes that Veeck had a microphone hidden in the booth and flipped it on, so one night Harry's voice came booming out over the whole park. Veeck's version of how that happened changed a little every time he told it. But that's the kind of owner he was. If something fun is happening in a corner of the ballpark, turn it up and point everybody at it.
So Bill Veeck believes, all the way down, that a ballgame is a show. And in 1979 the White Sox are not a great team, and people are not exactly lining up to see them, so the show is doing a lot of work. Mike Veeck said it flat out years later: "We were not doing well."
That's Bill's son. Mike Veeck. Who works for the team running promotions.

## THE IDEA
So you've got Steve Dahl at war with disco, on a station that's 97.9 on the dial. And you've got Mike Veeck, looking for a way to fill seats on a Thursday night.
Here's how they find each other. A sales guy at The Loop, Jeff Schwartz, tells Mike that Dahl wants to blow up a pile of disco records. The original plan, the way it's been told, was to do it at a shopping mall. And Mike Veeck hears "a guy wants to blow something up in public" and thinks: not a mall. A ballpark. My ballpark.
And Mike goes to Dahl and makes the pitch himself. Dahl's on board. Dahl's partner on the air, Garry Meier, is in too.
The plan comes together like this. July 12th, a twi-night doubleheader, White Sox and Tigers. Bring a disco record to the gate, and you get in for ninety eight cents. Because The Loop is 97.9. Get it.
Your records go in a big crate. Between games, Steve Dahl walks out to center field. And blows them up.
Now, how many people did they think were coming? Here's where it gets good. Dahl said years later the Sox drew "maybe 9,000 people on a good night." The way most accounts tell it, the hope was something like twenty thousand. A big night. A nice bump. And security got staffed for a crowd a lot smaller than what was about to happen, with most of the people working the gates.
[FILL: Accounts disagree on the exact number they were expecting. Every version agrees on one thing: it wasn't this.]

## THE CROWD
Okay. So. July 12th.
And it turns out that every single rock kid in Chicagoland, and northwest Indiana, and a good chunk of Wisconsin, heard about this and thought, "Ninety eight cents? To watch a guy blow up disco? I'm in."
The official attendance is 47,795. In a park that held about 44,500.
And that's the official number. The estimates of how many people were really there, inside and outside, go a lot higher. Mike Veeck thought it might have been as many as sixty thousand. One account says there were around twenty thousand more people outside who couldn't get in. Some of them got in anyway. Rusty Staub, who played for the Tigers that night, is quoted telling the New York Times: "People brought ladders. They were climbing in from the outside." That's how the quote gets passed around, anyway. Ladders. To a baseball game.
And the records. The crate fills up fast. So a lot of people don't put their record in the crate. They keep it. They're holding it. In their hands. In the stands. For a whole baseball game.
You can guess.
So game one starts, and the Tribune said it was played "under a constant bombardment of records and firecrackers." They had to stop play more than once.
And the Tigers' Rusty Staub, again, this guy has the best seat for all of it, said the records would come slicing down and stick in the ground. Stick. Like a throwing star. He said he begged his teammates to put on batting helmets. In the field. Defense, in a batting helmet, because the fans are throwing Bee Gees records at your head.
Ron LeFlore, the Tigers center fielder, said a golf ball came out of the stands and bounced between his legs while he was trying to catch a fly ball.
[RIFF: playing outfield while the crowd throws vinyl. Or use this:]
You're a major league outfielder. You've spent your whole life learning to track a fly ball. And now you have to track the fly ball, and the golf ball, and forty copies of "Disco Inferno" coming in sideways like they were thrown by ninjas.
And on top of that: there are firecrackers. There's beer. There is a smell in the air that is not entirely tobacco.
The Tigers manager, Sparky Anderson, had the best line about that part. He told the Tribune, "Beer and baseball go together." And then: "I think those kids were doing things other than beer."
Oh, and there was a baseball game. The Tigers won it, 4 to 1. Least important thing that happened in the building.

## THE BOOM
Between games. Steve Dahl comes out. Through a door in the center field wall, with Garry Meier, and with Lorelei from The Loop. And Dahl is dressed in army fatigues and a helmet. Because it's a war, remember. They circle the field in a Jeep, and the crowd throws firecrackers and beer at them, which in this context is love.
He gets to center field, gets on the mic, and he tells them, more or less word for word: this is now officially the world's largest anti-disco rally. We took all the disco records you brought tonight, we got them in a giant box, and we're going to blow them up real good.
And then he leads the chant. "Disco sucks." "Disco sucks." Tens of thousands of people.
[RIFF: what that chant sounds like in a ballpark. Or use this:]
I've been at Sox games where the whole park is chanting. It shakes your ribs. Now make it fifty thousand people who've been drinking since four in the afternoon, and the chant is about the Village People.
And then he hits it.
[Pause.]
And it is a real explosion. Not a sound effect this time. The crate blows. It tears a hole in center field. Vinyl goes everywhere, and chunks of the field go with it. There's smoke. There's fire.
And then they come over the walls.

## THE FIELD
[CLIP: The kid who stole second base]
Thousands of people. Some accounts say something like seven thousand. Pour out of the stands and onto the field.
And this is where I went digging, because "fans rushed the field" is not a story. Who? What did they do? So let me give you some of the guys.
There was a teenage vendor working the stands that night named Dave Gaborek. And Dave remembers one long-haired kid who jumps down out of left field, runs across the whole outfield, slides into second base, picks the bag up off the ground, and waves it over his head.
That's it. That's the guy. Slid into second like he'd earned it, and then stole it. Not the base. The actual base.
And he wasn't alone. People ran the bases like they were in the World Series. Sliding into home. Standing on the mound. A future Tribune sportswriter named Paul Sullivan was a kid in that crowd, and he's described it: running around on a major league field, sliding into the bases, standing on the pitcher's mound. He's also said he got off that field early, and that 1968 had something to do with it. The convention riots. Chicago kids remembered what happens when the cops show up to a crowd. Smart kid.
A Chicago radio sports guy named Les Grobstein was there. Before he left the field, he dug up a piece of the turf and took it home. A souvenir. Of grass.
And here's my favorite one. There was a twenty one year old from the South Side in that crowd named Michael Clarke Duncan. Yes. The big guy from The Green Mile. Oscar nominee. And by his own telling, he was in the first hundred or so people onto the field. He slid into third base. And he said he grabbed a bat out of the White Sox dugout on the way.
And then, years later, he admitted that he was actually a big disco fan.
[RIFF: Michael Clarke Duncan at Disco Demolition. Or use this:]
He didn't even hate disco. He just heard there was a riot on the South Side and didn't want to miss it. That's the most Chicago thing I've ever heard. "I don't agree with the cause, but I'm not missing this."
Then the bigger stuff. Somebody pulled up the bases. All of them. A photographer named Paul Natkin, who was shooting it that night, put it simply: "They pulled all the bases up. They tipped over the batting cage." One account from the crowd says somebody dragged the batting cage out and set it on fire.
Tigers pitcher Jack Morris told the New York Times years later that "all hell broke loose." Fans were tearing up the pitching rubber and digging up the dirt. People were going after home plate.
They're tearing up the grass. They're tearing out seats. They're lighting bonfires in the outfield, with records, and whatever else they can find.
Meanwhile, the White Sox pitcher who was supposed to start game two, Ken Kravec, is out there warming up when this starts. And the way it's been told, he's got shoes and records flying past his head. He goes back to the dugout. Everybody goes back to the dugout. The Tigers are in their dugout. The White Sox are in their dugout. Nobody is going out there.
And the people in charge are trying. The scoreboard flashes "PLEASE RETURN TO YOUR SEATS." Nothing.
Bill Veeck, the man with the wooden leg, goes out there himself, with a microphone, and stands about where home plate used to be, and begs people to go back to the stands, while a bonfire is going in center field. Steve Dahl gets on the mic. And Harry Caray. Remember Harry, the guy Bill Veeck turned into the seventh inning sing-along? Harry gets on the PA too. And there's an account that they played "Take Me Out to the Ball Game" to try to settle everybody down.
It did not settle everybody down.
[RIFF: Harry Caray asking politely. Or use this:]
If Harry Caray can't get you to behave, nobody can. That's a man who could get forty thousand people to sing in the seventh inning. And he couldn't get them off the grass.
Finally, the Chicago police come in. In riot gear. And one account says the people still in the stands, the ones who'd just come to see a baseball game, applauded when they showed up.
The field gets cleared. Thirty nine people get arrested, mostly for disorderly conduct. Some accounts say thirty seven. Nobody was seriously hurt, which, when you look at the pictures, is the real miracle of the night.

## THE FORFEIT
Now. There's supposed to be a second game.
And the White Sox want to play it. Bill Veeck looks at the field, and the field looks like a battlefield, there's a crater in center, and it's still smoking. And he wants to play.
The Tigers manager, Sparky Anderson, says no. He's not putting his guys out there. It's not safe. He wants the game.
The umpires call it off for the night. And the next day the American League president, Lee MacPhail, gives the game to Detroit. A forfeit. 9 to 0.
The White Sox forfeited a home game. Because their own promotion worked too well.
The Sox head groundskeeper, Roger Bossard, said decades later that he still has nightmares about what they did to his field.
And a Tribune columnist, David Israel, wrote the line that should be on the plaque: "Ten years after Woodstock, there was Veeckstock."
[FILL: As of right now, that's still the last forfeited game in American League history. Nobody in the league has forfeited a game since. The White Sox have one because a guy blew up a crate of Donna Summer records.]

## THE OLDER COUSIN
[CLIP: Ten Cent Beer Night]
And here's the thing that makes me crazy. Baseball had already learned this lesson five years earlier. And nobody listened.
June 4th, 1974. Cleveland. The Indians decide to have Ten Cent Beer Night. Ten cents. For a cup of beer. With basically no limit on how many you could buy.
They get 25,134 people, about twice what they expected. There are streakers. There are fans running onto the field all night.
And in the ninth inning, with the game tied, a nineteen year old kid jumps out of the stands, runs up to the Texas Rangers right fielder, Jeff Burroughs, and steals the hat right off his head. Burroughs goes down. And the Rangers manager, Billy Martin, thinks his guy got jumped, and he tells his team, by one player's memory, "Let's go get them, boys!" And the Rangers come out of the dugout carrying bats.
And then the Cleveland players come out with bats too. To save the other team. From their own fans.
The umpire, Nestor Chylak, got hit in the head with part of a stadium seat. He forfeited the game to Texas, and he called the fans "uncontrollable beasts," and said he'd never seen anything like it except in a zoo.
[RIFF: Ten Cent Beer Night vs. Disco Demolition. Or use this:]
So in 1974 baseball found out what happens when you hand a crowd cheap beer. And in 1979 the White Sox said, "Got it. What if we also hand them explosives and a stack of frisbees?"
Disco Demolition is Ten Cent Beer Night's younger brother. Same family. Same forfeit. Worse haircut.

## THE ARGUMENT
Now I have to slow down here, because this isn't only a funny story, and it would be bullshit of me to pretend it is.
There's a big argument about what this night was really about. And it's not settled.
One side of it: disco in 1979 is music that came up out of Black clubs, Latino clubs, and gay clubs. And a stadium full of mostly white kids chanting that it sucks and blowing it up can look like something uglier than a radio feud.
And there's a man named Vince Lawrence who was there. He was a teenager, fourteen or fifteen depending on the account, working that night as an usher at Comiskey, collecting records. He was one of the few Black people in the building, by his own telling. He took the job because he was saving up to buy a synthesizer.
And he's said for years that a lot of the records people were bringing weren't disco at all. He's named Tyrone Davis records. Curtis Mayfield. Otis Clay. Soul and R&B. The thing they had in common, he says, was that they were by Black artists.
The other side: Steve Dahl has always denied there was anything racist or homophobic about it. He's said it was about the music, and about a radio station that fired him. And plenty of the kids there would tell you they just liked rock and roll and thought ninety eight cents was a good deal.
I can't settle that for you. People who were there disagree. I'm telling you both sides because they're both on the record.
And here's the part I love, and it's true. Vince Lawrence, the kid who was saving for a synthesizer, got that synthesizer. And a few years later he co-wrote "On and On," which is one of the first house music records ever made. Here. In Chicago.
So the same night that was supposed to kill dance music, one of the guys who'd help invent the next kind of dance music was standing in the aisle in an usher's uniform.
[FILL: Disco didn't die in center field. It moved a few miles away, got a drum machine, and changed its name to house.]

## WHERE ARE THEY NOW
So what happened to everybody?
Steve Dahl's career took off after that night. He was on Chicago radio for decades. He's said he thought disco was probably on its way out anyway, and that the night just sped it up. And in 2016 he put out a whole book about it, "Disco Demolition: The Night Disco Died," full of photos and stories from people who were there.
Bill Veeck took the public heat. He stood in front of the press and took it, so his kid wouldn't have to. He sold the team not long after, and he's in the Hall of Fame now, for everything else he did. Eddie Gaedel, the scoreboard, the shorts. Mostly not this.
And Mike Veeck. The son. The promotions guy.
Mike's career went the opposite way from Dahl's. He was out at the White Sox before the next season. And he's always believed he got blackballed. Nobody in the majors wanted the guy who forfeited a ballgame.
And it got bad. He's talked about it himself. Drinking. A divorce. His dad dying. One profile says he lost his savings betting on jai alai, which is the most 1980s Florida sentence I've ever read.
Twenty years. That's how long it took before a major league team hired him again, the Tampa Bay Devil Rays, in 1999.
But here's the thing. He never stopped doing promotions. He just did them in the minor leagues, where nobody could stop him. He and a group that included Bill Murray, yes, that Bill Murray, from Wilmette, bought a minor league team in 1990. And he ended up running the St. Paul Saints, which he co-owns with Murray and others.
And he got weirder. In Charleston, South Carolina, his team held "Nobody Night." They padlocked the gates and made the fans wait in the parking lot until the fifth inning so they could set the record for the lowest attendance in baseball history. Official attendance: zero. He also announced a Vasectomy Night for Father's Day. That one got cancelled within hours.
And in 2001, when he was working for the Marlins, on the anniversary of Disco Demolition, they finally got him to apologize. To disco. He stood at home plate and delivered an apology to KC, of KC and the Sunshine Band. And he ended it with: "I'm sorry from the bottom of my leisure suit."
There's a whole Netflix documentary about him now, "The Saint of Second Chances." And he's said that Disco Demolition made him great at what he does.

## THAT GUY
[CLIP: That Guy verdict]
Okay. Time to name him. That Guy.
The suspects.
Suspect one: Steve Dahl. The man in the helmet. Who got fired on Christmas Eve, turned his whole radio show into a war, told his army to show up, and pressed the button.
Suspect two: Bill Veeck. The owner. Who spent thirty years teaching baseball fans that anything can happen at the ballpark, and then got surprised when anything happened at the ballpark.
Suspect three: Mike Veeck. The son. Who heard "this guy wants to blow up records at a mall" and said, "What if it was in center field, between games, in front of every rock kid in three states, for less than a dollar?"
And the verdict.
Steve Dahl did exactly what Steve Dahl said he was going to do. He said it on the air, every morning, for months. He's a radio guy. His whole job is getting people riled up. You don't get to be surprised when the radio guy riles people up.
The guy whose job it was to think about what happens when fifty thousand people show up for a promotion built for twenty thousand, and they're all holding frisbees made of vinyl, and the security is standing at the gates, and there's a bomb in center field, is the promotions guy.
So That Guy is Mike Veeck.
[THAT GUY STING]
And honestly, his dad is on the hook too. Mike learned it all from Bill. So it's a family business. A father and son, and a wooden leg full of cigarette ash.
But Mike's the one who paid for it. Twenty years of it. And then he went to the minor leagues and kept doing the exact same kind of thing, just with smaller crowds and no explosives. That's not a lesson learned. That's a guy who found a league that would let him keep going.

Dahl lit the fuse. But Mike Veeck sold fifty thousand tickets to the fuse for ninety eight cents each.
