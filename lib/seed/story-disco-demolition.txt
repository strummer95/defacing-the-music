## 1979
Okay. Quick context, for anybody who wasn't alive, or was alive and has chosen to forget.
In 1979, disco is everywhere. Saturday Night Fever turned the whole country into a dance floor with a mirror ball over it. Disco on the radio. Disco on TV. Disco versions of songs that had no business being disco.
And if you were a nineteen year old kid in Chicagoland who'd spent your paper route money on Led Zeppelin records, you felt like the world had moved on without asking you.
That kid had a guy. And that guy was on the radio.

## THE DJ
His name is Steve Dahl. In early 1978 he comes to Chicago from Detroit radio, because a Chicago station, WDAI, offers to double his salary to be their rock morning guy.
He starts in February 1978. Things are going great.
And then, on Christmas Eve 1978, WDAI switches its format. From rock. To disco.
And they fire him. On Christmas Eve.
[FILL: Everybody who's ever been laid off by email, sit with that for a second. Christmas Eve.]
The biggest disco song in the country that winter was "Le Freak," by Chic. And how that song got written is its own story. A wild one. We're saving it for a future episode. All I'll tell you now is it starts with the two guys who wrote it standing outside a nightclub door on New Year's Eve, getting told no.
So Steve Dahl lands across town at WLUP, The Loop, 97.9 on your dial. And he decides he's going to spend the rest of his life, or at least 1979, making the people who fired him sorry.
He calls his old station "Disco D.I.E." on the air. He mocks disco records every morning. He plays a disco song and then "blows it up" with a sound effect, and his catchphrase for it is "blow 'em up real good," which he swiped from an SCTV sketch.
And he gives his listeners a name. They're not fans. They're an army, at war with disco. He calls them the Insane Coho Lips.
Dahl's explained it himself. One of the big street gangs in Chicago back then was the Insane Unknowns. And every morning he drove past Burnham Harbor, where the coho salmon boats went out on Lake Michigan. A gang and a fish.
So that's the army. A bunch of Led Zeppelin kids named after a gang and a salmon. Disco never stood a chance.
He even puts out a parody single, "Do You Think I'm Disco?", and it actually charts. The B-side is called "Coho Lip Blues." Of course it is.
That's one half of this story. A guy with a microphone, a grudge, and a few hundred thousand people listening every morning.

## THE VEECKS
[CLIP: Bill Veeck's wooden leg ashtray]
The White Sox in 1979 are owned by Bill Veeck. And if you understand Bill Veeck, everything that happens next makes sense.
His dad was president of the Cubs, so he grew up in baseball, right here.
In World War Two he's in the Marines, and in the South Pacific a gun recoils and crushes his leg. After thirty six surgeries, the leg comes off. So he's got a wooden leg.
And Bill Veeck, a heavy smoker, cuts a hole in his wooden leg. And uses it as an ashtray.
That's the owner. That's who's in charge of this building.
And as an owner, Veeck is the king of promotions. Back in 1951, when he owns the St. Louis Browns, he sends a guy named Eddie Gaedel up to bat. Eddie Gaedel is three foot seven. Wears number one eighth on his back. Crouches down. The strike zone is about the size of a cigarette pack. He walks on four pitches.
At Comiskey, he puts in the exploding scoreboard. He puts the White Sox in shorts.
Grown men. Major league ballplayers. Sliding into second base in shorts. I'd defend this city in any bar in America, and I've got nothing for the shorts.
[TALK: Eddie Gaedel, the exploding scoreboard, or the shorts. Which Bill Veeck stunt would you have paid to see?]
And one more Bill Veeck thing, because it pays off later. Harry Caray singing "Take Me Out to the Ball Game" in the seventh inning? That started on the South Side, before he was the Cubs guy. Harry used to sing it in the booth, to himself. The story goes that Veeck hid a microphone in there and flipped it on, so one night Harry's voice came booming out over the whole park. That's the kind of owner he was. Something fun happening in a corner? Turn it up.
And in 1979 the White Sox are not a great team, so the show is doing a lot of work. Mike Veeck said it flat out years later: "We were not doing well."
That's Bill's son. Mike Veeck. Who works for the team running promotions.

## THE IDEA
So you've got Steve Dahl at war with disco. And you've got Mike Veeck, looking for a way to fill seats on a Thursday night.
Here's how they find each other. A sales guy at The Loop, Jeff Schwartz, tells Mike that Dahl wants to blow up a pile of disco records. The original plan, the way it's been told, was to do it at a shopping mall. And Mike Veeck hears "a guy wants to blow something up in public" and thinks: not a mall. A ballpark. My ballpark.
Mike goes to Dahl and makes the pitch himself. Dahl's in.
July 12th, a twi-night doubleheader, White Sox and Tigers. Bring a disco record to the gate, and you get in for ninety eight cents. Because The Loop is 97.9. Get it.
Ninety eight cents. In today's money, that's about four bucks and thirty five cents. For a major league game.
Your records go in a crate. Between games, Dahl blows them up in center field.
How many people did they expect? Dahl said years later the Sox drew "maybe 9,000 people on a good night." Most accounts say the hope was something like twenty thousand. And most of the security was working the gates.
[TALK: You get in for one record you want blown up. Which record are you bringing to the gate?]

## THE CROWD
Every rock kid in Chicagoland, and northwest Indiana, and a good chunk of Wisconsin, heard about this and thought, "Ninety eight cents? To watch a guy blow up disco? I'm in."
The official attendance is 47,795. In a park that held about 44,500.
Mike Veeck thought the real number might have been sixty thousand.
And the records. The crate fills up fast. So a lot of people don't put their record in the crate. They keep it. In their hands. In the stands. For a whole baseball game.
You can guess.
Game one starts, and the Tribune said it was played "under a constant bombardment of records and firecrackers." They had to stop play more than once.
The Tigers' Rusty Staub said the records would come slicing down and stick in the ground. Stick. Like a throwing star. He said he begged his teammates to put on batting helmets. In the field. Defense, in a batting helmet, because the fans are throwing Bee Gees records at your head.
Ron LeFlore, the Tigers center fielder, said a golf ball came out of the stands and bounced between his legs while he was trying to catch a fly ball. So now you're tracking the fly ball, the golf ball, and forty copies of "Disco Inferno" coming in sideways like they were thrown by ninjas.
And on top of that: firecrackers. Beer. A smell in the air that is not entirely tobacco.
The Tigers manager, Sparky Anderson, told the Tribune, "Beer and baseball go together." And then: "I think those kids were doing things other than beer."
Oh, and the Tigers won game one, 4 to 1. Least important thing that happened in the building.

## THE BOOM
Between games. Steve Dahl comes out through a door in the center field wall, with his on-air partner Garry Meier, and with Lorelei from The Loop. Dahl is dressed in army fatigues and a helmet. Because it's a war. They circle the field in a Jeep, and the crowd throws firecrackers and beer at them, which in this context is love.
Dahl told Chicago magazine what that lap felt like: "They are throwing beers and cherry bombs at us. And they're the people who like us!"
He gets to center field, gets on the mic, and tells them, more or less word for word: this is now officially the world's largest anti-disco rally. We took all the disco records you brought tonight, we got them in a giant box, and we're going to blow them up real good.
And then he leads the chant. "Disco sucks." Fifty thousand people who've been drinking since four in the afternoon, chanting about the Village People.
And then he hits it.
[Pause.]
And it is a real explosion. Not a sound effect this time. The crate blows. It tears a hole in center field. Vinyl goes everywhere, and chunks of the field go with it. There's smoke. There's fire.
And then they come over the walls.

## THE FIELD
[CLIP: The kid who stole second base]
A long-haired kid jumps down out of left field, runs across the whole outfield, slides into second base, picks the bag up off the ground, and waves it over his head.
That's from Dave Gaborek, a teenage vendor working the stands that night. Slid into second like he'd earned it, and then stole it. Not the base. The actual base.
And that kid was the first of thousands. Some accounts say something like seven thousand people poured onto the field.
And here's my favorite one. There was a twenty one year old from the South Side in that crowd named Michael Clarke Duncan. Yes. The big guy from The Green Mile. By his own telling, he was in the first hundred or so people onto the field. He slid into third base. And he said he grabbed a bat out of the White Sox dugout on the way.
And then, years later, he admitted that he was actually a big disco fan.
He didn't even hate disco. He just heard there was a riot on the South Side and wasn't missing it. That's the most Chicago thing I've ever heard. "I don't agree with the cause, but I'm not missing this."
[TALK: The field opens up at a game tonight. Which base are you sliding into, and what are you taking home?]
Then the bigger stuff. The photographer Paul Natkin: "They pulled all the bases up. They tipped over the batting cage." They tore up the grass, tore out seats, and lit bonfires in the outfield.
[CLIP: The organist and the three nuns]
And up in the stands, the White Sox organist, Nancy Faust, is watching all of this happen. Here's how she told it to Chicago magazine:
"At first I saw little fires breaking out in the outfield. Three nuns were sitting near me. They turned around and asked, 'What is everybody chanting?' In those days, 'Disco sucks' wasn't a nice thing to say. My friend told them, 'They're just going, Let's go, Sox.'"
The field is on fire, and somebody looks three nuns in the eye and lies to them. That guy's got bigger problems than disco.
Meanwhile, the White Sox pitcher who was supposed to start game two, Ken Kravec, is out there warming up. Here's Kravec, to Chicago magazine:
"The bullpen was right next to the stands. And the wall was only three feet high. The fans were right there. So I began my warm-ups, and every now and then a shoe would fly by me. Then a couple more shoes. And then they started to Frisbee the albums."
So he moves out to the main mound, to get away from the shoes. "I threw 10 pitches, and the next thing you know, the fans stormed the field."
Everybody goes back to the dugouts. Nobody is going out there.
The scoreboard flashes "PLEASE RETURN TO YOUR SEATS." Nothing.
Bill Veeck, the man with the wooden leg, goes out there himself with a microphone and begs people to go back to the stands, while a bonfire is going in center field. Steve Dahl gets on the mic. And Harry Caray gets on the PA too. There's an account that they played "Take Me Out to the Ball Game" to settle everybody down.
It did not settle everybody down.
Mike Veeck, years later, to Chicago magazine: "Harry was a pro. He was the dance band on the Titanic, playing through the disaster."
Finally, the Chicago police come in. In riot gear. And one account says the people still in the stands, the ones who'd just come to see a baseball game, applauded when they showed up.
The field gets cleared. Thirty nine people get arrested, mostly for disorderly conduct. Nobody was seriously hurt, which, when you look at the pictures, is the real miracle of the night.
Although it was close. White Sox pitcher Steve Trout walked out to look at the damage afterward, and here's what he told Chicago magazine:
"People tore the bases apart. Home plate was gone. There was a big spot in center field where the albums had been blown up. There was vinyl everywhere. I walked out to look at center field, and I heard something go by me. It was an album from the upper deck and landed next to my right foot. It was stuck in the ground. I said, 'Holy shit, I could have been killed by the Village People.'"
Killed by the Village People. Put that on the headstone.
[TALK: Steve Trout almost got taken out by a record from the upper deck. What's the worst thing you've ever seen thrown at a game or a show?]

## THE FORFEIT
Now. There's supposed to be a second game.
Bill Veeck looks at a smoking crater in center field and wants to play. Sparky Anderson says no. It's not safe.
The umpires call it off for the night. And the next day the American League president, Lee MacPhail, gives the game to Detroit. A forfeit. 9 to 0.
The White Sox forfeited a home game. Because their own promotion worked too well.
[FILL: As of right now, that's still the last forfeited game in American League history. The White Sox have it because a guy blew up a crate of Donna Summer records.]
The White Sox manager, Don Kessinger: "We have found a lot of ways to lose games this year, but I guess we've added a new wrinkle. It's tough to lose two games when you played only one."
Bill Veeck did not agree. Here's Bill: "I think the grounds for forfeiting are specious at best. It's true there was some sod missing. Otherwise, nothing was wrong."
Some sod missing. The man is calling a smoking crater a divot.
And here's the thing. Baseball had already learned this lesson five years earlier, and nobody listened. June 1974, Cleveland. Ten Cent Beer Night. Beers for a dime. In today's money, that's about sixty five cents. By the ninth inning a fan runs out and grabs a Texas outfielder's cap, the Rangers come out of the dugout with bats, the Indians come out with bats to help them, and Cleveland forfeits. Five years later, Chicago looks at that and goes, "What if records?"
And the ashtray leg comes back. That night, the guys from The Loop are in Bill Veeck's office, because the radio station is going to pay for the damage. Here's Jeff Schwartz, the station's sales manager, to Chicago magazine:
"Les Elias and I were in Bill Veeck's office at two o'clock in the morning waiting to see what the damage results were from the insurance company, because we were going to pay it. Bill goes, 'Gentlemen, I hope you don't mind. It is real late and my leg is killing me.' And in front of us, he unscrews his leg, puts it on the side of his desk, and proceeds to use it as an ashtray. He says, 'I don't know who this guy is, but, boy, does he know how to draw people.' Bill was an entrepreneur. He got it."
[TALK: You're Sparky Anderson. Do you send your guys back out onto that field for game two?]

## WHERE ARE THEY NOW
So what happened to everybody?
Start with a kid who was working that night. Vince Lawrence. He was a teenager, working as an usher at Comiskey, and he took the job because he was saving up to buy a synthesizer. He got the synthesizer. And a few years later he co-wrote "On and On," which is one of the first house music records ever made. Here. In Chicago.
So the same night that was supposed to kill dance music, one of the guys who'd help invent the next kind of dance music was standing in the aisle in an usher's uniform.
[FILL: Disco didn't die in center field. It moved a few miles away, got a drum machine, and changed its name to house.]
Steve Dahl stayed on Chicago radio for decades. He's said disco was probably on its way out anyway, and that night just sped it up.
Bill Veeck sold the team not long after, and he's in the Hall of Fame now, for everything else he did. Mostly not this.
Mike Veeck was out at the White Sox before long. But he never stopped doing promotions. He ended up running the St. Paul Saints, a minor league team he co-owns with a group that includes Bill Murray. Yes, that Bill Murray.
And in 2001, on the anniversary of Disco Demolition, he finally apologized. To disco. He stood at home plate and delivered an apology to KC, of KC and the Sunshine Band, and ended it with: "I'm sorry from the bottom of my leisure suit."

## THAT GUY
[CLIP: That Guy verdict]
Okay. Time to name him. That Guy.
Suspect one: Steve Dahl. The man in the helmet. Who got fired on Christmas Eve, turned his whole radio show into a war, told his army to show up, and pressed the button.
Suspect two: Bill Veeck. Who spent thirty years teaching fans that anything can happen at the ballpark, and then got surprised when anything happened.
Suspect three: Mike Veeck. The son. Who heard "this guy wants to blow up records at a mall" and said, "What if it was in center field, between games, in front of every rock kid in three states, for less than a dollar?"
And the verdict.
Steve Dahl did exactly what he said on the air he was going to do. And his plan was a shopping mall. That's a story the local news does for thirty seconds and everybody forgets by Monday.
Mike Veeck is the one who heard that and said no. Not a mall. My ballpark. He went to Dahl himself. It was his promotion: ninety eight cents, between two games of a doubleheader, with a crate of explosives in center field and the security standing at the gates. He didn't just allow it. He asked for it.
So That Guy is Mike Veeck.
[THAT GUY STING]
Bill's on the hook a little. Mike learned it all from him. But Mike made the call.
And Mike agrees. Here he is in Chicago magazine: "My old man said it best: 'It was a promotion that worked too well.' Then my dad went out and took the public berating for me. I'm the one who triggered it."
