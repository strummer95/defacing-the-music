## COLD OPEN
July 12th, 1979. A Thursday night. Comiskey Park, on the South Side of Chicago.
The White Sox are about to play the second game of a doubleheader against the Detroit Tigers. Except in center field there's a big wooden crate, and it is packed with disco records. Thousands of them.
A twenty four year old rock DJ in an army helmet is standing next to it, leading tens of thousands of people in a chant. Two words. The second word is "sucks."
And then he blows it up.
[Pause.]
About a minute later, thousands of people are on the field. There are fires in the outfield. Harry Caray is on the PA asking everybody to please go back to their seats.
Nobody goes back to their seats.

Today: a Chicago story. A ninety eight cent ticket, a radio station at war with another radio station, a baseball owner with a wooden leg he used as an ashtray, his son, who had one very bad idea, and what the trivia books call the last forfeit in American League history.
And somewhere in this story, there's a guy. There always is. This time there might be two of them, and they might be related. Keep an eye out.

## 1979
Okay. Quick context, for anybody who wasn't alive, or was alive and has chosen to forget.
In 1979, disco is everywhere. Saturday Night Fever came out two years earlier and basically turned the whole country into a dance floor with a mirror ball over it. Disco on the radio. Disco on TV.
And if you were a nineteen year old kid in Chicagoland who'd spent your paper route money on Led Zeppelin records, you felt like the world had moved on without asking you.
That kid had a guy. And that guy was on the radio.

## THE DJ
His name is Steve Dahl. And in early 1978 he comes to Chicago from Detroit radio, where he'd been doing well enough that a Chicago station, WDAI, offers to double his salary to come over and be their rock morning guy.
So he comes. He starts in February 1978. Things are going great.
And then, on Christmas Eve 1978, WDAI switches its format. From rock. To disco.
[Pause.]
And they fire him. On Christmas Eve.
[FILL: I need everybody who's ever been laid off by email to sit with that for a second. This man got fired on Christmas Eve so a radio station could play "Le Freak."]
So Steve Dahl lands across town at WLUP, The Loop, 97.9 on your dial. And he decides that he's going to spend the rest of his life, or at least 1979, making the people who fired him sorry.
He starts calling his old station "Disco D.I.E." on the air. He mocks disco records every morning. He does parody songs of the big disco hits. And he starts an army. His listeners. He calls them the Insane Coho Lips, and the whole idea is they are at war with disco.
[RIFF: the name "Insane Coho Lips." Or use this:]
I've read about the Insane Coho Lips more than once now and I still can't tell you exactly what it means. That's how you know it was 1979 and a radio guy came up with it at five in the morning.

## THE VEECKS
The White Sox in 1979 are owned by Bill Veeck. And you have to understand Bill Veeck, because he's one of the most interesting people who ever owned a baseball team, and also because if you understand him, everything that happens next makes sense.
Bill Veeck's dad was president of the Cubs. So he grows up in baseball, here in Chicago, and as a kid he's working at Wrigley Field.
In World War Two he's in the Marines, and in the South Pacific a gun recoils and crushes his leg. Over the years he has surgery after surgery on it, and the number you see over and over is thirty six, and eventually the leg comes off. So he's got a wooden leg.
And Bill Veeck, a heavy smoker, cuts a hole in his wooden leg. And uses it as an ashtray.
[Pause.]
That's the guy. That's the owner. That's who you're dealing with.
And as an owner, Veeck is the king of promotions. Back in 1951, when he owns the St. Louis Browns, he sends a guy named Eddie Gaedel up to bat. Eddie Gaedel is three foot seven. Wears number one eighth on his back. Crouches down. The strike zone is about the size of a cigarette pack. He walks on four pitches.
At Comiskey, Veeck puts in the exploding scoreboard: a White Sox guy hits a home run, and the scoreboard shoots off fireworks and plays sirens. He put names on the back of jerseys, which every team does now. He put the White Sox in shorts.
[RIFF: the White Sox in shorts. Or use this:]
Grown men. Major league ballplayers. Sliding into second base in shorts. I'm a Chicago guy, I'd defend this city in any bar in America, and I've got nothing for the shorts.
So Bill Veeck believes, all the way down, that a ballgame is a show. And in 1979 the White Sox are not a great team, and people are not exactly lining up to see them, so the show is doing a lot of work.
And Bill has a son. Mike Veeck. Who works for the team doing promotions.

## THE IDEA
So you've got Steve Dahl at war with disco. You've got Mike Veeck looking for a way to fill seats on a Thursday night. And Dahl's partner on the air, Garry Meier, is part of this too.
The plan comes together like this. July 12th, a twi-night doubleheader, White Sox and Tigers. Bring a disco record to the gate, and you get in for ninety eight cents. Because The Loop is 97.9. Get it.
Your records go in a big crate. Between games, Steve Dahl walks out to center field. And blows them up.
[Pause.]
And everybody involved thinks this is going to be a nice bump. A few extra thousand people. A fun night.
[FILL: Accounts disagree on what they were expecting. Every version agrees on one thing: it wasn't this.]

## THE CROWD
Okay. So. July 12th.
And it turns out that every single rock kid in Chicagoland, and northwest Indiana, and a good chunk of Wisconsin, heard about this and thought, "Ninety eight cents? To watch a guy blow up disco? I'm in."
The official attendance is 47,795. In a park that held about 44,500.
[Pause.]
And that's the official number. The estimates of how many people were actually there, inside and outside, go a lot higher than that. One account says there were around twenty thousand more people outside who couldn't get in. Some of them got in anyway. Climbing. Rusty Staub, who played for the Tigers that night, told the New York Times later: "People brought ladders." Ladders. To a baseball game.
And the security plan, which was built for a regular Thursday, has most of the people working the gates. Which means the field is not very guarded.
And the records. Not everybody puts their record in the crate. A lot of people hang on to it.
Now, the story goes that during the first game, people started sailing those records out of the stands like frisbees, and players were ducking them. That gets told a lot. I couldn't nail it down to my satisfaction, so it goes in the "story goes" pile.
What's not in dispute: there are firecrackers. There's beer. There is a smell in the air that is not entirely tobacco.

## THE BOOM
Between games. Steve Dahl comes out. And he's dressed in army fatigues and a helmet. Because it's a war, remember. He and Garry Meier circle the field in a Jeep, and the crowd throws firecrackers and beer at them, which in this context is love.
He gets to center field. He leads the crowd. "Disco sucks." "Disco sucks." Tens of thousands of people.
[RIFF: what that chant sounds like in a ballpark. Or use this:]
I've been at Sox games where the whole park is chanting. It shakes your ribs. Now make it fifty thousand people who've been drinking since four in the afternoon, and the chant is about the Village People.
And then he hits it.
[Pause.]
And it is a real explosion. The crate blows. It tears a hole in the outfield. Vinyl goes everywhere. There's smoke. There's fire.
And then they come over the walls.

## THE FIELD
Thousands of people. Some accounts say something like seven thousand. Pour out of the stands and onto the field.
They're running the bases. They're tearing up the grass. They're tearing out seats. They're lighting bonfires in the outfield, with records, and whatever else they can find. The Tigers had won the first game, 4 to 1, by the way. Least important thing that happened in the building. The Tigers are in their dugout. The White Sox are in their dugout. Nobody is going out there.
And the people in charge are trying. Bill Veeck gets on the mic and pleads with people to go back to their seats. Steve Dahl gets on. And Harry Caray, the voice of the White Sox at the time, years before he's the Cubs guy everybody remembers, gets on the PA too. And there's an account that they even played "Take Me Out to the Ball Game" to try to settle everybody down.
[Pause.]
It did not settle everybody down.
[RIFF: Harry Caray asking politely. Or use this:]
If Harry Caray can't get you to behave, nobody can. That's a man who could get forty thousand people to sing on key in the seventh inning. And he couldn't get them off the grass.
Finally, the Chicago police come in. In riot gear. And one account says the people still in the stands, the ones who'd just come to see a baseball game, applauded when they showed up.
The field gets cleared. Thirty nine people get arrested, mostly for disorderly conduct. Some accounts say thirty seven.

## THE FORFEIT
Now. There's supposed to be a second game.
And the White Sox want to play it. They look at the field, and the field looks like a battlefield, there's a crater in center, and it's still smoking. But the Sox want to play.
The Tigers manager, Sparky Anderson, says no. He's not putting his guys out there. It's not safe.
So the second game gets postponed. And the next day the American League president, Lee MacPhail, gives the game to Detroit. A forfeit. 9 to 0.
[Pause.]
The White Sox forfeited a home game. Because their own promotion worked too well.
[FILL: It was the first forfeit in the majors in five years. They don't happen. And the White Sox have one because a guy blew up a crate of Donna Summer records. That's in the record book forever. Next to their name.]

## THE ARGUMENT
Now I have to slow down here, because this isn't only a funny story, and it would be bullshit of me to pretend it is.
There's a big argument about what this night was really about. And it's not settled.
One side of it: disco in 1979 is music that came up out of Black clubs, Latino clubs, and gay clubs. And a stadium full of mostly white kids chanting that it sucks and blowing it up can look like something uglier than a radio feud.
And there's a man named Vince Lawrence who was there. He was a teenager, fourteen or fifteen depending on the account, working that night as an usher at Comiskey. He was one of the few Black people in the building, by his own telling. He took the job because he was saving up to buy a synthesizer.
And he's said for years that a lot of the records people were bringing weren't disco at all. He's named Tyrone Davis records. Curtis Mayfield. Otis Clay. Soul and R&B. The thing they had in common, he says, was that they were by Black artists.
[Pause.]
The other side: Steve Dahl has always denied there was anything racist or homophobic about it. He's said it was about the music, and about a radio station that fired him. And plenty of the kids there would tell you they just liked rock and roll and thought ninety eight cents was a good deal.
I can't settle that for you. People who were there disagree. I'm telling you both sides because they're both on the record.
And here's the part I love, and it's true. Vince Lawrence, the kid who was saving for a synthesizer, got that synthesizer. And a few years later he co-wrote "On and On," which is one of the first house music records ever made. Here. In Chicago.
So the same night that was supposed to kill dance music, one of the guys who'd help invent the next kind of dance music was standing in the aisle in an usher's uniform.
[FILL: Disco didn't die in center field. It moved a few miles away, got a drum machine, and changed its name to house.]

## AFTER
So what happened to everybody?
Steve Dahl became a legend. His career took off after that night. He was on Chicago radio for decades. He's said he thought disco was probably on its way out anyway, and that the night just sped it up.
Bill Veeck took the public heat. He sold the team not long after, and he's in the Hall of Fame now, for everything else he did. Eddie Gaedel, the scoreboard, the shorts. Mostly not this.
[Pause.]
And Mike Veeck. The son. The promotions guy.
Mike's career went the opposite way from Dahl's. He left the White Sox in 1980. And by most accounts, nobody in the majors wanted the guy who forfeited a ballgame. One account says it took about twenty years before he landed another big league job, with the Tampa Bay Devil Rays.
But here's the thing. He kept going. He ended up in the minor leagues, running the St. Paul Saints, which he co-owns with, among other people, Bill Murray. Yes. That Bill Murray. From Wilmette. And Mike Veeck became one of the most famous promotions guys in all of baseball, with the kind of stunts minor league teams are known for now.
And he's said that Disco Demolition made him great at what he does.

## THAT GUY
Okay. Time to name him. That Guy.
The suspects.
Suspect one: Steve Dahl. The man in the helmet. Who, to be fair, was fired on Christmas Eve, and then turned his whole radio show into a war, and then pressed the button.
Suspect two: Bill Veeck. The owner. Who spent thirty years teaching baseball fans that anything can happen at the ballpark, and then got surprised when anything happened at the ballpark.
Suspect three: Mike Veeck. The son. Whose idea it was to put a bomb in center field between games, and sell tickets for less than a dollar to every rock kid in three states.
[Pause.]
And the verdict.
I love Steve Dahl in this. Steve Dahl is the That Guy who's awesome. He got fired on Christmas Eve and turned it into the most famous radio stunt in Chicago history. But Steve Dahl did exactly what Steve Dahl said he was going to do. He told everybody. He said it on the air, every morning, for months.
The guy whose job it was to think about what happens when fifty thousand people show up for a promotion built for twenty thousand, and only half of them can fit in the building, and they're all holding frisbees made of vinyl, is the promotions guy.
So That Guy is Mike Veeck.
And honestly, his dad is on the hook too. Mike learned it all from Bill. So it's a family business. It's a father and son, and a wooden leg full of cigarette ash.
And Mike Veeck is awesome, because he's the only one in this story who paid for it for twenty years, and then turned it into a career anyway.
