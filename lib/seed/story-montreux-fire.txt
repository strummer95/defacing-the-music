## MONTREUX AND FUNKY CLAUDE
Montreux is a little resort town on Lake Geneva, in Switzerland. Old hotels. Mountains. Rich people in nice coats walking slowly. The loudest sound on a Tuesday is a spoon hitting a teacup.
And in the middle of it is the Casino. Now, "casino" makes you think Vegas, slot machines, a guy named Sal. This was more of an entertainment complex. It opened in 1881 as the Kursaal: a casino, a restaurant, a winter garden, a theatre. And for ninety years, every time somebody had an idea, they bolted another room onto it. And in 1969, a concert hall called the Hourglass.
And that hall was home to the thing that put this quiet town on the map. The Montreux Jazz Festival.
Which was the work of one guy. Claude Nobs.
Claude did not start out in music. He trained as a cook. Then he took a job at the Montreux tourism office. Which is maybe the least rock and roll job in the history of Europe.
But Claude had taste and he had nerve. He started putting on concerts while still at the tourist office, and in 1967 he launched the Montreux Jazz Festival. He ran it for decades.

## FRANK
Now. Frank Zappa. The mustache.
Born in Baltimore in 1940. His dad was a chemist who worked in the defense industry. And the way Frank told it, he learned to make gunpowder when he was about six, and kept experimenting with explosives until he was around fifteen.
So the one guy in this story who actually knew how to make things explode is the guy on stage.
Frank ended up in music. Not normal music. Doo-wop, satire, noise, jokes, orchestra stuff. Zappa was a composer who happened to have a rock band, the Mothers of Invention.
And by 1971, that band had a lineup I find hilarious. Two of the lead singers were Mark Volman and Howard Kaylan. From The Turtles.
"Happy Together." "Imagine me and you, I do." That Turtles.
So the guys who sang the sweetest, sunniest pop song of 1967 are now in Frank Zappa's band, going by Flo and Eddie, doing filthy comedy rock. And they still sang "Happy Together" in the Zappa shows.
That's the band that rolls into Montreux in December of 1971, with a keyboard player named Don Preston on a brand new Minimoog synthesizer.

## THE ROOM
Before anything catches fire, let's look at the room. Because this is where people made decisions, and nobody thought they were making decisions.
The Hourglass hall had a wooden ceiling. People remember curtains, papier-mache Christmas decorations, maybe rattan. What everybody agrees on is that up there was the kind of thing that burns.
Up on the wall was the hall's big emblem, a giant hourglass. A hydraulic clock. Full of liquid. When the Swiss held a tribunal about the fire the next year, one claim was that it held forty five hundred liters of paraffin. The casino's lawyer said no, no, it was only three thousand liters of mineral oil.
[FILL: "Only three thousand liters of mineral oil" is the most lawyer sentence I have ever read. That's your defense? It was the other flammable liquid?]
Outside, the building was covered in scaffolding, because it was being renovated.
Inside, somebody had taken out the chairs. So the crowd sat on the floor.
How many people? The papers said about two thousand. Zappa said twenty five hundred to three thousand kids, "well over capacity."
And according to Zappa, writing in his book years later, there was one more decision. His words: "Since more kids were outside, trying to get in, the organizers had cleverly chained the doors shut."
Cleverly.
Every club owner in history has had that thought. "If I lock that door, nobody sneaks in." Which is true. It is also true that nobody gets out. It's the same door. That's how doors work.
To be fair to the casino, one eyewitness said staff got all the emergency exits open fast. So it's Zappa's word against that.

## THE GUY WITH THE GUN
The show is Saturday, December 4th, 1971. In the afternoon. A matinee. Packed house, good show. They get into "King Kong," a long instrumental the Mothers liked to stretch out, and Don Preston takes a synthesizer solo.
And around a quarter after four, somebody in the audience fires something at the ceiling.
[CLIP: The test shot nobody stopped]
The guy who burned down the Montreux Casino took a test shot first.
A woman who was there told a Lausanne paper two days later that a young man near her fired a small pistol at the start of the concert. And nobody did anything. And then he fired it again, later, and that's the one that lit the ceiling.
So he warmed up. A whole Zappa set in between where somebody could've said, "Hey man, what's in the jacket?"
The common version, the one in the song, is a flare gun. Ian Gillan, the singer of Deep Purple, was in the crowd, and he's said he saw two flares go up. Claude Nobs said Gillan told him the guy was standing right next to him and fired before anybody could stop him.
Zappa, in one telling, called it more like a bottle rocket. Fire engineers who studied it fifty years later landed on some kind of firework or flare. Not proven.
And the man himself? The Swiss police named a suspect. He took off right after, they put out a search, and they never found him. The rumor is he went to South America. Never caught, never charged, never told his side. His name is out there online. I'm not saying it, because nobody ever proved it.

## IT'S PART OF THE SHOW
And for a second, nobody panics. Because it's a Zappa show. Weird stuff happens at a Zappa show. Smoke and fire might just be the bit.
A fan named Alain Rieder said that at first the fire was tiny, and he figured they'd put it out and the concert would keep going.
And another fan, Peter Schneider, wrote this, and I love him for his honesty. He looked behind him, saw a big ball of flame, and, quote, "Because I was very stoned it looked beautiful." He thought the fire was part of the show.
This guy watched the ceiling catch fire and thought, "Wow. Production values."

## EVERYBODY OUT
[CLIP: Zappa's calm evacuation]
With the ceiling on fire, Frank Zappa got on the mic and said, "if you'd kindly."
And we don't have to guess, because the show was taped. Zappa later put the recording out himself, and you can hear him telling people to stay calm. The line that gets quoted is: "Fire! If you'd kindly move calmly toward the exit, ladies and gentlemen. Calmly."
Ladies and gentlemen. The building is on fire and he says "if you'd kindly."
The room didn't have great ways out. So people went for the windows. Behind the curtains on each side of the stage were floor to ceiling windows. Zappa said one of his roadies picked up an equipment case and smashed the big window. And Schneider says what got him out was a firefighter with an axe.
My money's on the guy with the equipment case. Roadies break things for a living.
And then people jumped. About ten feet into the grass. Rieder said he hung off a ledge and let himself drop. One witness said that before jumping, they went back for their belongings. Which were exactly where they'd left them. In the burning building.
And the no-chairs thing, the thing that sounds like a bad decision? Schneider thinks it helped. No rows to climb over, broad daylight, and, his words, nearly everybody was so stoned the fear didn't kick in, so they walked out more or less in order.
The tourist office said everybody was out in three and a half minutes. Claude said about five. And Zappa said his roadies were the last ones out, and an explosion from the heating system blew them out the door.
Nobody died. Zappa said three people went to the hospital, one of them a roadie caught in that blast. For a fire that took down an entire building with a crowd inside, that is about as lucky as it gets.

## CLAUDE RUNS IN
[CLIP: Funky Claude runs into the fire]
The founder of the Montreux Jazz Festival crawled through his burning casino on all fours, looking for stoned kids.
While the place is burning, Claude Nobs, the chef, the tourist office guy, is going back in. The festival's own history, and his obituaries, credit him with pulling out several young people who had hidden inside the casino, because they thought they'd be safe from the fire in there.
One of the guys who went in with him, Michel Ferla, explained the search plan: "We crawled through the burning building on all fours. At that time a lot of marijuana was smoked. We wanted to be sure that no one had fallen asleep."
That's the most Swiss rescue in history. Calm. Thorough. Checking under every couch for a sleeping hippie.
That's why he's in the song. "Funky Claude was running in and out." That line is about a real man running into a real burning building to drag out kids who'd made a terrible decision about where to hide.
I want that on his headstone. Not "Founder of the Jazz Festival." "Ran Into The Fire."
[TALK: The building's on fire and you think the safest place is inside the building. What's going through your head?]
About eighty firefighters came in from all over the area. The scaffolding outside burned like torches. And one of their senior guys said the water system ran dry after two hours. So they started pumping water straight out of the lake.
They put the fire out with Lake Geneva.
They saved a garage next door. They did not save the casino. And everything the Mothers brought on that tour was inside. All their gear. Gone.

## DEEP PURPLE
Deep Purple. British hard rock band, classic lineup: Ian Gillan singing, Ritchie Blackmore on guitar, Roger Glover on bass.
And they're in Montreux on purpose. They were supposed to be touring America at the end of 1971. Then Gillan got sick, the dates fell through, and now they've got a hole in the calendar and a record to make.
So they booked the casino to record their next album in the off season, right after the casino's last show of the year. That last show was Zappa.
And they didn't book a regular studio. They booked a truck. The Rolling Stones Mobile. A recording studio in a truck, owned by the Rolling Stones. You park it outside a building, run cables in, and the building is a studio.
And Roger Glover has told the story: they got to their hotel rooms and there was a fruit basket, a bottle of Swiss wine, and a pass to see Zappa at the casino.
So the welcome gift was a ticket to watch your recording studio burn down.
And the story goes, from French-language press and retold by Claude himself, that Gillan got out fine and then went back into the burning building. For his girlfriend's jacket. Claude called it typical of him.

## THE GRAND HOTEL
Then they moved again, to the Grand Hotel, which was empty for the winter. Another decision that sounds insane and worked.
They parked the Stones truck at the main entrance and set up the band at the end of a corridor off the lobby. To kill the echo in a big empty hotel, they hauled in mattresses. Ian Paice said about a hundred of them, lining the hallway.
So the band that just lost its studio to a fire is now recording in a hotel hallway made of beds.
And since the truck was outside, every time they wanted to hear a take, somebody had to walk out through the hotel, out the front, into the truck. It was such a pain that, by one account, they mostly stopped listening to playbacks. They just played it and trusted it.
Gillan wrote the lyrics, and they're basically a news report of the week: the fire, Funky Claude, the Grand Hotel, and the man of the hour, who the song calls "some stupid with a flare gun." That's his official review.
The album was "Machine Head." It came out in March 1972. And nobody in the band thought "Smoke on the Water" was the single. Gillan has said it was just another riff to them. One retelling says it only made the album as filler.
It became a single in the States in 1973 and went to number four on the Billboard chart. And it never left. Every garage, every guitar store, every talent show.
And the casino? Rebuilt. It reopened in 1975 with a recording studio inside called Mountain Studios. In 1979, Queen bought it. Today there's a statue of Freddie Mercury on the lakefront.

## THE SERIOUS PART
Now I need to slow down for a minute, because Zappa's week was not done.
The fire took everything the Mothers had brought. They kept going anyway. They rented equipment and got to London, and six days later, on December 10th, 1971, they're playing the Rainbow Theatre.
And during the show, a man in the audience named Trevor Howell gets on stage and shoves Zappa off it. Into the orchestra pit. Onto concrete. Accounts put the fall at somewhere between ten and fifteen feet.
Zappa was badly hurt. A broken leg, a head injury, injuries to his back and neck. He was in the hospital for weeks and spent a long stretch of the next year in a wheelchair, off the road. He wrote later that the band thought he was dead, and that the injury to his throat dropped his voice for good.
Howell got a year in jail. Why he did it depends on which report you read, and I'm not going to guess.
That doesn't get a joke. That lineup of the Mothers was basically done. A guy who had just calmly walked a couple thousand people out of a burning building got thrown off a stage six days later.
