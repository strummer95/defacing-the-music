## COLD OPEN
December 4th, 1971. Montreux, Switzerland. A Saturday.
Frank Zappa and the Mothers are on stage at the Montreux Casino, on the shore of Lake Geneva, with the Alps sitting behind it like a postcard. They're well into the show. They're in the middle of a song called "King Kong." The keyboard player is off on a long, weird synthesizer solo.
And somebody in the crowd raises a flare gun. Points it up. And pulls the trigger.
Indoors.
[Pause.]
By the end of the night the whole building is gone. And across the water, a British band with nowhere to record is watching the smoke roll out over the lake, and somebody is writing down a title.
## TODAY
Today: a Swiss casino, a flare gun, a band losing every piece of gear it owned, a festival boss running into a burning building, a truck borrowed from the Rolling Stones, the Swiss police banging on a door in the middle of the night, and the riff. You know the riff.
And somewhere in this story, there's a guy. There always is. This time he had a flare gun. Keep an eye out for him.

## MONTREUX
Okay. Montreux. Let me set the table.
Montreux is a little resort town on Lake Geneva. Old hotels. Mountains. Rich people in nice coats walking slowly. The loudest sound on a Tuesday is a spoon hitting a teacup.
And in the middle of it is the Casino. Now, "casino" makes you think Vegas, slot machines, a guy named Sal. This was more of an entertainment complex. Gambling, sure, but also a concert hall, a place where things happened. And for a few years it had been home to something that put this quiet little town on the map.
The Montreux Jazz Festival.

## FUNKY CLAUDE
And that festival was the work of one guy. Claude Nobs.
Claude Nobs was born in 1936. He did not start out in music. He trained as a cook. Then he took a job at the Montreux tourism office. Which is maybe the least rock and roll job in the history of Europe.
But Claude had taste and he had nerve. He started putting on concerts in the mid sixties, while still at the tourist office, and in 1967 he launched the Montreux Jazz Festival. It turned into one of the biggest music festivals in the world, and Claude ran it for decades.
[RIFF: Claude Nobs, the tourist office guy who built a world famous festival. Or use this:]
Every town has a Claude. The guy at the chamber of commerce who says, "What if we got a band?" Most of the time you get a cover band at the sidewalk sale. Once in a while you get Montreux.

## FRANK
Now. Frank Zappa. The mustache.
Zappa was born in Baltimore in 1940. His dad was a chemist who worked in the defense industry. And here's the detail I can't get over. The way Frank told it, he learned to make gunpowder when he was about six. He kept experimenting with explosives until he was around fifteen.
[Pause.]
So hold onto that. The kid who made gunpowder in his bedroom grows up, and somebody else brings the fire to his show.
The family moved west, to California, and Frank ended up in music. Not normal music. His first album with the Mothers of Invention, "Freak Out!", came out in 1966. Doo-wop, satire, noise, jokes, orchestra stuff. Zappa was a composer who happened to have a rock band.
And by 1971, his band had a lineup that I personally find hilarious. Because two of the lead singers were Mark Volman and Howard Kaylan. If those names don't ring a bell: they were from The Turtles.
"Happy Together." "Imagine me and you, I do." That Turtles.
[Pause.]
So the guys who sang the sweetest, sunniest pop song of 1967 are now in Frank Zappa's band, going by Flo and Eddie, doing filthy comedy rock. And they still sang "Happy Together" in the Zappa shows. It's on the Fillmore East live album from that year.
So that's the band that rolls into Montreux in December of 1971. Zappa, Flo and Eddie, and a keyboard player named Don Preston, with a Minimoog synthesizer, which in 1971 was still a pretty exotic thing to bring on tour.

## THE SHOW
The show was in the afternoon. A matinee. Which feels wrong for a fire story, but here we are.
By most accounts it's a good show. A packed house, a couple thousand people. And they get into "King Kong," a long instrumental the Mothers liked to stretch out, and Don Preston takes a synthesizer solo.
And right in the middle of it, somebody in the audience fires a flare gun up at the ceiling.
[Pause.]
And the ceiling, I'm sorry to tell you, was the kind that catches fire.
Now the honesty part. The common version, the one in the song, is a flare gun. Zappa himself, in at least one telling, called it something more like a rocket or a Roman candle. So I can't tell you the exact model of stupid. Just that it was fired indoors, and it went up.
[RIFF: the logic of bringing a flare gun to a concert. Or use this:]
I have been to a lot of shows. I have seen people sneak in a lot of things. Flasks. Weed. A whole sandwich in a coat. I have never once, in thirty two years, seen a guy pat his jacket and go, "Got my flare gun. Let's do this."
And for a second, nobody panics. Because remember, it's a Zappa show. Weird stuff happens at a Zappa show. Smoke and fire might just be the bit.
And this next part is legendary, but the details shift depending on who's telling it. The story goes that somebody on stage made a joke. "Fire! Arthur Brown in person!"
[FILL: For the young people: Arthur Brown's big hit in 1968 was a song called "Fire," and he performed wearing a helmet that was on fire. Great joke, if you were alive in 1968.]
Who said it? Some accounts say Zappa, some say Mark Volman, some say Howard Kaylan. And one guy who was there that day has said the idea of Zappa laughing it off is nonsense. So it goes in the "the story goes" pile.

## EVERYBODY OUT
Here is where Frank Zappa becomes the responsible adult.
By the accounts that survive, Zappa told the crowd to stay calm and get out. Slowly. No running.
And that matters, because the room didn't have great ways out. The main doors were small for a crowd that size. So people also went out through a big plate glass window near the stage. Which got broken, so people could climb through it.
[Pause.]
A couple thousand people. A fire on the ceiling. A small front door. And a window.
And here's the thing I have to say clearly. As far as every account I could find goes, nobody died. Injuries were described as minor. For a fire that took down an entire building with a crowd inside, that is about as lucky as it gets.
[RIFF: the calm voice in an emergency. Or use this:]
Every band has the one member who's good in a crisis. The guy who, when the van catches fire on the interstate, is already on the shoulder with the extinguisher while the drummer films it. That day, that guy was Frank Zappa.

## CLAUDE RUNS IN
And Claude Nobs. The chef. The tourist office guy.
While the place is burning, Claude is going back in. The festival's own history, and his obituaries, credit him with pulling out several young people who had hidden inside the casino, because they thought they'd be safe from the fire in there.
[Pause.]
That is why he's in the song. "Funky Claude was running in and out." That line is about a real man running into a real burning building to drag out kids who'd made a terrible decision about where to hide.
I want that on his headstone. Not "Founder of the Jazz Festival." "Ran Into The Fire."

## DEEP PURPLE
Deep Purple. British hard rock band, and in 1971 they're right in the classic lineup. Ian Gillan singing. Ritchie Blackmore on guitar. Jon Lord on the organ. Roger Glover on bass. Ian Paice on drums.
And they're in Montreux on purpose. They'd booked the casino to record their next album, in the off season, after the casino's last show of the year. That last show was Zappa.
And they didn't book a regular studio. They booked a truck.
The Rolling Stones Mobile. A recording studio in a truck, owned by the Rolling Stones. You park it outside a building, run cables in, and now the building is a studio. That truck is on a lot of great records from that era, including parts of Led Zeppelin's fourth album.
So the plan is: Zappa plays the last show, everybody goes home, the casino goes quiet for winter, and Deep Purple moves in and makes a record with the Stones' truck parked outside.
And Deep Purple had passes to the show. Roger Glover has told the story: they got to their hotel rooms and there was a fruit basket, a bottle of Swiss wine, and a pass to see Zappa at the casino. Ian Gillan has said he was in the audience when the flare went up.
[Pause.]
So the welcome gift was a ticket to watch your recording studio burn down.
[Pause.]
The smoke drifted out over Lake Geneva. Smoke. On the water.
And yeah. That's the title. It was sitting right there.
Roger Glover gets credit for the title, and there are two versions of how. In one, he woke up from a dream a few days later with the phrase in his head. In another, he wrote it down right away. Either way, it's Roger's.

## THE PAVILION
So now Deep Purple has a truck, a booking, and no building.
And Claude Nobs, who has just had the worst day of his professional life, starts finding them somewhere else to record. Because that's the kind of guy he is.
First stop: a local hall called the Pavilion. They set up, and they start jamming. And out of that jam comes a riff.
[Pause.]
Ritchie Blackmore. Four notes. Well. You know.
[RIFF: the riff. If you've ever picked up a guitar, you've played it. Or use this:]
If you have ever picked up a guitar, you know this riff. You learned it before you knew what a chord was. You learned it on one string. Every guitar teacher in America has heard it ten thousand times and would like it to stop.
Blackmore has explained it a few ways. He's said it's built in fourths, that old, kind of medieval sounding interval. He's also said in an interview that it's Beethoven's Fifth, turned around. Plenty of musicians say it doesn't sound like that at all. So Beethoven goes in the "the story goes" pile.
And then the neighbors complain.
[Pause.]
It's the middle of the night in a quiet Swiss town, and a hard rock band is blasting away in a public hall. The band has told this one many times: the police showed up and started pounding on the door, and the road crew held the door shut so they could finish the take.
So the backing track for the most famous riff in rock history got finished while roadies physically held the door against the Swiss police.
Then they moved again, to the Grand Hotel, which was empty for the winter. They recorded in the hallways and rooms, with the Stones truck out back and cables running in through the windows. Gillan wrote the lyrics, and they're basically a news report of the week: the fire, Funky Claude, the Grand Hotel, and the man of the hour, who the song calls "some stupid with a flare gun." That's his official review.

## THE HIT
The album was "Machine Head." It came out in March 1972. And here's the funny part. Nobody in the band thought "Smoke on the Water" was the single. Gillan has said it was just another riff to them.
It became a single in the States in 1973, and it went to number four on the Billboard chart.
And it never left. Every garage, every guitar store, every talent show. All because some guy brought a flare gun to a Zappa matinee.
And the casino? Rebuilt. It reopened in 1975, with a recording studio inside called Mountain Studios. In 1979, Queen bought it. Freddie Mercury ended up making records in the building that replaced the one that burned, and today there's a statue of Freddie on the lakefront.

## THE SERIOUS PART
Now I need to slow down for a minute, because Zappa's week was not done.
The fire took everything the Mothers had brought. All their gear. Gone. And they kept going anyway. They rented equipment and got to London, and six days later, on December 10th, 1971, they're playing the Rainbow Theatre.
And during the show, a man in the audience named Trevor Howell gets on stage and shoves Zappa off it. Into the orchestra pit. Onto concrete. Accounts put the fall at somewhere between ten and fifteen feet.
[Pause.]
Zappa was badly hurt. A broken leg, a head injury, injuries to his back and neck. He was in the hospital for weeks, and he spent a long stretch of the next year in a wheelchair, off the road. Zappa wrote later that the band thought he was dead. He also said the injury to his throat dropped his voice for good.
Howell got a year in jail. And his reason depends on which report you read. One version, the one Zappa repeated, is that it was about his girlfriend and Zappa. Another contemporary report says he just didn't think the concert was good value for the money. So the motive goes in the "the story goes" pile. Either way, there are a lot of ways to complain about a ticket price, and this is the worst one.
That doesn't get a joke. That lineup of the Mothers was basically done. A guy who had just calmly walked a couple thousand people out of a burning building got thrown off a stage six days later.

## THAT GUY
Okay. Time to name him. That Guy.
The suspects.
Suspect one: the guy with the flare gun. He brought a flare gun to a concert hall and fired it at a ceiling. There's a name that floats around online as the police suspect, but I couldn't find solid enough proof to say any name on this show. So he stays "the guy."
Suspect two: the building. A packed hall where one flare could take the whole place down, with exits tight enough that people went out a window. I don't know the Swiss fire codes of 1971, and I won't pretend to. But the room didn't help.
Suspect three: Frank Zappa. Kidding. Partly. The man made gunpowder at six and his show was the one that caught fire. That's either cosmic justice or a coincidence and I'm calling it a coincidence.
[Pause.]
And the verdict.
It's the flare gun guy. No twist. There's no office full of guys here. There's no stagehand who said yes. There's one guy in a crowd of a couple thousand people who thought, "You know what this King Kong solo needs?"
But here's the "that guy is usually awesome" part. And I hate it. Without that guy, there is no "Smoke on the Water." No fire, no song. Every kid who ever learned that riff on one string owes a little piece of it to the worst decision anybody made in Switzerland that year.

## OUT
So that's it. One Swiss casino. One flare gun. A Zappa band that lost all its gear and then had a week that got worse. A chef from the tourist office running into a fire. Roadies holding a door against the police. And four notes every guitar player on Earth can play.
And the lesson, if there is one: if the casino is on fire, do not hide in the casino. Claude will come get you, but don't make Claude do that.
