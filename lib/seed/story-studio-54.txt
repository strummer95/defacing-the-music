## THE BACK DOOR
[CLIP: "Le Freak" was originally called "Fuck Off"]
One of the biggest disco songs ever made was originally called "Fuck Off." And it was aimed at one guy. A doorman.
Here's how that happens. The band is Chic. Two guys run it: Nile Rodgers on guitar and Bernard Edwards on bass. If you've heard "Good Times," you know them. That bass line got lifted for "Rapper's Delight," and a version of it shows up in Queen's "Another One Bites the Dust." Nile plays that tight, choppy rhythm guitar that every funk player on earth has tried to steal. I've tried. It's harder than it sounds.
By the end of 1977, Chic has a hit, "Dance, Dance, Dance," and they're getting noticed. And Grace Jones, who's performing at Studio 54 that New Year's Eve, wants to talk to them about producing her next album. So she invites them to the club.
Here's Nile, telling Billboard about it in 2016:
"She told us to come to the back door of Studio 54 and tell them that we were personal friends. We'd seen all the stuff about them separating the M&Ms and all that stuff so we wanted to get it exactly right. Grace has a very interesting, affected voice so we tried to put on her voice and say exactly what she said, thinking the bouncer would know. We ended up sounding like a cross between Bella Lugosi and Marlene Dietrich, but the guy slammed the door in our faces and told us, 'Ah, fuck off.'"
They knock again. And Nile says the guy told them, "I told you, Ah fuck off."
Grace Jones forgot to leave their names at the door.
And Nile told Sound On Sound magazine what they were wearing for this: "our suits must have cost us a couple of thousand bucks each, and our really fancy shoes had got soaked trudging through the snow."
A couple thousand bucks each. In today's money, that's about ten grand. Per suit. In a slush puddle. On New Year's Eve.
[TALK: You're dressed up, you're on the list, and the bouncer won't let you in. Do you knock twice?]
Nile's apartment was a block away. So they grab champagne from the corner liquor store, Dom Perignon, which Nile says they called "rock'n'roll mouthwash," go back to his place, plug in, and start jamming. Nile wanted a riff song, something like Cream's "Sunshine of Your Love." And what comes out of their mouths over that riff is what the doorman said.
Here's Nile, to Sound On Sound:
"So 'Fuck Off' was a protest song, and we actually thought it was pretty good. 'Aaaaahh, fuck off!' It had a vibe. I was thinking 'This could be the anthem of everybody who gets cut off on the street by a cab driver or any kids who want to say this to their parents.'"
And they really wanted to finish it that way. Then the radio problem kicks in. So they try "freak off." Nile called that "pretty hideous." And then: "For one second the light bulb went on and I sang 'Aaaaahh, freak out!'"
That's "Le Freak." It hits number one on the Billboard Hot 100 in December 1978. It gets knocked off the top twice, and comes back twice. Nile says it was the biggest selling single in Atlantic Records history for thirty three years.
And there's a bonus. Because they sing the "Ahh" with that fake French thing, Nile says French speakers still think they're singing "Africa."
So a bouncer told two guys to go away, and they turned it into one of the biggest songs of the decade. The doorman should get a royalty check. Instead he got anonymity.

## THE CLUB
So who owned this door?
Two guys from Brooklyn. Steve Rubell and Ian Schrager. They met in college at Syracuse. Steve was the guy out front. Ian was the guy in the office. Steve had been running a couple of steakhouses called the Steak Loft. Then they opened a disco in Queens, and they wanted Manhattan.
They found an old building at 254 West 54th Street. It opened in 1927 as an opera house and went broke. Then CBS turned it into a TV studio for decades. "What's My Line?" "Password." "Captain Kangaroo." Captain Kangaroo's old stage is about to become the most famous dance floor on earth.
They put about four hundred grand into it. In today's money, that's about two million. Big moving sets. Lighting designers from Broadway. And hanging over the dance floor, a giant man in the moon, holding a spoon up to his nose. Ian Schrager told WWD in 2020 that a lot of people thought it was promoting cocaine, and, his words, "Nothing could be further from the truth."
It's a man in the moon with a spoon at his nose. In a disco. In 1977.
And the drugs were real. Quaaludes, cocaine, all over the place. That's not a joke, it's just what the place was.
Opening night is April 26th, 1977. The room holds about twenty five hundred. Around four thousand people get in, and invited celebrities get stuck outside. Schrager says Cher ended up on the front page of the New York Post in overalls and a straw hat, and Steve called him at five in the morning: "We made it."
There's one thing they forgot. A liquor license.
[CLIP: The hottest club in the world served fruit punch]
So the hottest club in the world opens without a liquor license. They get around it with a catering permit, the kind you'd get for a wedding, one night at a time. Then the state stops giving them permits, and in May 1977 the state liquor boss shows up himself.
The Madison State Journal, two days later: "Roth said that he entered the club, ordered two drinks, and after the bartenders accepted his money, police were called to close the club."
Seven hundred people inside. Jack Nicholson. Warren Beatty. Mick Jagger. Steve gets arrested and calls it a misunderstanding. Steve said a well-known lawyer told him it was fine. And the Reno Evening Gazette adds, "The lawyer was in Europe."
They reopen the next night serving fruit juice and soda. For months. The Gazette asked the real question: "Will it pull in the VIPs and Beautiful People with fruit juices?"
It did. That's how good the door was.

## THE ROPE
[CLIP: Studio 54 turned away the president of Cyprus]
Studio 54 turned away the president of Cyprus. The way the story gets told, the doorman thought he was the president of Cypress Hills Cemetery.
And it's not the only one. When a son of the Saudi king got rejected, the Saudi embassy wrote Steve Rubell a letter asking him not to do it again. Warren Beatty got snubbed. The Fonz, Henry Winkler, got snubbed.
Getting in usually cost seven or eight bucks. In today's money, that's about forty. Money wasn't the problem. Steve was the problem. He stood out front himself and picked. Felipe Rose from the Village People told PBS what that sounded like: "He would say, 'yes, no, you can stay, you can go, maybe so.' His own nursery rhyme because he was having a ball doing that."
And Steve bragged about it. He told New York magazine in November 1977, "I turned away 1,400 people last Saturday."
The door guy most nights was Marc Benecke, who says he got the job at eighteen because Steve asked him what he was doing for the summer. Here's Benecke on how they picked:
"We called it casting a play. Or tossing a salad. We don't want all tomatoes."
People lost their minds over it. According to histories of the club, some rejected guests pulled guns, and security cleared out trash cans for blocks around the club, because they were worried about violence. And Schrager told WWD, "I've seen wives leave their husbands to get in and couples split up. I'm serious."
[TALK: Would you leave your wife on a sidewalk to get into Studio 54? Be honest.]

## THE NIGHTS
About a week after opening, Bianca Jagger has a birthday party there. The club was normally closed Mondays, so they opened it up for her. Here's Schrager, to WWD, describing it: "We had two nude people with clothes [body]-painted on them. The woman came out with long blonde hair, pulling a birthday cake. She got off the horse and Bianca jumped on, in a red dress on a white horse."
A white horse. Indoors. The photo went everywhere, and it's still the picture most people think of when they hear "Studio 54."
And Steve loved a stunt. For Andy Warhol's fiftieth birthday in 1978, Myra Scheer, who worked for both owners, says Steve dumped eight hundred one dollar bills over Warhol's head. Eight hundred bucks. In today's money, that's about four grand. And later that money turned up in the club's books, written down as a Warhol expense. Warhol to New York magazine: "Why would Steve do that? No wonder people are afraid to go there now."
For a Valentine's Day party in 1979, they brought in three thousand Dutch tulips and about four thousand square yards of sod from Bermuda. Grass. Shipped in. For a disco.

## THE MONEY
[CLIP: Steve Rubell told a reporter he was hiding money from the IRS]
In the fall of 1977, Steve Rubell sat down with Dan Dorfman, the money columnist at New York magazine, and told him this, as Grazia magazine quoted it years later: "The profits [are] astronomical. Only the mafia does it better."
That's bad. Here's worse. Dorfman asked about revenues, and Steve said: "It's a cash business and you have to worry about the IRS. I don't want them to know everything."
He said that to a reporter. Who wrote it down.
The club made about seven million dollars its first year. In today's money, that's about thirty seven million. And a lot of it was cash. Here's how it worked, according to Scott Nilsson, who worked the front of the club: "They would close the register and pull out the money. The first set of tapes was reported and the second set was not."
Steve's name for the system was "cash-in, cash-out and skim." And according to the documentary "Studio 54," the books had a column with the word "skim" written on it. They labeled the crime.
Ian Schrager, looking back, to WWD in 2020: "We weren't some organized gang. We were the gang that couldn't shoot straight. All this money was coming in and it was the worst kept secret in town."

## THE RAID
December 14th, 1978. Morning. The club's closed. A reported thirty one federal agents show up with a search warrant, and somebody, probably a cleaning man, lets them in.
The tip came from a guy named Donald Moon, a former employee. The prosecutor, Peter Sudler, told Grazia why he did it: "He was angry about being passed over for a promotion." Moon told the IRS there was a second set of books, and money, in the ceiling tiles.
And then Ian Schrager shows up for work.
[CLIP: Ian Schrager walks into the raid]
Here's the Village Voice, a month later, reading from the government's complaint:
"Schrager walked into Studio 54 on December 14 at 9:30 a.m. just after the federal agents had arrived. Schrager was carrying books, records, and other documents. After the agents identified themselves, Schrager placed these materials on the floor. On top of the pile an agent found a white envelope with five packets of what turned out to be unusually high-quality cocaine."
[Pause.]
He saw the feds, walked in anyway, and set the cocaine down on top of the pile. Sudler, decades later: "I thought it was incredibly stupid."
The Voice also says agents found three hundred Quaaludes in the safe. Sudler says they found manila envelopes, one for every day, and that the skim was "almost 80 percent. Typically, you don't skim that much."
Their lawyer was Roy Cohn, one of the most feared lawyers in New York, and his line was that all the agents found was, quote, "an appointment book and a list of parties coming up at Studio 54."
And that night, Studio 54 opened. Like normal.
[TALK: The feds just carried your books out the front door. Do you open the club that night?]

## THE FALL
In June 1979 a grand jury indicts them. The government says they skimmed two and a half million dollars, as much as sixty percent of what came in. In today's money, that's about twelve million.
That same month, Casablanca Records puts out a Studio 54 double album, "A Night at Studio 54," with Steve and Ian as executive producers. Side one, track one: "Le Freak." The club's own album opens with the song about getting kept out of the club.
And while all this is going on, they put about a million two into a new look for the club. In today's money, that's about five million. And according to one history of the building, they did it expecting rock music to make a comeback.
In November 1979 they plead guilty. In January 1980 they each get three and a half years and a twenty thousand dollar fine. In today's money, that fine is about seventy eight thousand.
To get their time cut, they talk to prosecutors about other club owners. Sudler says he set up the meeting with a little bait: "I ordered a slew of Chinese food." And then: "Rubell looked around and asked for some of the food. I said that they had to be part of the team."
Steve Rubell got flipped by Chinese food.
Schrager's version, in the documentary: "It isn't as if we named names."
[TALK: What's your price to rat out the competition? Is Chinese food enough?]

## THE LAST PARTY
Saturday night, February 2nd, 1980. Two days before they report to prison, Studio 54 throws them a going away party.
Here's how the wire story started, in the Garden City Telegram out in Kansas, that Thursday: "The owners of Studio 54 are headed for prison, but that didn't stop the popular disco from giving them a flashy going away party attended by about 3,000 people."
Diana Ross and Liza Minnelli sang to them. "My Way" played. At least one guy showed up in a "Free Steve Rubell" T-shirt. And word has it Diana Ross lost a shoe.
Myra Scheer remembers how the staff felt: "It was like Mom and Dad [were leaving]." And Steve's bodyguard, Freddie, told her, "If there was any way I could go to jail with him, I would."
And then the ending writes itself. By the end of that month, the state takes away the liquor license, because you can't hold one if you're a convicted felon. And the club goes right back to serving fruit punch.
Studio 54 opened on fruit juice and closed on fruit punch.
[TALK: Somebody throws you a going away party before prison. What song are you walking out to?]

## LEGEND OR FACT
The horse. Half legend. She got on it. She didn't ride in on it. Bianca Jagger wrote to the Financial Times in 2015: "Mick Jagger and I walked into Studio 54." The horse was already there, and she "made the foolish decision to get on it for a few minutes." Nile Rodgers was there, and he told the Evening Standard, "That was made to seem like she came galloping in like Sir Galahad, but she wasn't on it a long time."
The cash in the ceiling. Depends who you ask. The prosecutor says the tip was money and books in the ceiling tiles. Schrager, to WWD: "No, not true, folklore. But there were equally silly places. I had money stashed in the trunk of my car."
So his defense is that the money wasn't in the ceiling. It was in his trunk.
And the big one. Did Steve's Mafia brag bring in the IRS? Probably not by itself. The tip came from a guy mad about a promotion, more than a year after the brag. But it sure didn't help.

## WHERE ARE THEY NOW
Steve and Ian served about thirteen months. When they got out, they went into hotels, Morgans, the Royalton, the Paramount, and helped invent the boutique hotel. Schrager got a presidential pardon in 2017.
Steve Rubell died in 1989, of AIDS-related illness. He was forty five.
Nile Rodgers became one of the biggest producers alive. He produced Bowie's "Let's Dance" and Madonna's "Like a Virgin." He played on Daft Punk's "Get Lucky." Bernard Edwards died in 1996, in Tokyo, hours after playing a tribute show for Nile.
And Grace Jones? Nile co-produced her album "Inside Story" in 1986. They made the record. It just took nine years and a door.
The building's a Broadway theater now. These days anybody can get in. You just need a ticket.

## THAT GUY
[CLIP: That Guy verdict]
Okay. Time to name him. That Guy.
The suspects.
Grace Jones. She forgot to put two guys on the list.

And Steve Rubell.
Here's the verdict. Grace Jones forgot a list. Everybody forgets a list. The doorman was doing exactly what he was hired to do, and he accidentally gave the world "Le Freak." That man deserves a plaque.
But the door was Steve's. He built the whole business on the word "no." He stood out front and turned away fourteen hundred people on one Saturday and bragged about it. Chic didn't get kept out by accident. They got kept out by a system Steve designed.
And then the one time Steve should have said no, he said yes, to a reporter. "I don't want them to know everything." He wouldn't let you in his club. But he'd tell a magazine where the money went.
That Guy is Steve Rubell.
