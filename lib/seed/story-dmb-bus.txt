## THE BUS
Here's something about tour buses, because a lot of you have never had the honor.
A tour bus is a house on wheels. Bunks, a lounge, a little kitchen. And a bathroom.
And that bathroom goes somewhere. It goes into a tank under the bus. In the RV world they call it the blackwater tank. Black. As opposed to gray, which is your sink and your shower. Black is the other stuff.
And that tank needs to be emptied. And there are places you do that. Dump stations. Campgrounds. Specific pipes. It is a whole procedure. Every driver knows it.
[FILL: Ask anybody who's ridden on a band bus. The first rule they tell you, before where to sleep, before where the beer is, is about the toilet. Bus guys are very serious about the toilet.]
And according to the lawsuit the state of Illinois filed later, on this bus, emptying the tank was a matter of flicking a switch. A toggle. Behind the driver's seat.
Not a lever you trip with your knee. Not a button next to the radio. A switch you have to reach back for.

## THE WEEKEND
So, August 2004. The band is playing a two-night stand at Alpine Valley. That's up in East Troy, Wisconsin, about ninety minutes from the city, a big outdoor amphitheater out by a ski hill. If you grew up around here, you've been to Alpine Valley, and you've sat in traffic leaving Alpine Valley for longer than the show.
And between shows, the band's not sleeping in East Troy. They've got rooms at the Peninsula. On Superior Street, just off Michigan Avenue. A very nice hotel. The kind of place where the doorman's coat costs more than my car.
So on Sunday, August 8th, with the second Alpine Valley show still to play that night, Stefan Wohl is driving Boyd Tinsley's bus into downtown Chicago. Alone. Nobody from the band is on it. He's headed for the Peninsula. One report of the state's complaint says he was coming from a lot on the west side of the river to pick somebody up at the hotel.
And his route takes him over the Kinzie Street bridge.

## THE RIVER
Now, for out-of-towners, let me tell you about the Chicago River, because this matters.
We are weird about our river. In 1900 this city reversed the direction it flows. Made it run backwards, away from the lake, because we were dumping so much sewage into it that it was getting into our drinking water.
So the river has a history with this exact problem. I want that on the record.
Then for a hundred years, the city worked on cleaning it up. And by 2004, the river's a showpiece. Every St. Patrick's Day we dye it green and thousands of people in plastic hats stand on the bridges and cheer for a river changing color.
And in summer, the thing to do is the architecture boat tour. You sit on an open deck, a guide points out the buildings, and you go "ooh" at the Wrigley Building. It's honestly great. Do it. Maybe check the bridges first.
[RIFF: the architecture boat tour. Or use this:]
You sit there with a cold drink, a guide talks about Mies van der Rohe, and for about ninety minutes you feel very cultured. Nobody tells you to look up. Nobody's ever needed to.
That Sunday, the boat is called Chicago's Little Lady. It's running the one o'clock Chicago Architecture Foundation tour. Open top. More than a hundred people on it.
One of them is a guy named Tracy Dangott. He's there with his wife at the time and two friends. And they've picked their seats on the open deck. Which, on a beautiful day, is where you sit. You'd be an idiot to sit inside.
And the boat is heading under the Kinzie Street bridge.

## THE BRIDGE
The Kinzie Street bridge crosses the North Branch of the river, not far from where it meets the main branch. It's a movable bridge, and the deck is open steel grating. Metal mesh. When you drive over it your tires hum. And if you're standing under it, you can see daylight through it.
Which means, if something liquid comes down on top of it, it doesn't stay on top of it.
So. The boat goes under. The bus goes over. Later, police would put out a still from a security camera with a time stamp on it: a black and silver bus on the Kinzie Street bridge at 1:18 in the afternoon.
And somewhere up there, Stefan Wohl flicks the switch.

## THE CHARGES
And now it gets official.
The state attorney general, Lisa Madigan, sues. The band and the driver. Water pollution and public nuisance. The city gets in on it too.
And in January 2005, the Cook County State's Attorney charges Stefan Wohl with misdemeanors. Reckless conduct, and discharging contaminants to cause water pollution. He turns himself in.
And here's the line that matters. Before they charged him, prosecutors didn't just watch the tape. They talked to engineers. About the bus. About the tank. About the switch. And the prosecutor, a guy named Robert Egan, said: "We were satisfied with what they told us that it could not have been an accident."
In March he pleads guilty. He gets eighteen months of probation, a hundred and fifty hours of community service, and a ten thousand dollar fine. The maximum. In today's money, that's about seventeen thousand. And the fine goes to Friends of the Chicago River, a group whose entire job is protecting the river.
So the guy who dumped the tank paid the river people. That's the closest thing to justice this story has.
His lawyer never called reporters back. And as far as I could find, Stefan Wohl never told anybody, in public, why.
And the band fires him.
