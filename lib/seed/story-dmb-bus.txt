## COLD OPEN
August 8th, 2004. A Sunday afternoon in Chicago. Sunny. Summer. The good kind of day, the kind we get maybe eleven of a year.
An open-top tour boat is gliding down the Chicago River. More than a hundred people on the deck, cameras out, sunglasses on, listening to a guide talk about the buildings.
The boat slides under the Kinzie Street bridge. An old bridge, with a deck made of open steel grating. You can look up through it and see the sky.
And at that exact moment, a big shiny tour bus is crossing that bridge.
[Pause.]
And the people on that boat are about to find out what's in it.
## TODAY
Today: a jam band from Virginia, a two-night stand in Wisconsin, a very nice hotel on the Magnificent Mile, a switch behind the driver's seat, and one of the worst boat rides in the history of the Great Lakes region.

The Dave Matthews Band is from Charlottesville, Virginia. Dave Matthews himself grew up partly in South Africa, and he settled in Charlottesville in the mid eighties. ## THE BUS
I need to explain something about tour buses, because a lot of you have never had the honor.
A tour bus is a house on wheels. Bunks, a lounge, a little kitchen. And a bathroom.
And that bathroom goes somewhere. It goes into a tank under the bus. In the RV world they call it the blackwater tank. Black. As opposed to gray, which is your sink and your shower. Black is the other stuff.
[Pause.]
And that tank needs to be emptied. And there are places you do that. Dump stations. Campgrounds. Specific facilities with specific pipes. It is a whole procedure. Every driver knows it.
[FILL: Ask anybody who's ridden on a band bus. The first rule they tell you, before where to sleep, before where the beer is, is about the toilet. Bus guys are very serious about the toilet.]
And according to the lawsuit the state of Illinois filed later, on this bus, emptying the tank was a matter of flicking a switch. Behind the driver's seat.
Keep that switch in your head.

And his route takes him over the Kinzie Street bridge.

## THE RIVER
Now let me tell you about the Chicago River, for out-of-towners, because this matters.
The Chicago River is ours. We are weird about it. In 1900 this city reversed the direction the river flows. Turned it around. Made it run backwards, away from the lake, because we were dumping so much sewage into it that it was getting into our drinking water.
[Pause.]
So the river has a history with this exact problem. I want that on the record.
And then for a hundred years, the city worked on cleaning it up. And by 2004, the river's a showpiece. Every St. Patrick's Day we dye it green and thousands of people in plastic hats stand on the bridges and cheer for a river changing color.
And in summer, one of the big things to do, for tourists and locals, is the architecture boat tour. You sit on an open deck, a guide points out the buildings, and you go "ooh" at the Wrigley Building. It's honestly great. If you've never done it, do it. Maybe check the bridges first.
[RIFF: the architecture boat tour. Or use this:]
You sit there with a cold drink, a guide talks about Mies van der Rohe, and for about ninety minutes you feel very cultured. Nobody tells you to look up. Nobody's ever needed to.
That Sunday afternoon, the boat is called Chicago's Little Lady. Open top. More than a hundred people on it. Sources say anywhere from a hundred to a hundred and twenty.
And it's heading under the Kinzie Street bridge.

## THE BRIDGE
The Kinzie Street bridge crosses the North Branch of the river, not far from where it meets the main branch. It's a movable bridge, and the deck is open steel grating. Metal mesh. When you drive over it your tires hum. And if you're standing under it, you can see daylight through it.
[Pause.]
Which means, if something liquid comes down on top of it, it doesn't stay on top of it.
So. The boat goes under. The bus goes over.
And somewhere up there, Stefan Wohl flicks the switch.

## THE DROP
The figure everybody uses is about eight hundred pounds of human waste. One other report put it at eighty to a hundred gallons. I'm not going to argue about the units. It is a lot. It is the entire tank.
And it comes through the grate in a sheet.
[Pause.]
There was a Chicago Tribune reporter on that boat. And the passengers described a downpour of foul-smelling, brownish-yellow slurry. That's from the reporting. I didn't come up with "slurry." I wouldn't have. It ruined people's clothes. Several people got sick.
[RIFF: the sound of it. Or use this:]
I keep thinking about the half second before anybody understands what it is. It's sunny. There's no cloud. And something wet is falling on you. Your brain says rain. Your brain tries really hard to say rain.
And then, according to Chicago magazine, which did an oral history of this, years later, the guide on the boat tries to keep it together. The guide tells everybody that it appears some water has hit the deck.
[Pause.]
And a man from the back of the boat says: "That's not water, buddy."
[FILL: That's the most Chicago sentence ever spoken. No panic. No screaming. Just a guy correcting the record. I would like that man to be our mayor.]
And then, according to people on board, the screaming started.
The boat gets back to the dock. The crew cleans it. And everybody gets a refund on their ticket, which was twenty five bucks.
Twenty five dollars. For that. I'd want a little more than my twenty five back. I'd want the boat.

## THE CHARGES
And now it gets official.
The state attorney general, Lisa Madigan, sues. The band and the driver. Water pollution and public nuisance. The city gets in on it too.
And in January 2005, the Cook County State's Attorney charges Stefan Wohl with misdemeanors. Reckless conduct, and discharging contaminants to cause water pollution. He turns himself in.
In March he pleads guilty. He gets eighteen months of probation, a hundred and fifty hours of community service, and a ten thousand dollar fine. The maximum. And the fine goes to Friends of the Chicago River, which is a group whose entire job is protecting the river.
[Pause.]
So the guy who dumped the tank paid the river people. I like that. That's the closest thing to justice this story has.
And the band fires him.
[FILL: I'd love to know what he puts on the next job application. "Reason for leaving previous position."]

## THE BAND PAYS
And the band. To their credit, once it's clear it was their bus, they own it.
They put out a statement saying the whole thing was awful, and it went against so many of the principles they hold dear. Environmentalism. Accountability. And, mostly, principles of humanity.
[Pause.]
Principles of humanity. I don't think I've ever seen those words used about a bus toilet before or since.
They donated fifty thousand dollars to the Chicago Park District, and fifty thousand to Friends of the Chicago River. And in April 2005 they settled with the state for two hundred thousand dollars. They didn't have to admit guilt. And as part of the deal they had to follow written procedures for emptying the bus tanks, and keep a written log of every single tank dump. Until 2010.

There were also personal injury lawsuits from people on the boat, separate from all that.

## THAT GUY
Okay. Time to name him. That Guy.
The suspects.
Suspect one: the Dave Matthews Band. Their name's on the bus. They said all the buses were parked. They paid the settlement. And they'll be answering for it at every Chicago show until the sun burns out.
Suspect two: the Kinzie Street bridge. Open steel grating over a river full of tour boats. If you designed a bridge specifically so this could happen, it would look exactly like the Kinzie Street bridge.
Suspect three: Stefan Wohl. The man who was alone on that bus, on that bridge, with that switch.
[Pause.]
And the verdict.
This is the rare episode where it's not a whole office full of guys. It's one guy. It's Stefan Wohl. He pleaded guilty. Nobody else was on the bus.
Dave Matthews was not on the bus. Boyd Tinsley was not on the bus. The band didn't build the bridge, and the bridge didn't flick the switch. The bridge has been sitting there minding its own business for about a hundred years.
And the band's mistake was saying "it wasn't us" before they checked the tape. That's bad. That's a lesson. But they paid for it. They paid for it in cash, and they'll pay for it in jokes forever.
So That Guy is Stefan Wohl. And I'll even give him the "awesome" part, because the rule is the rule. Of all the bridges, in all the cities, on all the tours in America, he found the one bridge with holes in it, over the one river where we already reversed the flow once because of this exact problem, at the one moment a boat full of tourists was underneath, next to the one gym with cameras on it.

I don't know if that's a guy with terrible luck or a guy with incredible aim. Either way, he's That Guy.
