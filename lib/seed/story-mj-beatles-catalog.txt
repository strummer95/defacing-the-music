## THE FRIENDSHIP
Today's story is about money. No riot, no fire, nobody gets hurt. Just two of the most famous musicians who ever lived, one dinner table, and the worst piece of advice a Beatle ever gave.
Here's the setup. Michael Jackson meets Paul McCartney at a party on the Queen Mary, the old ocean liner parked in Long Beach, California, in the seventies. And according to Michael's own telling, Paul walks up and says he's written a song for him.
The song is called "Girlfriend." Paul ends up keeping it and putting it out with Wings in 1978. Then Quincy Jones, producing Michael's "Off the Wall," hears it and goes, this would be great for Michael. Quincy had no idea Paul had already offered it to him. So the song finds Michael twice.
And Michael wants to pay that back. Paul told David Letterman, in July 2009, how it happened. Michael called him up on Christmas Day one year and asked, "Do you want to make some hits?"
Paul's answer: "So I said yeah, sure, you know being of the hit-making variety."
Being of the hit-making variety. That's the most British way anyone has ever said "I'm Paul McCartney."
So they do it. "The Girl Is Mine," on "Thriller." Then "Say Say Say," which goes to number one for six weeks at the end of 1983. And Michael is staying at Paul and Linda's house outside London while they work. Hanging out with the family. Eating Linda's cooking. Paul on Letterman: "He came to my house, and we got to know the family and stuff."
And then there's the "Say Say Say" video. Shot in October 1983 in Los Alamos, California, directed by Bob Giraldi, the "Beat It" guy. And the plot of the video, I promise I'm not making this up, is that Paul and Michael play a pair of traveling con men. Mac and Jack. Selling a fake miracle potion to suckers in the Old West.
[Pause.]
The video is about two guys who make money by getting one over on people. They were telling us the whole time.

## THE BINDER
[CLIP: Paul shows Michael the binder]
So here's the dinner. Around 1981, at the McCartney house. And Paul pulls out a thick book. It's every song Paul owns the publishing on.
Now, quick lesson, because this whole story hangs on it. When a song makes money, there's the writer's side and the publisher's side. The writer wrote it. The publisher owns it, licenses it, and keeps a big piece of every dollar it ever makes. Radio, covers, movies, commercials. Forever, basically.
And by 1981, Paul has gone hard into owning other people's songs. His company, MPL, owns the Buddy Holly catalog. It owns the music to "Grease," "Annie" and "A Chorus Line." According to Zack O'Malley Greenburg's book "Michael Jackson, Inc.," Paul tells Michael: "This is what I do. I bought the Buddy Holly catalogue, a Broadway catalogue."
And Michael is fascinated. He's asking how you buy songs, what they earn. And here's Paul, on Letterman, telling it in his own words:
"It actually kind of fell apart a little bit later because he was talking to me and asking my business advice and one of the things I said to him was 'Think about getting into music publishing,' and then he looked at me and I thought he was joking and he said 'I'm going to get yours.'"
I'm going to get yours.
[Pause.]
That's not a joke. That's a heist movie trailer. And Paul thought he was kidding.

## HOW THE BEATLES LOST THEIR OWN SONGS
Now, why didn't Paul just own the Beatles songs himself? Because he and John had already lost them once, in one of the dumbest weeks in music business history.
Back in 1963, the Beatles' music publisher, Dick James, sets up a company called Northern Songs to hold the Lennon and McCartney songs. John and Paul are kids. Paul said it on Letterman: "We got signed when we were 21 or something in a back alley in Liverpool."
And in March 1969, Dick James and his partner sell their big chunk of Northern Songs to ATV, a British TV company run by Lew Grade. They don't ask John and Paul. They don't even warn them.
And where are John and Paul that week? Both on their honeymoons. Paul married Linda on March 12th. John married Yoko on March 20th and went straight to Amsterdam to lie in bed for peace. The news hits the papers on March 28th. John reads it while he's in the bed thing.
[CLIP: Dick James's excuse]
And Dick James's excuse, to the Daily Mirror, the next day: "To telephone them would have been difficult."
Difficult. One guy is lying in a hotel bed in Amsterdam on purpose, with the press invited in. He is the easiest man in the world to find. You could have walked in and handed him the paperwork.
And a week later, James told the Mirror, "I really thought I was acting for them."
John and Paul fight it all year, and they lose. ATV ends up owning Northern Songs. John and Paul still get their writer's money, but the songs, the publishing, belong to Lew Grade. Which is why, twelve years later, Paul is sitting at that dinner table explaining publishing to Michael Jackson. He learned it the hard way.

## GIVE ME BACK MY BABIES
So Paul spends years trying to buy them back. And in 1981 he gets close.
Lew Grade's company is struggling, and Northern Songs might be for sale. Paul teams up with, of all people, Yoko Ono. John had been shot and killed in New York in December 1980. Paul and Yoko had a famously rough history, but now they're on the same side, and they offer Grade around twenty one million pounds.
Here's Paul, to The Times of London, December 1981, word for word: "He should not screw me for what he could get from somebody else. I'm not interested in buying his whole company. I just want my songs. Give me back my babies, Lew!"
Give me back my babies, Lew. That should be on a T-shirt.
Grade backs away when bigger companies start sniffing around his whole business. And in 1982, an Australian billionaire named Robert Holmes à Court takes over Grade's company. Now he's got the songs.
And in 1984, he puts the whole music company, ATV Music, up for sale. About four thousand songs. Two hundred fifty one of them are Beatles songs.
This is it. This is the moment Paul has been waiting fifteen years for.
And Paul passes.
The story, from Paul's side, is that he didn't want to buy John's half alone, because it would look like Paul grabbing John's songs. So he wanted Yoko in on it. And Yoko said no.
And when Michael's lawyer called Paul's lawyer to ask if Paul was bidding, the answer, according to J. Randy Taraborrelli's biography, was no. It was "too pricey."
And then there's the seller's side. Bert Reuter, who negotiated the sale for Holmes à Court, told Australian TV in 2009: "We had given Paul McCartney first right of refusal but Paul didn't want it at that time."
First right of refusal. They offered him first crack, and he refused.
And Yoko? Her reasoning, from Taraborrelli's book, in November 1990: "If Paul got the songs, people would have said, 'Paul finally got John.' And if I got them, they'd say, 'Oh, the dragon lady strikes again.'"

## THE AUCTION
Michael, meanwhile, has been shopping.
His lawyer is a young guy named John Branca. And Branca's first test for Michael, according to Greenburg's book, is a couple of old songs from a songwriter named Ernie Maresca. Michael doesn't recognize them. So Branca sends him a tape. A few days later Michael calls back: "You gotta get those songs! I danced to them all weekend."
They buy them. Then more. Sly Stone songs. Dion songs. Sixties hits. Michael is building a catalog, a little at a time, the way Paul told him.
Then, in September 1984, Branca tells Michael ATV is for sale. According to TheWrap's reporting, Branca's line was, "It includes a few things you might be interested in."
A few things. Like "Yesterday."
Michael's answer, per Greenburg: "You gotta get me that catalogue."
Plenty of advisers tell him it's too much money. Michael's answer, again from Greenburg: "You can't put a price on a Picasso." And: "They're the best songs that have ever been written."
On November 20th, 1984, Branca sends Holmes à Court a bid by telex. Forty six million dollars.
And Holmes à Court is a nightmare. He keeps changing the deal. The contract goes through eight drafts. Michael's team spends over a million bucks on lawyers and accountants, reading close to a million pages of contracts over four months.
And when the Americans complain he's not playing by the rules, Holmes à Court says this, per Greenburg: "The Viet Cong didn't play by the rules, and look what happened."
[Pause.]
That's a billionaire comparing selling "Hey Jude" to a jungle war.
At one point, during a meeting about raising money, Michael passes Branca a handwritten note. It says, "John please let's not bargain. I don't want to lose the deal." And then, in capital letters: "IT'S MY CATALOGUE."
In May 1985, Michael's team actually walks away. And in June, they hear two music executives, Marty Bandier and Charles Koppelman, have a tentative deal for fifty million. Those two are going to come back.
So Branca goes to work. Bandier and Koppelman's money is coming from MCA, run by Irving Azoff, who also happens to be working with Michael. Branca points that out, and Azoff pulls the funding.
Then, according to Greenburg, Branca hops on the Concorde to London to close it. And who's on the same plane? Bandier and Koppelman. Headed to the same deal. Three and a half hours across the Atlantic, at the speed of sound, all three of them pretending to read.

## THE PENNY LANE CLAUSE
[CLIP: The Penny Lane clause]
And then Holmes à Court adds his final conditions.
One: Michael has to fly to Perth, Australia, and appear on the Channel Seven Perth Telethon. Holmes à Court owned the TV station.
Two: "Penny Lane" comes out of the deal. Holmes à Court is giving that one to his teenage daughter. Every Beatles song in the catalog goes to Michael, except one, because a teenager in Perth liked it best.
[FILL: Imagine being that kid. Other kids got a car for their birthday. She got a Beatles song.]
And that's it. Bandier tries to go up by half a million. Holmes à Court says no. Bandier remembered it in Greenburg's book: Holmes à Court told him, "I'm selling this to Michael Jackson."
And Bandier's explanation of why they lost, to TheWrap: "Charles and I couldn't do the moonwalk."
August 10th, 1985. Michael Jackson buys ATV Music for forty seven and a half million dollars. In today's money, that's about a hundred and forty five million.
And Michael gives John Branca a Rolls-Royce.
In October, Michael flies to Perth and keeps his end. The local paper there remembered it forty years later: he did a short interview, met two kids, couldn't perform for contract reasons. And Jeff Newman, the telethon host, said, "Michael was very shy. I can still remember him being quite unfirm with his handshake."
Twenty hours of flying to deliver the softest handshake in Western Australia. For "Yesterday." Worth it.

## JUST BUSINESS, PAUL
So how did Paul take it?
Here's the twist people get wrong. Paul didn't lose his money from those songs. As a writer he still got his writer's share, same as always. What Michael owned was the publisher's side, the part Dick James and Lew Grade had held all those years.
So Paul, being Paul, figures: my friend owns it now. Great. Here's Paul on Letterman: "I thought, 'He is the guy historically placed to give Lennon/McCartney a good deal at last.'"
And then: "So I kept thinking it was time for a raise... You would, you know? I did talk to him about it. He kept saying 'That's just business Paul.'"
By 2001, Paul told the New York Post, "He won't even answer my letters, so we haven't talked and we don't have that great a relationship."
And Michael's side of it? According to Taraborrelli, Michael said: "If he didn't want to invest $47.5 million in his own songs, then he shouldn't come crying to me now."
[Pause.]
Which, I hate to say it, is exactly the advice Paul gave him. Buy the songs.
And Paul, same 2001 interview, on why he won't just buy them back: "The trouble is I wrote those songs for nothing and buying them back at these phenomenal sums ... I just can't do it."
So there it is. Paul McCartney taught Michael Jackson the publishing business, and Michael Jackson answered Paul's request for a raise with "That's just business, Paul." He listened. He listened too well.

## REVOLUTION, BROUGHT TO YOU BY NIKE
And then the songs start showing up in commercials.
March 26th, 1987. Nike debuts a TV ad during The Cosby Show. Basketball, running, sweat, and the Beatles' actual recording of "Revolution." John Lennon screaming over sneakers.
According to USA Today, Nike paid two hundred fifty thousand bucks to Michael for the publishing and the same again to Capitol-EMI for the recording. In today's money, Michael's half is about seven hundred thirty thousand.
And here's the part nobody remembers. Yoko was for it. She told the San Francisco Chronicle in September 1987: "Michael Jackson was very considerate. He said no to 50 offers until this one, which seemed tasteful enough."
Fifty offers. Michael turned down fifty, and the one he said yes to was a shoe company using a song about revolution.
The other Beatles were not for it. On July 28th, 1987, Apple, the Beatles' company, sued Nike, its ad agency and Capitol-EMI for fifteen million dollars. Not Michael. Michael had the right to license the song. Apple's statement: "The Beatles position is that they don't sing jingles to peddle sneakers, beer, pantyhose or anything else."
And George Harrison, in November 1987: "If it's allowed to happen, every Beatles song ever recorded is going to be advertising women's underwear and sausages."
Paul, in February 1988, kept it short: "Generally, I don't like it, particularly with the Beatles stuff."
[FILL: And here's the funny part. According to Taraborrelli, Paul had put Buddy Holly songs in commercials himself. The guy who owned Buddy Holly was upset about his songs being in ads.]
Nike pulled the ads by March 1988. The lawsuit settled in November 1989, terms secret. And Michael kept going. He even recorded his own version of "Come Together," a Beatles song he now owned, and it finally came out on his 1995 album "HIStory."

## SONY
In 1995, Michael merges ATV with Sony's publishing company. Sony pays him a reported hundred million or so for half. In today's money, that's over two hundred million. And the deal, the story goes, got sealed while Michael was in Tokyo for a concert. The new company is called Sony/ATV, and it becomes one of the biggest music publishers in the world.
So the guy who bought the songs for forty seven and a half million sold half of them ten years later for about double that, and still owned the other half. Michael was right. Paul was right. Paul was right first.
Michael died in June 2009. And in 2016, his estate sold his half to Sony for seven hundred fifty million dollars. In today's money, that's over a billion.

## GETTING THEM BACK
[CLIP: The 56-year loophole]
So how does Paul get his songs back? Not with money. With a law Congress passed in 1976.
Under U.S. copyright law, if you wrote a song before 1978 and signed it away, you can reclaim the American rights after fifty six years. You just have to file the paperwork on time. So a Beatles song from 1962 comes back in 2018.
Paul starts sending Sony/ATV the notices as early as October 2008. And then, in December 2016, Duran Duran loses a case in London against a Sony/ATV company, trying to do the same thing with their songs. A British court says their contracts don't let them. So Paul decides not to wait.
January 18th, 2017. Paul McCartney sues Sony/ATV in federal court in New York, asking a judge to confirm he can take back more than two hundred sixty songs. Starting with "Love Me Do," on October 5th, 2018.
Sony/ATV said the suit was "both unnecessary and premature," and also said it has "the highest respect for Sir Paul McCartney." Which is the corporate way of saying "we love you, see you in court."
And who was running Sony/ATV in 2017? Marty Bandier. The guy who couldn't do the moonwalk. He lost the auction to Michael in 1985, and in 2007 Sony made him chairman of the company that owned the songs anyway. So Paul ends up suing the guy who lost the Beatles catalog, for the Beatles catalog.
In June 2017, they settle. Confidential. Nobody has said what Paul got. But the law works one song at a time, each on its own fifty six year date, starting with "Love Me Do" in 2018. And Paul stopped suing.
It took him forty nine years from that honeymoon week. Give me back my babies, Lew. Lew didn't. Congress did.

## THAT GUY

Okay. Who's That Guy?
Suspect one: Dick James. Sold the Beatles' songs out from under them while they were on their honeymoons because phoning was "difficult."
Suspect two: Yoko. Said no to going in with Paul, because of the dragon lady headlines. And then told everybody Michael owning the songs was a blessing.
Suspect three: Robert Holmes à Court. Viet Cong negotiating, eight drafts, and he kept "Penny Lane" for his kid.
Suspect four: Michael. Bought the songs, wouldn't give Paul a raise, sold "Revolution" to Nike. But he told Paul exactly what he was going to do, to his face, at Paul's own table. And then he did it. That's not a villain. That's a man of his word.
And that's why the verdict is the guy who taught the class.
He told Michael publishing is where the money is. Michael said "I'm going to get yours," and he thought it was a joke. He had first right of refusal and his own lawyer called it too pricey. ## WHERE ARE THEY NOW
John Branca stayed Michael's lawyer, and after Michael died he became co-executor of the estate. He did all right. He has a Rolls.
"Penny Lane" went to Holmes à Court's daughter. Michael's company kept running it for her.
Marty Bandier ran Sony/ATV until 2019. Then he stepped down. Like Paul said: that's just business.
And Paul McCartney is a billionaire. According to the Sunday Times Rich List, he's been the richest musician in Britain for as long as they've kept the list. He still owns Buddy Holly. And for the first time since he was twenty one, he's getting his own songs back.
