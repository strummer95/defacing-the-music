## THE ORIGINAL
To understand how bad the sequel was, you have to understand how weirdly nice the first one was.
August 1969. A dairy farm in Bethel, New York, owned by a guy named Max Yasgur. And Max is not a hippie. Max is a conservative Republican dairy farmer who rents his field to a bunch of long-haired kids anyway.
The promoters were expecting about fifty thousand people. Somewhere around four hundred thousand show up. The fences go down, and it's a free concert now.
It rains. It turns into a mud pit. The food runs out. And here's the part that makes it Woodstock instead of a disaster: it mostly works.
A commune called the Hog Farm runs the free kitchen. Their guy, Wavy Gravy, gets on the mic and tells the crowd that what they have in mind is breakfast in bed for four hundred thousand.

## THE SEQUEL TO THE SEQUEL

## 1969 VERSUS 1999
Now. What did a nineteen year old in 1999 actually want to hear?
Because the culture moved. The original Woodstock poster said "3 Days of Peace and Music." The big songs were about the garden, and peace, and getting it together.
In 1999, the number one album in America, a few weeks before the festival, was Limp Bizkit's "Significant Other." It came out in June, sold somewhere around six hundred and thirty five thousand copies its first week, and knocked the Backstreet Boys off the top of the chart.
That's the mood. Boy bands on one side, guys in red caps yelling on the other, and the guys in red caps were winning.
And on that album, there's a song called "Break Stuff."
If you somehow have never heard it: it opens with Fred Durst telling you it's just one of those days. And then for about two and a half minutes he explains, in great detail and with a lot of swearing, that he is in a terrible mood and he is going to break things, and possibly your face.

## BREAK STUFF
[CLIP: Why Break Stuff was the spark]
About two thirds of the way through the set, they play "Break Stuff."
And this is where it turns. Let me tell you why.
"Break Stuff" did not burn Woodstock down. The big fires were the next night, Sunday. Anybody who tells you Limp Bizkit played one song and the place went up in flames has the timeline wrong.
But "Break Stuff" is where this crowd went from miserable to destructive. That's the moment. Before it, you've got a hot, angry, ripped off crowd. During it, the walls start coming down.
People pull plywood off the Peace Wall, and off a lighting tower, and start surfing on it. Big sheets of plywood floating across the crowd with people standing on them. People climb the towers.
And the song playing while that starts is a song whose entire message is "I'm in a bad mood and I'm going to break things." You couldn't have picked a worse soundtrack for that exact moment if you'd held auditions.
Now this next bit is real. Near the end of the set, there are photos of Fred himself on a torn down panel of the wall, surfing the crowd.
