## COLD OPEN
July 1999. Saturday night. Rome, New York.
A couple hundred thousand people are standing on hot concrete, on an old Air Force base, and they have been standing there for three days.
Earlier that afternoon, Alanis Morissette played. A nice, mellow set. Everybody swayed.
And now Fred Durst walks out, looks out at all of it, and says, more or less:
"Don't let anybody get hurt. But I don't think you should mellow out. That's what Alanis Morissette had you motherfuckers do."
[Pause.]
And then Limp Bizkit plays "Break Stuff."

## THE IDEA
Okay. Some context, for anybody who wasn't there, or wasn't born.
The original Woodstock was 1969. Peace, love, mud, Jimi Hendrix playing the national anthem at dawn. Half a million people on a farm in upstate New York. It's the most famous concert in history.
In 1994 they did a twenty-fifth anniversary. Also muddy. Mostly fine.
And in 1999, for the thirtieth, the promoters decide to do it again. Bigger. Michael Lang, one of the guys behind the original, and a big-time concert promoter named John Scher.
Now, the original Woodstock was a mess too. Let's be honest. It rained, the food ran out, the roads were jammed for miles, people were sleeping in the mud. But it was a mess in a field, with farmers handing out sandwiches. People remember it like a big hug.
[FILL: The farmer who rented them the land, Max Yasgur, became a folk hero. Nobody in 1999 was becoming a folk hero.]
So the promoters are trying to bottle that. Thirty years later. For a generation raised on MTV and mosh pits.
And they don't pick a farm.
[Pause.]
They pick Griffiss Air Force Base. In Rome, New York. A decommissioned military base.
I need you to picture what's on a military base. It's not grass. It's runways. It's tarmac. Miles and miles of flat concrete, built so planes can land on it. There are almost no trees, because trees and planes don't get along.
[FILL: I looked this up twice because I assumed I was wrong. The two main stages were about a mile and a half apart. On concrete. People walked that, back and forth, all weekend.]
And they hold it in late July.

## THE HEAT
Thursday to Sunday. July 22nd to the 25th. A couple hundred thousand people. Tickets were about a hundred and fifty bucks, which in 1999 was a lot of money for a nineteen year old.
And it's hot. High nineties, pushing a hundred. Now put that on top of a runway, which soaks up the sun all day and throws it right back up into your face. No shade. You're cooking from both directions. Like a panini.
[RIFF: the hottest show you've ever played or been at. Or use this:]
I've played some hot rooms. I've played a bar in August where the AC was a box fan pointed at the drummer. That room was a walk-in freezer compared to this.
And here's the move that makes the whole weekend. At the gates, security takes away the water and food people tried to bring in.
So now, if you want water, you buy it from them.

## THE PRICES
A bottle of water at Woodstock '99 cost four dollars.
[Pause.]
In 1999 money. Do that math on a hundred degree day, on a runway, for four days.
There were free water fountains. There just weren't enough of them, the lines were long, and as the weekend went on, some of them broke. Some of the pipes broke. And the water coming out of the broken ones mixed with everything else on the ground.
Then there's the food.
A slice of pizza was twelve dollars.
Okay. I'm from Chicagoland. I have definitely had pizza that was worth twelve bucks a slice. Even in 1999 money.
But Woodstock wasn't selling twelve dollar pizza. They were selling cheese cardboard. And charging twelve dollars for it.
[RIFF: the Chicago pizza rant. Go as long as you want. If you want a starting point:]
Real pizza has a structure. It has a philosophy. Woodstock pizza was a paper plate that somebody described cheese to.
So needless to say, a diet of Woodstock "pizza" and warm beer is not something the human body enjoys digesting. Even if you're nineteen and invincible.
Consequently, by Saturday, the plumbing gives out. The porta-potties overflow. And they don't get emptied fast enough. And the broken water lines turn the low spots into what everybody called mud.
It was not mud.
[Pause.]
So now you don't just have an exhausted crowd. You've got a couple hundred thousand people standing ankle deep in something that is not mud, staring at each other like extras in Mad Max. Or like Burning Man attendees, if Burning Man had good music.
[FILL: There were even reports of trench mouth. That's a gum infection they named after soldiers in World War One. At a music festival. In 1999.]

## SATURDAY
So that's the mood by Saturday. Hot. Broke. Thirsty. Covered in not-mud.
And Saturday night, Limp Bizkit takes the east stage.
Now here's where the legend kicks in, and I'm going to tell it the way it usually gets told, and then I'm going to tell you what we can actually prove.
The way it usually gets told: the organizers go to Fred before the set and say, "Fred. It's getting crazy out there. They're hot, they're tired, they're hungry, and the ditch weed they're passing around ain't calming anybody down. We need you to go out there and settle everybody down."
[Pause.]
Has anybody ever proven that conversation happened? No. That's the "legend goes" pile.
But if it did happen, that's like dropping me into a convention of people selling crystals and homeopathic water vials and not expecting me to be an asshole.
[RIFF: the crystals convention. This is your line, run with it. Or:]
You don't send the guy with the flamethrower to the fireworks factory and ask him to keep it down. You just don't. That's not a plan. That's a dare.
Here's what we can prove, because there's tape.
Earlier that day, Alanis Morissette played. Mellow. Lovely. And Fred gets on the mic and tells the crowd not to let anybody get hurt, but that he doesn't think they should mellow out. Because that's what Alanis Morissette had them do. With a word in there I'll let you look up.
And then they play "Break Stuff."

## BREAK STUFF
If you've never heard "Break Stuff," let me tell you what this song does to a room.
[RIFF: what "Break Stuff" does to a room. Or use this:]
Put it on in any bar, at any wedding, at any point after eleven o'clock, and something changes in people's faces. Grown adults with mortgages start shoving each other. It's a song about having a shitty day and wanting to break things. That's the whole song. It is not subtle. It's not supposed to be.
Now play it to a couple hundred thousand people who've been cooking on concrete for three days with four dollar water.
People tear plywood off the walls around the field and crowd surf on it. They climb the sound towers. The crowd gets rougher and rougher.
The story goes that Fred himself rode one of those pieces of plywood out over the crowd. That's on some lists, it's in some retellings, and I've never seen it nailed down, so it goes in the pile.
And to be fair to Fred, because I said I'd try: he did say "don't let anybody get hurt." He's said since that he was trying to get people jumping, not tearing the place apart. And Limp Bizkit didn't build the walls out of plywood.
But you don't hand that crowd a song called "Break Stuff" and act surprised when they take it as instructions.

## SUNDAY
Sunday is the last night. And it gets worse.
Somebody, an anti-gun-violence group, has the idea to hand out candles. Thousands of them. For a vigil during the Red Hot Chili Peppers' set. This is three months after Columbine, so it's coming from a real, decent place.
[Pause.]
But think about where those candles are going. A field full of trash, with plywood walls, on a crowd that's been baking and getting ripped off for three days.
So the candles become fires. And the fires get fed with the walls.
And on stage, the Red Hot Chili Peppers, who'd been asked to pay tribute to Jimi Hendrix, play Hendrix's "Fire."
[FILL: I didn't write that. I couldn't write that. If I pitched that, they'd tell me it was too much.]
After the music stops, it turns into a riot. Vendor booths get torn apart and looted. Trucks get set on fire. ATMs get broken open. State troopers in riot gear come in to clear the field.
The peace and love festival ends with riot police.

## THE SERIOUS PART
Now I'm going to stop joking, for a minute.
Three people died at Woodstock '99. A twenty four year old named David DeRosia collapsed during Metallica's set on Saturday, from the heat, and died. A woman was hit by a car. A forty four year old man died with a heart condition.
And women reported being sexually assaulted in the crowds that weekend.
[Pause.]
Those people are not the joke. They never are on this show.
The joke is the people who looked at a concrete runway in July, took away everybody's water at the gate, charged four bucks to buy it back, and thought that was a plan.

## THAT GUY
Okay. Time to name him. That Guy.
The suspects.
Suspect one: Fred Durst. He's been the easy answer for over twenty years. HBO made a documentary in 2021, "Woodstock 99: Peace, Love, and Rage." Then Netflix made "Trainwreck" in 2022. And in a lot of people's heads, the face of the whole thing is a guy in a red hat, telling a crowd not to mellow out, and then playing "Break Stuff."
Suspect two: the promoters. The guys who picked a concrete Air Force base. In July. Who took away the water at the gate and sold it back for four bucks. Who built the walls out of plywood.
Suspect three: whoever handed a few thousand candles to a crowd standing on a pile of firewood.
[Pause.]
And the verdict.
Fred did not help. Fred did not fucking help. Fred poured gasoline on a fire. And honestly, Fred is kind of awesome in this story, in the That Guy way. He did the most Fred Durst thing a person could possibly do, at the worst possible moment, and it's a big part of why anybody still talks about this festival.
But Limp Bizkit didn't pick the runway. Limp Bizkit didn't price the water. Limp Bizkit didn't build the walls out of plywood, or hand out the candles.
So That Guy is the promoters. And I have to be honest: it's not one guy. It's a whole office full of guys, who looked at four dollar water on a hundred degree runway and called it a plan.
[RIFF: your verdict. Do you let Fred off? Or use this:]
Fred lit the match. The promoters built the house out of matches, sold tickets to it, and charged you four bucks to put it out.
