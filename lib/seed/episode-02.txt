## THE ORIGINAL
To understand how bad the sequel was, you have to understand how weirdly nice the first one was.
August 1969. A dairy farm in Bethel, New York, owned by a guy named Max Yasgur. And Max is not a hippie. Max is a conservative Republican dairy farmer who rents his field to a bunch of long-haired kids anyway.
The promoters were expecting about fifty thousand people. Tickets were eighteen bucks for all three days. That's about a hundred and sixty five dollars today.
Somewhere around four hundred thousand people show up. Some counts go higher. A few hundred thousand more never even made it, because the roads were jammed for miles.
The fences go down. Nobody can sell tickets to that many people, so from the stage they basically announce it's a free concert now.
It rains. It turns into a mud pit. The food runs out. And here's the part that makes it Woodstock instead of a disaster: it mostly works.
A commune called the Hog Farm runs the free kitchen. Their guy, Wavy Gravy, gets on the mic and tells the crowd that what they have in mind is breakfast in bed for four hundred thousand.

## BREAK STUFF
[CLIP: Why Break Stuff was the spark]
About two thirds of the way through the set, they play "Break Stuff."
And this is where it turns. Let me tell you why.
"Break Stuff" did not burn Woodstock down. The big fires were the next night, Sunday. Anybody who tells you Limp Bizkit played one song and the place went up in flames has the timeline wrong.
But "Break Stuff" is where this crowd went from miserable to destructive. That's the moment. Before it, you've got a hot, angry, ripped off crowd. During it, the walls start coming down.
People pull plywood off the Peace Wall, and off a lighting tower, and start surfing on it. Big sheets of plywood floating across the crowd with people standing on them. People climb the towers.
And here's how that works, because it's not complicated. One guy pulls a board off a wall. Nothing happens to him. Then another guy does it. Nobody stops him either. And then it starts looking permitted. It's like looting. Once a few people are doing it and nobody's coming, everybody figures the rules are off. It snowballs, and every new board makes the next one easier.
And the song playing while that starts is a song whose entire message is "I'm in a bad mood and I'm going to break things." You couldn't have picked a worse soundtrack for that exact moment if you'd held auditions.
So the song didn't light the fire. The song is where the crowd figured out it was allowed to start pulling the place apart.
Now this next bit is real. Near the end of the set, there are photos of Fred himself on a torn down panel of the wall, surfing the crowd.
The lead singer of the band that everybody blames for the riot rode a piece of the Peace Wall over the crowd. I had that one in the legend pile at first because it sounds made up. It isn't.
To be fair to Fred, because I said I'd try: he's said since that "Break Stuff" means start jumping and singing, not start burning the place down. "That's not what I meant." He's also said he didn't see anybody getting hurt, because when you're looking out at a sea of people from a stage twenty feet in the air, you can't see what's going on in the middle of it.
And honestly? I believe him that he didn't see it. That doesn't mean he didn't help cause it.

## WHAT IT CHANGED
So did anybody learn anything? Kind of. Mostly the people who were watching.
Less than three months later, in October 1999, a brand new festival opens in the desert, on a polo field in Indio, California. It's called Coachella.
And they went the other way, hard. Their ads bragged about free water fountains. Plenty of restrooms. Misting tents. Rolling Stone reported they sold bottled water for two bucks, about four today, half the price of Woodstock's.
Free water was the selling point. A festival advertised "we won't let you die of thirst" like it was a headliner.
Every festival you've been to since, with a refill station and a guy in a vest yelling "hydrate," you're partly looking at the ghost of four dollar water on a runway.
And what do people think of Woodstock '99 now? In 2021 HBO puts out "Woodstock 99: Peace, Love, and Rage." In 2022 Netflix does "Trainwreck: Woodstock '99." The Ringer did a whole podcast series on it. Twenty five years later it's not a festival anymore, it's a genre. It's the festival you point to when you want to say how not to do it.
In the HBO one, they ask Michael Lang if he'd ever do another festival. And he says, "If it works."
