## DARK SIDE OF THE RAINBOW
[CLIP: Dark Side of the Rainbow]
Start Dark Side of the Moon on the lion's third roar. You'll never unsee it.

The legend: if you play Pink Floyd's Dark Side of the Moon over The Wizard of Oz with the sound off, they line up. And the band did it on purpose.

[TALK: Legend or fact? Ask Pat if he's ever tried it.]

Here's how it spread. Mid-nineties, an internet message board for Pink Floyd fans. Somebody posts that if you mute The Wizard of Oz and play the album, it syncs up. A 19-year-old college intern at a newspaper in Fort Wayne, Indiana, named Charlie Savage, reads that and writes it up in 1995. He grew up to be a national security reporter for the New York Times. And he says: "That quirky little article I wrote when I was 19 is going to follow me around for the rest of my life."

Then a Boston rock DJ talks about it on the air, and it explodes. By 1997 Pink Floyd's record label had to put out a statement: "Capitol did not have anything to do with this." In 2000, Turner Classic Movies actually aired the movie with the album on the second audio channel.

The matches people swear by: Dorothy balancing on a fence while the song says "balanced on the biggest wave." The song "Money" kicks in right when the movie switches to color. And the album ends on a heartbeat while Dorothy listens to the Tin Man's chest.

So, the band. Roger Waters, on Joe Rogan: "Bullshit." Then, "Nothing to do with anyone in Pink Floyd." Then, "Maybe it's cosmic coincidence!" Alan Parsons, who engineered the album, called it "a fabrication by someone who had much too much time on their hands," and asked the best question: "We didn't have VCRs back then; how could we have done it?" David Gilmour, on Jimmy Fallon, deadpan: "Well, of course it was." Then: "There was no planning that out. I only heard about it years later."

Verdict: LEGEND. It's a coincidence. But it's a real coincidence, and that's what makes it fun.

Somewhere out there is the anonymous guy on a Pink Floyd message board who was stoned enough to try it, and sober enough to type it up.

## PHIL COLLINS AND IN THE AIR TONIGHT
[CLIP: In the Air Tonight]
Phil Collins watched a man drown, and then sang about it to his face. Right?

The legend, and there are a lot of versions: Phil's friend is drowning, and a guy nearby could save him, and just doesn't. Phil can't swim, he can't do anything, and he never forgets that guy's face. So he writes "In the Air Tonight," sends the guy a front-row ticket, puts a spotlight on him in front of thousands of people, and sings, "I was there and I saw what you did." In some versions the cops arrest him right there at the show.

[TALK: Legend or fact? Ask Pat which version he heard.]

Eminem even put it in "Stan." That's how big this got.

Here's Phil, to the BBC: "I was just fooling around. I got these chords that I liked, so I turned the mic on and started singing." And: "The lyrics you hear are what I wrote spontaneously." And the kicker: "I don't know what this song is about. When I was writing this, I was going through a divorce."

That's it. That's the drowning. A guy whose marriage just blew up, alone in a room with a drum machine, angry. On Jimmy Fallon he put it simply: "None of it's true." And, "I was angry."

The drums? Also an accident. Before that, Phil was drumming on a Peter Gabriel session, and a talkback mic run through a heavy compressor picked him up by mistake. The engineer, Hugh Padgham: "The most unbelievable sound came out." That's the drum fill Mike Tyson air-drums in The Hangover. That's the drum fill a gorilla played in a chocolate commercial.

Verdict: LEGEND. Nobody drowned. Nobody got a front-row ticket. A sad guy improvised a song and the internet wrote a murder mystery around it.

Blame the first person who heard "I was there and I saw what you did," turned to a buddy and said, "you know what that's about, right?" And every person who's ever told it at a party since then.

## HENDRIX AND LONDON'S PARAKEETS
[CLIP: Hendrix's parakeets]
London is full of wild green parrots. And everybody blames Jimi Hendrix.

The legend: in 1968, Jimi Hendrix let a pair of parakeets go on Carnaby Street in London. And every wild parakeet in London today comes from Jimi's two birds. Estimates put it around thirty thousand of them in and around London now. Bright green. Screaming. In England.

[TALK: Legend or fact? Ask Pat what Jimi would even be doing with parakeets.]

There's a rival legend too: the birds escaped from the movie set of The African Queen, Humphrey Bogart, 1951.

So in 2019, scientists at Queen Mary University of London actually tested it. And here's how: they used geographic profiling. That's the method police use to find where a serial killer lives, based on where the crimes happen. They ran it on more than five thousand parakeet sightings, from 1968 to 2018, to find where the birds started.

Carnaby Street? Nothing. The movie studio? Nothing. They also searched 3,697 old newspaper pages mentioning parakeets, going back to 1804. Not one release by a rock star or a movie star. The oldest mention of the Hendrix story they could find was on the BBC's website in 2005.

And the birds were in England way before Jimi. There's a sighting in Norfolk in 1855. A pair with five babies in central London in 1886.

The real answer is boring: people kept letting their pet birds go. Especially during the parrot disease panics of 1929 and 1952. One 1929 newspaper reported authorities in Berlin "recommended people take precautions and not to allow parrots to kiss or bite them."

The lead scientist said the parakeets are "a consequence of repeated releases and introductions, and nothing to do with publicity stunts by musicians or movie stars."

Verdict: LEGEND.

Blame everybody in 1929 who got scared of a parrot kiss and opened the window. Jimi's innocent. For once.
