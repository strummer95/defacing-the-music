## WHO IS BILL GRUNDY
The man who put the Sex Pistols on television is not who you'd think.
Bill Grundy. Born in Manchester in 1923. Son of a factory owner. Studied geology at the University of Manchester.
Geology. Rocks. That's the start of this story. A rock guy.
When Granada Television launches up north, Grundy gets on the air reading the news, while he's still working as a geologist. And he turns out to be good at it. Sharp. Quick. A little prickly. The kind of TV guy who's smarter than the show he's on and makes sure you notice. He also wrote for the humor magazine Punch, where he played up an image of himself as a Dean Martin type. A drinker. That's going to come up.
And here's the detail that blew my mind. October 1962, on a Granada show called People and Places, Bill Grundy introduces a young band from Liverpool on their first ever TV appearance.
The Beatles.
[Pause.]
Same Bill Grundy. The guy who introduced the Beatles to television is the same guy who introduced the Sex Pistols to television. He's both bookends of an entire era of British music, and he probably didn't like either of them very much.
[RIFF: Bill Grundy, the accidental bookends of British rock. Or use this:]
That's like being the doctor who delivers a baby, and then twenty years later you're the cop who arrests him. You were there for both. You should've seen it coming.
By 1976, Grundy is presenting Today, a teatime news magazine show from Thames Television. And Today wasn't national. It was only seen in the Thames region. London and the area around it. A local show.
Which, for what's about to happen, doesn't matter at all.

## THE SHOP
The Sex Pistols basically start in a clothing store. A shop on the King's Road in London called Sex, run by Malcolm McLaren and Vivienne Westwood. Clothes built to upset people. Stuff you could not wear to your aunt's house.
McLaren isn't a musician. He's a guy with ideas, a shop, and a very good sense of what makes people angry.
Two of his customers are a couple of kids, Steve Jones and Paul Cook. They want to start a band. And from around 1974, McLaren starts advising them.
[CLIP: Steve Jones steals Bowie's gear]
Steve Jones, the Sex Pistols' guitar player, got some of the band's first gear by stealing it from David Bowie.
July 1973. Bowie plays two nights at the Hammersmith Odeon. The last shows he ever did as Ziggy Stardust. Big moment in rock history. And Steve Jones, eighteen, sneaks into the venue and steals gear. Cymbals. An amp head. Microphones. And, he says, Bowie's own microphone, with lipstick on it.
[RIFF: stealing from Bowie. Or use this:]
His explanation, to MusicRadar: "I love Bowie but I was a kleptomaniac, I couldn't help it." That's the most rock and roll thing I've ever heard. "I love you so much, I'm taking your microphone."
Now, the details of how he got in change depending on who's telling it. Steve says he hid up in the rafters. Paul Cook says they'd just go around the back. Pick your favorite. Either way, part of the early Sex Pistols setup was hot Bowie gear.
[TALK: Pat, your hero's microphone, lipstick and all, sitting right there. Do you take it?]

## JOHNNY
The Sex Pistols need a singer.
And in 1975, a kid walks into the shop. John Lydon. Born in London to Irish parents. Grew up in a council flat in Finsbury Park, North London.
When Lydon was seven, he got meningitis. Spent about a year in hospital, in and out of a coma. He's said it messed with his memory, and he's called it the first step that put him on the road to Rotten.
And according to the most repeated version, McLaren notices him because he's wearing a T-shirt that says "I Hate Pink Floyd."
[Pause.]
Not "I love" anything. "I hate Pink Floyd." That's the résumé. That's the cover letter.
And the audition. August 1975. In the shop. No band. No rehearsal room. They put Alice Cooper's "I'm Eighteen" on the shop's jukebox, and Lydon sings along to it. Flails around. Sneers. And that's it. He's in.
[FILL: Lydon tells it like he was the one who put the song on. Others say the band talked him into it. Either way, the audition was a jukebox. Somebody should frame that jukebox.]
Steve Jones gives him the name Johnny Rotten. Because of his teeth.
That's how bands work. You don't pick your nickname. Your guitar player picks it, and it's always about your worst feature.
With Glen Matlock on bass, they play their first gig in November 1975, at an art college in London. And for the next year, they build up a following. Kids from the suburbs, dressed like the shop. One bunch gets called the Bromley Contingent. They're about to be on TV.

## THE BOOKING
October 1976. The Sex Pistols sign with EMI. Huge label. The company behind the Beatles' records. The reported advance is forty thousand pounds. In today's money, that's about three hundred thousand pounds.
They put out their first single, "Anarchy in the U.K." They're rehearsing for a tour, the Anarchy Tour.
[CLIP: Freddie Mercury's teeth]
The Sex Pistols got on TV because Freddie Mercury hadn't been to the dentist in fifteen years.
Queen is on EMI too. And Queen is booked on Today, with Bill Grundy, on December 1st. And at the last minute, Queen cancels. The story goes that Freddie had a toothache so bad he had to make his first trip to a dentist in about fifteen years. The fifteen years is the legend part. The toothache turns up in nearly every retelling.
[FILL: If Freddie flosses, punk might show up a little later. Think about that next time you skip it.]
So the show needs a band. EMI's publicist, Eric Hall, told the Telegraph that a Today producer called him "asking whether I had anyone for him because they needed to fill their 'pop slot.'" And Hall says he answered, "I've got one which you won't want, the Sex Pistols."
[Pause.]
That's the pitch. "You won't want them." And they said yes.
And the band didn't even want to go. They're in rehearsal. Glen Matlock remembered, "This big limousine turned up outside this place." They only got in after McLaren phoned and told them, "If you don't do it your wages will be stopped this week."
And Hall says McLaren had demanded "a limousine and champagne and beer" to get them there. And then Hall added, "I suppose I was partly responsible for them going on and drinking so much."
So the label sent four drunk punks to replace Queen on a teatime show. Along with a few of their fans from the Bromley Contingent, including a young woman who'll soon be famous herself, Siouxsie Sioux.
And here's the part nobody tells you. The producer, Mike Housego, later said the band had only been given ninety seconds. And Thames's own files show a researcher had warned him ahead of time that they might swear.
They knew. Somebody wrote it down.

## THE INTERVIEW
So here's how the Sex Pistols' ninety seconds went. Fair warning: transcripts of this interview don't match word for word, so I'm going with the version the Guardian published, and the bits every version agrees on.
Grundy introduces them, and right away he's needling them. He says they're as drunk as he is. Later, he told the Guardian he was "stone-cold sober. You cannot do a job like I do without being sober."
Then he goes after the money. They got forty thousand pounds from a record company. Isn't that against their whole anti-materialism thing?
And Glen Matlock says, "the more the merrier."
And Steve Jones says, "We've fucking spent it, ain't we?"
That's the first one. It kind of slides by.
Then Grundy brings up Beethoven and Mozart, and Johnny Rotten mutters a rude word under his breath. Grundy catches it. And instead of moving on, like any host in the history of television would, he asks Johnny to say it again.
[Pause.]
He asks him to say it again. On live teatime television.
And Johnny does.
Then Grundy turns to the fans standing behind the band. Siouxsie tells him she's always wanted to meet him. And Grundy says, "We'll meet afterwards, shall we?"
And that is when Steve Jones wakes up. He calls Grundy a dirty old man.
And here's the exchange, as the transcripts give it:
Grundy: "Well keep going, chief, keep going. Go on. You've got another five seconds. Say something outrageous."
Jones: "You dirty bastard."
Grundy: "Go on, again."
Jones: "You dirty fucker."
Grundy: "What a clever boy."
Jones: "What a fucking rotter."
[RIFF: "What a fucking rotter." The most British insult ever said on TV. Or use this:]
"Rotter." That's a word from an old movie. That's what a guy in a top hat calls another guy in a top hat who cheated at cards. Steve Jones drops two F-bombs and then reaches all the way back to 1910.
And Grundy wraps it up. He tells the viewers he hopes he'll be seeing them soon. And then he turns to the band and says, "I'm not seeing you again."
Roll credits. The band is bouncing around in the background. The whole thing was a couple of minutes.

## THE PAPERS
[CLIP: The lorry driver kicks in his telly]
A forty-seven-year-old lorry driver got so furious about the Sex Pistols swearing in front of his eight-year-old son that he kicked in the screen of his brand new TV.
That's how the papers reported it. The TV cost three hundred eighty pounds. In today's money, that's about twenty-nine hundred. And then, according to the coverage, he phoned Thames to complain about the damage to his telly.
He kicked it, and then he called the TV station like they owed him a TV.
Johnny Rotten's response, later: "Haven't they ever heard of the off button?"
It's been repeated in pretty much every history of punk since. But it comes from tabloid coverage during a tabloid frenzy, and even his name gets told different ways. So it goes in the "the papers reported" pile.
[RIFF: the lorry driver. Or use this:]
He's so angry about bad language in front of his kid, that he, in front of his kid, kicks a TV until it breaks. That's not a lesson in manners. His son learned more words that night from Dad than from the Sex Pistols.
And the lorry driver wasn't alone. The Guardian reported two days later that the studio's twelve switchboard lines were jammed with complaints.
The next morning, the papers go nuclear.
The Daily Mirror's front page, December 2nd: "The Filth and the Fury!"
The Daily Express: "Fury at Filthy TV Chat."
The Daily Telegraph: "4-Letter Words Rock TV."
And the show wasn't even national. Most of the country never saw it. And now the whole country is reading about it over breakfast. That's how the Sex Pistols went national. Not on TV. In the papers, about TV.
And it sold records. "Anarchy in the U.K." sold eighteen hundred copies the day after.
[TALK: Pat, what's the angriest you've ever been at a TV? Did the TV survive?]

## THE ANARCHY TOUR
The Sex Pistols' Anarchy Tour was supposed to go all over Britain. Within a day, venues started bailing. Preston's entertainments officer said, "We don't ever want them here." By one later count, thirteen of the nineteen dates got called off.
It spread so far that Lou Reed said a London venue cancelled his shows, too. Because it lumped him in with the Pistols. Lou Reed. Collateral damage.
In Derby, the council said the other bands on the bill could play, but the Pistols could only go on if they did a private show for the council first. An audition. For the city council.
McLaren said no. His words: "We're not going to encourage censorship. If we perform for these idiots we'll end up doing matinees for every council in the country."
And then there's Caerphilly, in Wales, December 14th, 1976. One of the few shows that actually happened, at the Castle Cinema.
A fan named Dave Smitham wrote about it years later, and Far Out magazine retold it: the town's pubs hadn't just closed. They'd barricaded their windows. There was a preacher in the car park across the street, shouting at the line of teenagers. When he took a break, carol singers took over.
And inside? Here's Smitham: "Valley's punks and the curious gathered themselves in the cinema's seven front rows. Row upon row of empty seats tiered to the back of the unheated, seedy auditorium… In marked contrast to the anticipated mayhem, everyone was exceedingly well-behaved."
[Pause.]
The town barricaded the pubs, brought in a preacher and a choir, and the punks sat in the front seven rows and behaved.

## THE FALLOUT
So what happened to everybody?
Bill Grundy got suspended for two weeks. Thames's director of programmes, Jeremy Isaacs, called it "a gross error of judgment" caused by "inexcusably sloppy journalism." And Grundy? He said he'd set out "to prove that these louts were a foul-mouthed set of yobs. And that is what I did prove."
He never worked in prime-time television again. Today itself ended the following year.
[FILL: Twenty years of solid TV, the Beatles' first TV intro, and he's remembered for two minutes of daring a punk band to swear. That's show business. You can do a thousand good gigs and they'll remember the one where your pants split.]
On January 6th, 1977, EMI dropped the Pistols. The statement said EMI was "unable to promote this group's records in view of the adverse publicity generated over the last two months."
Glen Matlock was out of the band in early 1977, and Sid Vicious came in on bass, despite, by most accounts, not really knowing how to play it yet.
[CLIP: Signed outside Buckingham Palace, dropped in six days]
In March 1977, the Sex Pistols signed with A&M Records on a table set up outside Buckingham Palace. At seven in the morning. And the deal lasted six days.
The signing party was at A&M's offices. Far Out's account: Sid smashed up a bathroom so hard he cut himself and trailed blood through the halls. Johnny yelled at everybody he met. Steve Jones got caught in another bathroom with a fan.
And the label had pressed up "God Save the Queen." About twenty-five thousand copies. Nearly all of them got destroyed. The few that survived are some of the most valuable records in the world.
Now, the official story at the time was that A&M's own stars, including Rick Wakeman from Yes, threatened to leave the label if the punks stayed. Wakeman told Mojo, in 2025, that this was "total bollocks." He says A&M in America didn't want the band, and the U.K. boss needed an excuse. Wakeman: "So he used me and Richard Carpenter, saying that we sent them letters saying that we'd leave the label."
[Pause.]
Richard Carpenter. Of the Carpenters. They blamed the Sex Pistols getting fired on the guy from the Carpenters.
[TALK: Pat, six days. What's the fastest you've ever been fired from anything, and was there a bathroom involved?]
And the band walked away with about seventy-five thousand pounds. In today's money, that's about half a million. For six days.
Then they signed with Virgin, put out "God Save the Queen" in the middle of the Queen's Silver Jubilee, and it went to number two.
And Freddie Mercury got one last scene. Not long after, Queen and the Pistols ended up recording at the same studio in North London. Sid starts heckling him: "Ah, Freddie Mercury, still bringing ballet to the masses are you?"
And Freddie, without missing a beat: "Oh yes, Mr Ferocious, dear. We're doing our best."
That Grundy clip has been called the most requested clip in British TV history. And Bill Grundy died in 1993, in Stockport, at sixty-nine. The first line of his obituary was never going to be the Beatles.

## THAT GUY
[CLIP: That Guy verdict]
Okay. Time to name him. The Sex Pistols That Guy.
The suspects.
Suspect one: Steve Jones. The guy who actually said it. Stole gear from Bowie, swore on live TV, called a grown man a rotter. He's guilty of everything he's ever been accused of, mostly by himself.
Suspect two: Freddie Mercury's teeth. And, fine, EMI, who offered up a band with the words "you won't want them," sent a limo full of champagne, and threatened their wages if they didn't get in.
Suspect three: Bill Grundy. The host. The one with twenty years of experience.
[Pause.]
And the verdict.
It's Bill Grundy. His researcher warned the producer they might swear. He asked Johnny to repeat the rude word. He flirted with a fan on air. And then, with the kid already swearing at him, he said, "Go on. Say something outrageous." He didn't just let it happen. He asked for it. Twice.
[THAT GUY STING]
Now, this one is disputed. Some people say Grundy was set up, the old guard getting ambushed by kids who wanted a headline. And there are people who've argued he deserved better than being remembered for this. That's fair.
But Bill Grundy introduced the Beatles to TV, and then he introduced the Sex Pistols to the whole country. Not on purpose. But he did it. That's a career.
