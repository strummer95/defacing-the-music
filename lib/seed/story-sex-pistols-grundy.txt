## COLD OPEN
December 1st, 1976. London. Teatime.
Families across the London area are sitting down in front of the telly for the early evening news magazine show. Local stories. Light interviews. Something to have on while the kettle boils.
And on screen, a fifty-something TV host in a suit is leaning toward a young guitar player, and he's egging him on. Go on. You've got five seconds. Say something outrageous.
[Pause.]
And the kid does.
Live. No delay. Into living rooms. At dinner.
By the next morning, it's on the front page of every paper in the country.
## TODAY
Today: a geologist who became a TV host, a clothing shop on the King's Road, a guitar player who stole his gear from David Bowie, a singer who auditioned to a jukebox, Freddie Mercury's teeth, and a couple of minutes of television that made a whole country lose its mind.
Also a lorry driver and his telly. We'll get there. That one comes with an asterisk.
And somewhere in this story, there's a guy. There always is. And this time, the guy might be the one asking the questions.

## WHO IS BILL GRUNDY
First I need you to meet the host. Because he's not who you'd think.
Bill Grundy. Born in Manchester in 1923. Son of a factory owner. Studied geology at the University of Manchester.
[Pause.]
Geology. Rocks. That's the start of this story. A rock guy.
When Granada Television launches up north, Grundy gets on the air reading the news, while he's still working as a geologist. And he turns out to be good at it. Sharp. Quick. A little prickly. The kind of TV guy who's smarter than the show he's on and makes sure you notice.
And here's the detail that blew my mind. October 1962, on a Granada show called People and Places, Bill Grundy introduces a young band from Liverpool on their first ever TV appearance.
The Beatles.
[Pause.]
I looked this up twice. The guy who introduced the Beatles to television is the same guy who introduced the Sex Pistols to television. He's both bookends. He's the opening act and the closing act of an entire era of British music, and he probably didn't like either of them very much.
[RIFF: Bill Grundy, the accidental bookends of British rock. Or use this:]
That's like being the doctor who delivers a baby, and then twenty years later you're the cop who arrests him. You were there for both. You should've seen it coming.
By 1976, Grundy is presenting Today, a teatime news magazine show from Thames Television. And this is important for later. Today wasn't national. It was only seen in the Thames region. London and the area around it. A local show.
Which, for what's about to happen, doesn't matter at all.

## THE SHOP
Now, the Sex Pistols.
The Sex Pistols basically start in a clothing store. A shop on the King's Road in London called Sex, run by Malcolm McLaren and Vivienne Westwood. Clothes built to upset people. Stuff you could not wear to your aunt's house.
And Malcolm McLaren is our first side character. Because McLaren isn't a musician. He's a guy with ideas, a shop, and a very good sense of what makes people angry.
Two of his customers are a couple of kids, Steve Jones and Paul Cook. They want to start a band. And from around 1974, McLaren starts advising them.
[Pause.]
Now let me tell you about Steve Jones. Because Steve Jones, by his own admission, was a thief. Not a little thief.
In July 1973, David Bowie plays two nights at the Hammersmith Odeon. The last shows he ever did as Ziggy Stardust. Big moment in rock history. And Steve Jones, eighteen, sneaks into the venue and steals gear. Cymbals. An amp head. Microphones. And, he says, Bowie's own microphone, with lipstick on it.
[RIFF: stealing from Bowie. Or use this:]
He's said he loved Bowie and he couldn't help it. He was a kleptomaniac. That's the most rock and roll thing I've ever heard. "I love you so much, I'm taking your microphone."
Now, the details of how he got in change depending on who's telling it. Steve says he hid up in the rafters. Paul Cook says they'd just go around the back. Pick your favorite. Either way, part of the early Sex Pistols setup was hot Bowie gear.

## JOHNNY
They need a singer.
And in 1975, a kid walks into the shop. John Lydon. Born in London to Irish parents. Grew up in a council flat in Finsbury Park, North London.
And when Lydon was seven, he got meningitis. Spent about a year in hospital, in and out of a coma. He's said it messed with his memory, and he's called it the first step that put him on the road to Rotten.
So that's the guy. And according to the most repeated version, McLaren notices him because he's wearing a T-shirt that says "I Hate Pink Floyd."
[Pause.]
Not "I love" anything. "I hate Pink Floyd." That's the résumé. That's the cover letter.
And the audition. August 1975. In the shop. No band. No rehearsal room. They put Alice Cooper's "I'm Eighteen" on the shop's jukebox, and Lydon sings along to it. Flails around. Sneers. And that's it. He's in.
[FILL: Lydon tells it like he was the one who put the song on. Others say the band talked him into it. Either way, the audition was a jukebox. Somebody should frame that jukebox.]
Steve Jones gives him the name Johnny Rotten. Because of his teeth.
[Pause.]
That's how bands work. You don't pick your nickname. Your guitar player picks it, and it's always about your worst feature.
With Glen Matlock on bass, they play their first gig in November 1975, at an art college in London. And for the next year, they build up a following. A crowd of fans start showing up to every show. Kids from the suburbs, dressed like the shop. One bunch gets called the Bromley Contingent. Remember them. They're about to be on TV.

## THE BOOKING
October 1976. The Sex Pistols sign with EMI. Huge label. The company behind the Beatles' records. And the reported advance is forty thousand pounds.
They put out their first single, "Anarchy in the U.K." They're about to start a tour, the Anarchy Tour.
And now we need to talk about Freddie Mercury's teeth.
[Pause.]
Queen is on EMI too. And Queen is booked on Today, with Bill Grundy, on December 1st. And at the last minute, Queen cancels, because Freddie Mercury needs emergency dental work.
The story goes that Freddie hadn't been to a dentist in about fifteen years, and the only appointment he could get was the same time as the show. I can't prove the fifteen years. But the dental emergency is in every account.
[FILL: If Freddie flosses, punk might show up a little later. Think about that next time you skip it.]
So EMI needs to fill the slot, and they send their new band. The Sex Pistols. And along with the band come a few of their fans from the Bromley Contingent, including a young woman who'll soon be famous herself, Siouxsie Sioux.
And here's the setup. A live teatime show. A host who's been on TV for twenty years and has nothing to prove. And four guys and a few of their fans, in a TV studio, about to go live.

## THE INTERVIEW
So I'm going to walk you through this. And honesty check up front: there are a bunch of transcripts of this interview floating around, and they don't match word for word. So I'm going to quote the bits that every version agrees on, and paraphrase the rest.
Grundy introduces them, and right away he's needling them. In one widely reported line, he says they're as drunk as he is. Later, he insisted he was sober.
[Pause.]
Then he goes after the money. He brings up that they got forty thousand pounds from a record company. Isn't that against their whole anti-materialism thing?
And Glen Matlock says, "the more the merrier."
And then, depending on the transcript, Steve Jones says something like, they've already spent it. With a swear in the middle.
That's the first one. It kind of slides by.
Then Grundy brings up Beethoven and Mozart, and Johnny Rotten mutters a rude word under his breath. Grundy catches it. And instead of moving on, like any host in the history of television would, he asks Johnny to say it again.
[Pause.]
He asks him to say it again. On live teatime television.
And Johnny does.
Then Grundy turns to the fans standing behind the band. Siouxsie tells him she's always wanted to meet him. And Grundy flirts back, something like, did you really, we'll meet afterwards, shall we?
And that is when Steve Jones wakes up. He calls Grundy a dirty old man.
And Grundy, instead of shutting it down, eggs him on. "Go on, you've got another five seconds. Say something outrageous."
[Pause.]
And Steve Jones calls him a "dirty bastard."
Grundy says go on, again.
"You dirty fucker."
And then the line that I think about more than the swearing: "What a fucking rotter."
[RIFF: "What a fucking rotter." The most British insult ever said on TV. Or use this:]
"Rotter." That's a word from an old movie. That's what a guy in a top hat calls another guy in a top hat who cheated at cards. Steve Jones drops two F-bombs and then reaches all the way back to 1910.
And Grundy wraps it up. Closes the show. Roll credits. The band is bouncing around in the background. The whole thing was a couple of minutes.

## THE PAPERS
And the next morning, the papers go nuclear.
The Daily Mirror's front page, December 2nd: "The Filth and the Fury!"
The Daily Express: "Fury at Filthy TV Chat."
The Daily Telegraph: "4-Letter Words Rock TV."
[Pause.]
Remember, the show wasn't even national. Most of the country never saw it. And now the whole country is reading about it over breakfast. That's how the Sex Pistols went national. Not on TV. In the papers, about TV.
Now the lorry driver. And this is the asterisk I promised.
The papers reported that a forty-seven-year-old lorry driver, watching at home with his eight-year-old son, got so furious about the language that he kicked in the screen of his brand new TV. He's quoted as saying it blew up and knocked him backwards.
[Pause.]
That's how the papers told it. It's been repeated in pretty much every history of punk since. But it comes from tabloid coverage during a tabloid frenzy, and even his name gets told different ways in different retellings. So I'm putting it in the "the papers reported" pile.
[RIFF: the lorry driver. Or use this:]
I need you to picture this guy. He's so angry about bad language in front of his kid, that he, in front of his kid, kicks a TV until it explodes. That's not a lesson in manners. That's a new kind of language. His son learned more words that night from Dad than from the Sex Pistols.

## THE FALLOUT
So what happened to everybody?
Bill Grundy got suspended for two weeks. The reporting says Thames called it a gross error of judgement. And it's generally agreed it wrecked his career. Today itself ended the following year.
[FILL: Twenty years of solid TV, the Beatles' first TV intro, and he's remembered for two minutes of daring a punk band to swear. That's show business. You can do a thousand good gigs and they'll remember the one where your pants split.]
The Sex Pistols' Anarchy Tour started falling apart. Local councils and venues banned the shows. Sources disagree on how many dates actually happened, but most of the tour got cancelled.
In January 1977, EMI dropped them. Their statement said it felt it couldn't promote the band's records, given the bad publicity.
Then in March 1977, they signed with A&M Records. On a table set up outside Buckingham Palace. At seven in the morning.
[Pause.]
That deal lasted six days.
So in about six months, the Sex Pistols got signed and dropped by two of the biggest labels in the world. And it only made them bigger. Glen Matlock was out of the band in early 1977, and Sid Vicious came in on bass, despite, by most accounts, not really knowing how to play it yet. And then they put out "God Save the Queen" in the middle of the Queen's Silver Jubilee, because of course they did.
And that Grundy clip? It's been called the most requested clip in British TV history. In 2000, a documentary about the band took its title straight off the Mirror's front page: "The Filth and the Fury."
Bill Grundy died in 1993, in Stockport, at sixty-nine. And the first line of his obituary was never going to be the Beatles.

## THAT GUY
Okay. Time to name him. That Guy.
The suspects.
Suspect one: Steve Jones. The guy who actually said it. Stole gear from Bowie, swore on live TV, called a grown man a rotter. He's guilty of everything he's ever been accused of, mostly by himself.
Suspect two: Freddie Mercury's teeth. And, fine, EMI, who sent a punk band to a teatime show as a substitute for Queen, like those are the same thing. That's a whole office full of guys.
Suspect three: Bill Grundy. The host. The one with twenty years of experience.
[Pause.]
And the verdict.
It's Bill Grundy. He asked Johnny to repeat the rude word. He flirted with a fan on air. And then, with the kid already swearing at him, he said, "Go on. Say something outrageous." He didn't just let it happen. He asked for it. Twice.
Now, this one is disputed. Some people say Grundy was set up, that he was the old guard getting ambushed by kids who wanted a headline. And there are people who've argued Grundy deserved better than being remembered for this. That's fair.
But here's the "that guy is usually awesome" part. Bill Grundy introduced the Beatles to TV, and then he introduced the Sex Pistols to the whole country. Not on purpose. But he did it. That's a career.

You can't dare a punk band to say something outrageous and then act surprised when they do. That's like handing Keith Moon a match.
