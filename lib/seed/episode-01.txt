## THE SMOTHERS BROTHERS
Quick thing for anybody under fifty, because I had to look some of this up too.
The Smothers Brothers Comedy Hour was a Sunday night variety show on CBS. Tom and Dick Smothers. Brothers. Folk singers who did comedy. Tommy played the dumb one, Dick played the straight man, and the bit was mostly Tommy losing an argument with his brother. "Mom always liked you best." That was their line.
And on paper that sounds like the safest show on television. Two guys in matching sweaters doing folk songs.
But they were actually the cool show. They booked rock bands. They got in fights with CBS about politics and the war almost every week. Network censors basically had a desk with their name on it.
And here's the decision nobody talks about. The show asked for the smashing. Pete Townshend said it in a VH1 interview years later: the Smothers Brothers had a pretty radical show for the time, and they bravely asked the band if they'd destroy their instruments. So The Who didn't sneak this onto CBS. CBS sent them an invitation.
And one thing I got wrong in the pilot, so let me fix it here. This wasn't live. They taped it on a Friday, September 15th, 1967, at CBS in Los Angeles, and it aired that Sunday night.
[Pause.]
Which is somehow worse. Because that means CBS had two full days with the tape. Two days to watch a drum kit explode and a guitar player come out of the smoke with his hair on fire. And somebody at CBS watched it, and thought about it, and said, "Yeah. Sunday. Family hour. Send it."
[FILL: So, to recap the booking: a network that's already nervous about this show lets it bring on the band that destroys its own equipment for a living. And then says, "Can you make the ending bigger?"]

## THE SETUP
The Who come on to do two songs. "I Can See for Miles," which was brand new, and is going to become their biggest American hit. And "My Generation," which is the one where they wreck everything.
And for TV, the wrecking has to be bigger. It's a stage show, but now it's a stage show on a screen the size of a toaster. So the plan is a small explosive charge in Keith's bass drum. A flash. A pop. A little puff of smoke. Great television. Everybody goes home.
Now here's where the story gets murky, and I'm going to be straight with you about it, because nobody agrees on exactly what happened.
Start with the part almost nobody tells. The show's producer, a guy named Allan Blye, said years later that in rehearsal the charge was a powder puff. A little poof. Not good enough for television. So he asked for a bigger effect. And the band, it turns out, had already told their own guy the same thing.
[FILL: So that's two different people asking for more boom, and neither one of them knows the other one asked. That's not a plan. That's a group text with no group.]
And then there's Keith.
The most common version: Keith pays off a stagehand to load extra flash powder. Some versions say the crew loaded the normal amount, and Keith added more on top when nobody was watching.
And how much extra? That's where the legend takes over. Depending on who's telling it, it was double. Three times. Ten times. Nobody measured it. Nobody was standing there with a kitchen scale. The number just gets bigger every time somebody tells it at a bar.
[FILL: And I owe you one here. Back in the pilot I said "ten times" like it was a fact. It's not. Ten times is the legend. The truth is "a lot more than the guy who asked for a bigger bang had in mind."]
And I keep thinking about the stagehand. Because in every version of this, there's a guy. There's a crew guy holding a can of flash powder, and a twenty one year old Englishman with a reputation for blowing up toilets walks up and goes, "Mate. A bit more."

And it is not a pop.

It is a bomb. On a soundstage. There's a wall of smoke where the drums used to be, and when the footage clears, the heads and hoops are blown clean off the bass drum.
The blast reportedly took out one of the cameras and the studio's monitors too. So for a second, the people running the show couldn't even see the thing that just happened on their show.
Roger Daltrey was out front with the microphone, minding his own business. Years later he told Ultimate Classic Rock, "It blew me flat on my face." The singer. Not even near the drums.

Now. My favorite part. And this is on tape, so no asterisk.
[CLIP: Pete takes Tommy Smothers' guitar]
The smoke is still clearing. Pete Townshend's hair is still smoking. The drummer is bleeding. The audience doesn't know if this is the bit or a real emergency.
And Tommy Smothers walks back out. With an acoustic guitar. Smiling. Because the show must go on.
[Pause.]
Pete takes the guitar out of his hands. And smashes it on the floor.
[FILL: Not angry. Not a fight. Just, "Oh, I'll take that." Like a waiter clearing a plate.]
And Tommy just kind of stands there. Holding the air where his guitar was. On his own show. In front of the whole country.
Now here's the part I didn't know until I went digging. The guitar was the bit. Tommy said it himself, decades later, in an oral history of the show: the whole thing was supposed to end with Pete smashing his guitar. That was the plan. The comedy button on the segment.
The bomb was not the plan. Tommy said there was shrapnel everywhere, and he honestly thought there were going to be dead bodies. And because he's walking out there looking around in a panic, everybody watching figured it couldn't be part of the act. He called it a real, authentic moment. Which is a very polite way of saying, "I thought I was going to die on my own show and then a guy took my guitar."

That's the best part. Tommy still does the bit. The building just blew up, he's got no idea if his guest drummer is alive, and he hands over the guitar right on cue anyway. That's a professional. I think about that a lot. A guy built an entire career on being the nice brother who loses arguments, and then he loses his guitar to a British art student covered in soot. That's the most Tommy Smothers thing that could have possibly happened.

## THE LEGEND
One more piece of this, and this one gets the biggest asterisk of the episode.
The story goes that Bette Davis was backstage that night, waiting to go on. Bette Davis. Old Hollywood royalty. All About Eve. And when the bass drum went off, she was so shaken up that she fainted. Into the arms of Mickey Rooney.
[Pause.]
Is that true? Here's what I can tell you. Bette Davis and Mickey Rooney really were on the show that week. So the casting is right.
But Dick Smothers, the straight-man brother, said he was standing in the wings next to Bette Davis, watching, when it went off. Here's Dick, in the Hollywood Reporter's oral history of the show: "Bette Davis could see an atom bomb go off and wouldn't faint. I don't even know if she blinked." And Tommy has denied the fainting story too.

## THE FALLOUT
So what happened to everybody?
Keith Moon got bandaged up and went right back to being Keith Moon for another eleven years. If anything, the explosion was great for business. It's one of the most famous clips in rock and roll.
Pete Townshend got a story he's told for over fifty years, and ears that still ring.
"I Can See for Miles" became The Who's biggest hit single in America. So, in a way, the explosion worked. Two months later nobody was asking who opened for Herman's Hermits.
[FILL: Herman's Hermits, meanwhile. Wonderful band. Lovely people. Did not explode on television. And nobody's making a podcast about them.]
And the Smothers Brothers? CBS canceled the show in 1969. Not because of The Who. They had a long fight with the network over politics and censorship, and the network finally just pulled the plug. But I'd like to think somebody at CBS had a folder, and the drum kit was in it.
