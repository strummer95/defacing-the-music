## 1992
Okay. Context. Because if you were not alive and holding a cassette in 1992, you need to understand how big these two bands were.
In August 1991, Metallica put out the self-titled record. Everybody calls it the Black Album. "Enter Sandman." "Nothing Else Matters." Your mom knew "Enter Sandman." That's how big.
A month later, Guns N' Roses put out "Use Your Illusion I" and "Use Your Illusion II." Two albums. Same day. Because one wasn't enough for Axl.
So in 1992, somebody has the idea: put them together. One stadium tour. Metallica and Guns N' Roses, co-headlining. The biggest metal band in the world and the biggest rock band in the world, every night, for the price of one ticket.
[RIFF: what that ticket meant if you were sixteen in 1992. Or use this:]
If you were sixteen in 1992 and you got a ticket to this, you were the most important person in your high school for about a week. You wore the shirt to school. You wore it until your mom made you take it off.

## WHOSE IDEA WAS THIS
So who's the somebody? Who had the idea?
By the account in Louder, the music magazine, the idea of hitting stadiums with Guns came from Lars Ulrich. Metallica's drummer. The business brain of the band.
May 12th, 1992. A club in Hollywood called the Gaslight. Lars and Slash sit down at a press conference and announce it. And Lars says, quote, "I've always wanted to play with Guns N' Roses since we met them in 1987."
That's sweet. That's a guy who's a fan. And that's a guy who has never had to wait for Axl Rose.
Then somebody has to figure out who goes on last. And this is the decision the whole night hangs on. Guns N' Roses were adamant that they close every show. And Metallica? By most accounts, Metallica happily went along with it. Fine. You close. We'll go on first, play hard, and be at the bar.
[FILL: Axl wanted Nirvana to open. Kurt Cobain said no. So Faith No More got the slot. Even the opening act was a whole thing.]
Now, here's why that decision matters. Metallica showed up on time. Every night. Played. Went home. Guns N' Roses had a habit of going on late. Like, late. Accounts from the tour describe fans waiting two or three hours after Metallica finished before Guns came out.
And they didn't ease into it, either. Opening night, July 17th, RFK Stadium in Washington, D.C. The break between bands was supposed to run about two hours. It took three. Night one. That's the first data point. Everybody on that tour knew, from the very first night, that the band going on last was the band that couldn't be counted on to go on.
[FILL: If you've ever been in a band with a guy who's always late to practice, multiply him by a stadium.]

## JAMES
Let me tell you about James Hetfield. Because the guy we're about to set on fire had a hell of a road to get here.
James grew up in Southern California. His parents were Christian Scientists. And if you don't know, that's a faith that leans hard on prayer instead of doctors.
When James was sixteen, his mother died of cancer. She had refused medical treatment because of her beliefs.
He wrote about it later. The song "The God That Failed," on the Black Album, comes out of that. He's said it's less about blaming God and more about the fact that his mom's faith didn't save her.
I'm not going to make a joke there, and I'm telling you this for a reason: this is a guy who grew up in a house where you didn't go to the doctor. Keep that in your head for the hospital part.
A couple years later, in 1981, he answers an ad. A kid named Lars Ulrich, a Danish kid whose dad was a pro tennis player, is looking for people to start a band. Lars plays drums. Sort of. That's Metallica.
And here's a little piece of foreshadowing. In 1986, Metallica's opening for Ozzy Osbourne, and James breaks his wrist. Skateboarding. Can't play guitar. So the band hands his rhythm guitar parts to their guitar tech, a guy named John Marshall, and James just sings.
File John Marshall away. He's the guy who keeps getting the call.
[FILL: Imagine your whole job is tuning another guy's guitars, and every few years, the universe goes, "Hey. Get out there."]

## AXL
And then there's Axl.
Axl Rose was born William Bruce Rose Jr., in Lafayette, Indiana. A Midwestern kid. Not far from here, honestly. A couple hours down the road from Chicago. And in the early eighties he heads out to Los Angeles with his buddy Izzy Stradlin, and a few years later they're Guns N' Roses, and "Appetite for Destruction" is selling more copies than God.
[CLIP: Axl walks off and gets arrested at JFK]
And Axl, by 1992, has a reputation. For being brilliant. And for walking off.
Here's the big one. July 2nd, 1991. Riverport Amphitheatre, outside St. Louis. In the middle of "Rocket Queen," Axl spots a fan with a camera. Accounts differ on the exact order of events, but Axl goes after the guy, ends up in the crowd, and then the band leaves the stage.
And St. Louis riots. Dozens of people hurt. Damage in the hundreds of thousands. A judge issues a warrant for Axl for inciting a riot.
And here's my favorite part. A year later, July 1992, Axl flies back into New York. And they arrest him at the airport. At JFK. That same summer. Five days before the tour with Metallica even starts.
He ended up with probation and a fifty thousand dollar donation to local charities.
[RIFF: getting arrested at the airport. Or use this:]
You know how your worst fear at the airport is that they lose your bag? Axl's was a little different. Axl got off the plane and his bag was fine. Axl was what got lost.
So that's who's closing this show. A singer who had already walked off one stage and watched a city riot. A year earlier. And everybody who signed off on the billing knew it, because it was in every newspaper in America.

## THE BIG OWE
[CLIP: The Big Owe, paid for with cigarettes]
Now, the venue. Montreal's Olympic Stadium.
It was built for the 1976 Summer Olympics. And Montreal locals have a nickname for it. Officially it's "the Big O."
Unofficially? "The Big Owe."
Because the thing cost so much money that Quebec didn't finish paying it off until 2006. Thirty years. And a big chunk of it was paid by a special tax on tobacco. Quebec basically paid for its stadium with cigarettes.
[FILL: The final bill, with interest and repairs, comes out to about one point six billion Canadian dollars. They were still paying it off when the iPod came out.]
And here's the kicker, at least according to the stadium history folks. In 2006, Quebec brought in an indoor smoking ban. Which cut into the tobacco tax. Which slowed down paying off the stadium. So Montreal's health got better, and its stadium payments got worse.
In 1992 it's also where the Montreal Expos play baseball. Put a pin in the Expos. They come back in this story in a very specific way.
That's the room. A concrete UFO that the whole province chipped in for, one pack of smokes at a time. And by the end of the night, people are going to be throwing parts of it.
And the guy putting on the show in Montreal is a local legend. Donald Tarlton. In the business he was Donald K. Donald, and when he died, the concert trade press called him the ultimate Canadian impresario. And he went into this one, by his own account years later, nervous. He told the Montreal Gazette, quote, "People had told me to be careful. So I was not looking for aggravation at all, whatsoever."
He was not going to get his wish.

## PYRO PETE
Before we get to the fire, you need to meet the fire guy. Or, actually, the fire guy who wasn't there.
[CLIP: Lars calls the pyro guy a traitor]
There's a pyro tech named Pete Cappadocia. Goes by Pyro Pete. He'd done pyrotechnics for both bands before this tour. Metallica and Guns. So when the two bands team up, Pete's the obvious guy to do both.
And Lars catches wind of this. And Lars, according to Pete on a podcast called Appetite for Distortion years later, says, more or less, "I don't want you shooting Guns N' Roses. I only want you shooting us."
So Pete has to pick one. And Guns offered him the better deal. He goes with Guns.
And Pete says Lars had one of his people confirm how much Guns was paying him, and then Lars called him back, cursed him out, and called him a traitor.
[RIFF: getting cursed out by Lars Ulrich over a paycheck. Or use this:]
Imagine getting a voicemail from Lars Ulrich calling you a traitor. Over your job. You're not in the band. You blow stuff up for a living. And the drummer of the biggest metal band on Earth is taking it personally that you went where the money was. Which, honestly, is a very Lars thing to take personally.
So Metallica's pyro that summer is run by somebody else. Pete describes him as Metallica's own guy, a guy who'd shot their pyro in Europe. I couldn't find his name in anything. And I'm not going to go digging for it, and you'll see why.

## FADE TO BLACK
So. August 8th, 1992. Faith No More opens. Metallica comes on. And the show's going great.
And Metallica has a big production. Lots of pyro. And when they get to "Fade to Black," there's a pyro cue at the front of the stage.
And here's a little detail. "Fade to Black" opens with that clean, pretty guitar part, so James is wearing a double-neck guitar for it. A big ESP double-neck. Two guitars bolted together. That guitar is going to come up again in about a minute.
James walks into the cue.
[Pause.]
Now. How. This is the part where the stories don't all line up, and I'm going to give you all of them, because I don't get to pick.
James's own version: he was confused about where he was supposed to stand. He walked forward. Walked back. And the guy running the pyro didn't see that he'd walked back out into that spot.
Pyro Pete, the guy who was working for Guns that night, has told it this way: the stage monitors had been moved, and a piece of the pyro had been moved, and James walked to his spot. And by one write-up of that interview, he took about an extra half step and put his arm right over the cue just as it went.
There's also a version that the pyro had gotten bigger. Far Out Magazine says the crew had increased the charges and put them at the front of the stage and out on the wings. And one account says James got told about the new stuff on the wings, but nobody told him the old stuff out front was still there too. That's one account. I can't nail it down past that.
And the general summary you'll see everywhere is "a miscommunication with the pyro crew."
[FILL: Honestly, all of those can be true at the same time. That's what makes it scary. Nobody has to screw up very much.]
And the flame went up. Accounts describe it as around twelve feet. And it caught James on the left side. His arm. His hand.
And that double-neck? James wrote about it in his guitar book, "Messengers," years later. The guitar took some of the flame. It shielded him from the worst of it. The neck is unpainted wood, so you can still see where it got scorched. He kept it.
[RIFF: the half step. Every musician knows the stage mark. Or use this:]
Every musician who's ever played a real stage knows the tape on the floor. You hit your mark. And most nights, if you miss it by half a step, the worst that happens is you're out of the light. On this night, half a step was the difference between a great show and a burn unit.

## THE NUTS
So James gets off stage. And he's in a lot of pain. And this next part is his own story, so it's his to tell, and he tells it a little bit proud.
He's backstage, waiting for a ride to the hospital. And somebody, a security guy by most tellings, bumps right into his burned hand.
And James says, quote, "I just lost it. I screamed and punched him right in the nuts."
[RIFF: the worst possible moment to bump into James Hetfield. Or use this:]
There is a list of people you do not bump into. And at the top of that list, permanently, is the guy from Metallica whose arm was on fire a few minutes ago. Whatever else that security guy did in his career, nobody remembers it. They remember this.
Then he's off to Montreal General Hospital. A hospital spokesperson confirmed he got treated there, and that he checked out during the night. The guy from the house with no doctors, getting patched up and walking out before sunrise.
And back at the stadium, Lars, Kirk Hammett and Jason Newsted went out and told the crowd they couldn't keep going. Metallica's done for the night.

## THE WAIT
So now there's a giant hole in the night. And everybody's looking at Guns N' Roses to fill it.
Lars said it later, on VH1's "Behind the Music," more or less: this was the moment. It was up to Guns to go out and play a blinding three-hour set and save the night.
And Guns N' Roses does what Guns N' Roses does.
They take their time.
By the Montreal Gazette's account, it was more than two hours after Metallica left before Guns came on. Some put it at two hours and fifteen minutes. Sitting in a concrete stadium. In August. After you just watched the singer of the other band get hurt.
[RIFF: the longest you ever waited for a band. Or use this:]
I've waited for bands. I've waited for drummers who were "five minutes out" for an hour. But I've never waited two hours, in a stadium, right after watching the other singer catch fire. That's not a wait. That's a hostage situation with merch.

## NINE SONGS, MAYBE
So Guns comes out. And, look, Axl had been having throat trouble. They'd already called off shows that summer because of it. Slash had told the Montreal Gazette that Axl had a problem with a vocal cord. So this was not made up on the spot.
They also had trouble with the monitors. Slash said the PA and the monitors were feeding back the whole time. Duff and Gilby Clarke, according to Axl, said they couldn't hear.
And at some point that night, according to the Gazette's look back, Axl tells the crowd, flat as a pancake, "There ain't no rules, Montreal."
Which is a weird thing to say right before you prove there are rules, and the main rule is the singer leaves whenever he wants.
So they start. And they don't finish.
How long did they play? Depends who you ask. The New York Times said about fifty five minutes. The Seattle Times said more like fifteen. UPI said five songs. The Gazette count was nine. I'm going with "not long."
And Axl walks off. And somewhere in there he tells the crowd, more or less, that this will be their last show for a long time. The exact words get quoted different ways, so I'm not going to pretend I know which ones.
And backstage, the promoter, Donald Tarlton, is scrambling. He told the Gazette that people like him were trying to figure out, with managers and agents and representatives, how to get Axl back on stage. And, his words, "Finally, it didn't work." He described what he got from Axl and his people that night as "a complete lack of rational thought."
[Pause.]
And the crowd had now seen two headliners. Neither one finished.

## THE RIOT
And Montreal goes off.
Fans pour out of the stadium and into the streets around it. Depending on whose estimate you believe, it's anywhere from a couple thousand people to ten thousand.
They set fires. Small fires in the stands, fires in garbage cans. They trash whole sections of seating. They smash windows. A police cruiser gets flipped over. A police spokesman said thirty police cars got damaged. Eight officers had minor injuries. Twelve people got arrested that night. Police showed up in riot gear and used tear gas to get the streets back.
And the Expos pin? Here it is. Rioters broke into the Expos souvenir shop and looted it. So somewhere in Montreal in August 1992, a guy who just got stiffed by Guns N' Roses walked home in a stolen Expos hat. The one team in the building that night that had nothing to do with any of it.
And the damage, depending on the source, was in the hundreds of thousands of dollars. One account puts it close to four hundred thousand.
[RIFF: rioting at the stadium your own province is still paying for. Or use this:]
Think about that. These are Quebec taxpayers. Trashing a stadium that Quebec taxpayers were still paying for. With cigarette money. They were punching themselves in the wallet.
And the people who ran the stadium, the Régie des installations olympiques, reportedly banned Guns N' Roses from the building for life.

## AFTERMATH
So, where did everybody land?
Guns N' Roses did not come back to Montreal with the original guys for a long time. They played there in 2010, in a later lineup. But Axl, Slash and Duff didn't share a Montreal stage again until 2017. Twenty five years. And they did it at a park on an island, not at the stadium. By most accounts, that one went fine. Nobody flipped anything.
And Faith No More, the opening band who had to watch all of this? They got kicked off the tour about six weeks later. Mostly because of friction with the Guns camp.
And Metallica? Metallica didn't play the Olympic Stadium again until August 2023. Thirty one years. Two nights.
And then the blame game. Because there's always a blame game. Doug Goldstein, who managed Guns N' Roses, has pinned the Montreal disaster on, of all people, Lars. The way Goldstein tells it, Lars had been messing with the pyro cues before the show.
And who stepped in to say that's not what happened? Pyro Pete. The guy Lars called a traitor. The guy who'd been on the Guns payroll that night. Defending Lars. Against Guns's own manager.
[FILL: That's the most rock and roll thing in this whole episode. The traitor shows up with an alibi for the guy who called him a traitor.]

## THE SERIOUS PART
Back to James. I want to be straight about what happened to him, because it's easy to turn this into a punchline and it wasn't one.
James Hetfield suffered second and third degree burns. Reports at the time described deep second degree burns on the back of his left hand, and second and third degree burns up his arm.
Jason Newsted, the bass player, has said if James had been breathing in at that moment, he would have been dead. Newsted also said, about seeing the arm, "That was his whole fucking arm."
[Pause.]
Lars promised the crowd the band would come back to Montreal to make it up.
And here's the part I respect. Metallica postponed about six shows. Canceled one, in Vancouver. And seventeen days later, August 25th, they were back. Outside Phoenix. James on stage with his arm wrapped from his elbow to his fingers. Singing. Not playing.
And who's playing rhythm guitar?
John Marshall. The guitar tech. Again. He finished the tour.
And years later, Jason Newsted said that accident is what brought Metallica together. And James made a full recovery. He's still out there playing guitar, which, after what happened to that hand, is a big deal.
And about the pyro guy, the one nobody names? James wrote in "Messengers" that he doesn't hate the pyro guy. Doesn't hate anyone or anything. Things happen for a reason. If the guy who got burned is letting it go, I'm letting it go too.
