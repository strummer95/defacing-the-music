## 1992
Okay. Context. Because if you were not alive and holding a cassette in 1992, you need to understand how big these two bands were.
In August 1991, Metallica put out the self-titled record. Everybody calls it the Black Album. "Enter Sandman." "Nothing Else Matters." Your mom knew "Enter Sandman." That's how big.
A month later, Guns N' Roses put out "Use Your Illusion I" and "Use Your Illusion II." Two albums. Same day. Because one wasn't enough for Axl.
So in 1992, somebody has the idea: put them together. One stadium tour. The biggest metal band in the world and the biggest rock band in the world, every night, for the price of one ticket.

## WHOSE IDEA WAS THIS
So who's the somebody?
By the account in Louder, the music magazine, the idea of hitting stadiums with Guns came from Lars Ulrich. Metallica's drummer. The business brain of the band.
May 12th, 1992. A club in Hollywood called the Gaslight. Lars and Slash announce it at a press conference. And Lars says, quote, "I've always wanted to play with Guns N' Roses since we met them in 1987."
That's sweet. That's a guy who's a fan. And that's a guy who has never had to wait for Axl Rose.
Then somebody has to figure out who goes on last. And this is the decision the whole night hangs on. Guns N' Roses were adamant that they close every show. And by most accounts, Metallica happily went along with it. Fine. You close. We'll go on first, play hard, and be at the bar.
[FILL: Axl wanted Nirvana to open. Kurt Cobain said no. So Faith No More got the slot. Even the opening act was a whole thing.]
Here's why that matters. Metallica showed up on time. Every night. Guns N' Roses had a habit of going on late. Opening night, July 17th, RFK Stadium in Washington, D.C. The break between bands was supposed to run about two hours. It took three. Night one. Everybody on that tour knew, from the very first night, that the band going on last was the band that couldn't be counted on to go on.

## THE GUITAR TECH
Quick bit of foreshadowing, because it pays off.
In 1986, Metallica's opening for Ozzy Osbourne, and James Hetfield breaks his wrist. Skateboarding. Can't play guitar. So the band hands his rhythm guitar parts to their guitar tech, a guy named John Marshall, and James just sings.
Keep an eye on John Marshall. He's the guy who keeps getting the call.
[FILL: Imagine your whole job is tuning another guy's guitars, and every few years, the universe goes, "Hey. Get out there."]

## AXL AND ST. LOUIS
And then there's Axl.
Axl Rose was born William Bruce Rose Jr., in Lafayette, Indiana. A Midwestern kid, a couple hours down the road from Chicago. He heads out to Los Angeles in the early eighties with his buddy Izzy Stradlin, and a few years later they're Guns N' Roses, and "Appetite for Destruction" is selling more copies than God.
And by 1992, Axl has a reputation. For being brilliant. And for walking off.
[CLIP: Axl, the camera and the fire hose]
July 2nd, 1991. Riverport Amphitheatre, outside St. Louis. Brand new venue. In the middle of "Rocket Queen," Axl spots a biker in the crowd taking pictures with a camera he wasn't supposed to have.
Louder, the music magazine, has what Axl said into the mic: "Wait, take that! Take that! Now! Get that guy and take that!"
Security doesn't move fast enough for him. So Axl goes into the crowd after the guy himself. Accounts differ on the exact order of events. But he gets hauled back up, grabs the mic, and says, by Louder's telling: "Well, thanks to the lame-ass security, we're going home!"
He slams the mic down. And the band leaves.
And St. Louis riots.
People tear the plastic seats off their mounts and throw them at security. They pull down the video screens. They hang off the cables under the giant lighting rig. And the crew comes up with a plan. Here's Louder:
"A fire hose was rolled out onstage, the idea being to blast anyone who dared try and break the cops' perimeter."
That was the plan. The hose, by that same account, had almost no water pressure. And one guy jumped up on stage, stepped into the trickle, dropped his pants, and waved himself at the police. The big tactical response, and a guy used it as a shower.
Fans walked out with the band's gear. Speaker cabinets. Mixing boards. And one eyewitness remembered a guy in the parking lot selling Guns N' Roses piano keys. Five bucks each. In today's money, that's about twelve.
I'll be straight about this part: sixty five people got hurt that night, twenty five of them police officers. Damage ran into the hundreds of thousands of dollars.
[TALK: You're in the parking lot and a guy's selling Axl's piano keys. Do you buy one?]
A judge issues a warrant for Axl for inciting a riot. And Axl just doesn't go back to Missouri.
Until a year later. July 12th, 1992. Axl flies into New York, and they arrest him at the airport. At JFK. Five days before the tour with Metallica even starts. Axl said he spent his time in custody, his words, "writing autographs for cops."
He ended up with probation and fifty thousand dollars to local charities. In today's money, that's about a hundred and fifteen thousand.
So that's who's closing every show on this tour. A singer who walked off a stage and watched a building riot, one year earlier. It was in every newspaper in America. Everybody who signed off on the billing knew.

## THE BIG OWE
[CLIP: The Big Owe, paid for with cigarettes]
Montreal's Olympic Stadium was built for the 1976 Summer Olympics. Officially, locals call it "the Big O."
Unofficially? "The Big Owe."
Because the thing cost so much that Quebec didn't finish paying it off until 2006. Thirty years. And a big chunk of it was paid by a special tax on tobacco. Quebec basically paid for its stadium with cigarettes.
[FILL: The final bill, with interest and repairs, comes out to about one point six billion Canadian dollars. They were still paying it off when the iPod came out.]
In 1992 it's also where the Montreal Expos play baseball. Put a pin in the Expos. They come back in a very specific way.
That's the room. A concrete UFO the whole province chipped in for, one pack of smokes at a time.
And the guy putting on the show in Montreal is a local legend. Donald Tarlton. In the business he was Donald K. Donald, and when he died, the concert trade press called him the ultimate Canadian impresario. He went into this one nervous. He told the Montreal Gazette, quote, "People had told me to be careful. So I was not looking for aggravation at all, whatsoever."
He was not going to get his wish.

## PYRO PETE
Before we get to the fire, you need to meet the fire guy. Or, actually, the fire guy who wasn't there.
[CLIP: Lars calls the pyro guy a traitor]
There's a pyro tech named Pete Cappadocia. Goes by Pyro Pete. He'd done pyrotechnics for both bands before this tour. So when the two bands team up, Pete's the obvious guy to do both.
And Lars, according to Pete on a podcast called Appetite for Distortion years later, says, more or less, "I don't want you shooting Guns N' Roses. I only want you shooting us."
So Pete has to pick one. Guns offered him the better deal. He goes with Guns.
And Pete says Lars had one of his people confirm how much Guns was paying him, and then Lars called him back, cursed him out, and called him a traitor.
You're not in the band. You blow stuff up for a living. And the drummer of the biggest metal band on Earth is taking it personally that you went where the money was. Which is a very Lars thing to take personally.
[TALK: Would you take the extra money if it meant Lars Ulrich calling you a traitor?]
So Metallica's pyro that summer is run by somebody else. Pete describes him as Metallica's own guy, who'd shot their pyro in Europe. His name isn't in anything I found. And I'm not going digging for it, and you'll see why.

## FADE TO BLACK
August 8th, 1992. Faith No More opens. Metallica comes on. And the show's going great.
Metallica has a big production. Lots of pyro. And when they get to "Fade to Black," there's a pyro cue at the front of the stage.
"Fade to Black" opens with that clean, pretty guitar part, so James is wearing a double-neck for it. A big ESP double-neck. Two guitars bolted together. That guitar is going to matter in about a minute.
James walks into the cue.
[Pause.]
Now, how. The stories don't all line up, so you get all of them.
James's own version: he was confused about where he was supposed to stand. He walked forward. Walked back. And the guy running the pyro didn't see that he'd walked back into that spot.
Pyro Pete has told it this way: the stage monitors had been moved, and a piece of the pyro had been moved, and James walked to his spot. By one write-up of that interview, he took about an extra half step and put his arm right over the cue just as it went.
And Far Out Magazine says the crew had increased the charges and put them at the front of the stage and out on the wings.
[FILL: All of those can be true at the same time. That's what makes it scary. Nobody has to screw up very much.]
The flame went up. Accounts describe it as around twelve feet. And it caught James on the left side. His arm. His hand.
And that double-neck? James wrote about it years later in his guitar book, "Messengers." The guitar took some of the flame and shielded him from the worst of it. The neck is unpainted wood, so you can still see where it got scorched. He kept it.
Most nights, if you miss your mark by half a step, the worst that happens is you're out of the light. On this night, half a step was the difference between a great show and a burn unit.

## THE NUTS
[CLIP: James punches the guy who bumped his hand]
James Hetfield, with his arm freshly burned, punched a guy in the nuts. And this is his own story, so it's his to tell, and he tells it a little bit proud.
He's backstage, waiting for a ride to the hospital. And somebody, a security guy by most tellings, bumps right into his burned hand.
James: "I just lost it. I screamed and punched him right in the nuts."
There is a list of people you do not bump into. And at the top of that list, permanently, is the guy from Metallica whose arm was on fire a few minutes ago. Whatever else that security guy did in his career, nobody remembers it. They remember this.
Then he's off to Montreal General Hospital. A hospital spokesperson confirmed he got treated there, and that he checked out during the night.
And back at the stadium, Lars, Kirk Hammett and Jason Newsted went out and told the crowd they couldn't keep going. Metallica's done for the night.
[TALK: Somebody bumps your burned hand. Is the punch fair?]

## THE WAIT
So now there's a giant hole in the night. And everybody's looking at Guns N' Roses to fill it.
Lars said it later on VH1's "Behind the Music," more or less: this was the moment. It was up to Guns to go out and play a blinding three-hour set and save the night.
And Guns N' Roses does what Guns N' Roses does.
They take their time.
By most counts, it was two hours and fifteen minutes after Metallica left before Guns came on. Sitting in a concrete stadium. In August. After you just watched the singer of the other band get hurt.
I've waited for drummers who were "five minutes out" for an hour. I've never waited two hours, in a stadium, right after watching the other singer catch fire. That's not a wait. That's a hostage situation with merch.
And when they come out, look, Axl had been having throat trouble. They'd already called off shows that summer because of it. Slash told the Montreal Gazette that Axl had a problem with a vocal cord. So this was not made up on the spot. And at some point that night, according to the Gazette, Axl tells the crowd, "There ain't no rules, Montreal."
Which is a weird thing to say right before you prove there are rules, and the main rule is the singer leaves whenever he wants.
How long did they play? The New York Times said about fifty five minutes. The Seattle Times said more like fifteen. UPI said five songs. The Gazette count was nine. I'm going with "not long."
And Axl walks off. Somewhere in there he tells the crowd, more or less, that this will be their last show for a long time.
Backstage, Donald Tarlton is scrambling. He told the Gazette, "people like myself were trying to figure out with managers and agents and representatives how to get him back onstage. Finally, it didn't work." He described what he got from Axl and his people that night as "a complete lack of rational thought."
The crowd had now seen two headliners. Neither one finished.
[TALK: Two hours in the seats after the other singer got burned. How long do you wait before you go home?]

## THE RIOT
And Montreal goes off.
Fans pour out of the stadium and into the streets around it. Depending on whose estimate you believe, it's anywhere from a couple thousand people to ten thousand.
They set small fires in the stands and in garbage cans. They trash whole sections of seating. They smash windows. A police cruiser gets flipped over. A police spokesman told UPI thirty police cars got damaged and eight officers had minor injuries. Twelve people got arrested. Police showed up in riot gear and used tear gas to get the streets back.
And the Expos pin? Here it is. Rioters broke into the Expos souvenir shop and looted it. So somewhere in Montreal in August 1992, a guy who just got stiffed by Guns N' Roses walked home in a stolen Expos hat. The one team in the building that had nothing to do with any of it.
Quebec taxpayers, trashing a stadium Quebec taxpayers were still paying for. With cigarette money. They were punching themselves in the wallet.
And the people who ran the stadium reportedly banned Guns N' Roses from the building for life.
[TALK: Two riots in two years, both on a walk-off. At what point is it on the guy and not the city?]

## AFTERMATH
So where did everybody land?
Axl, Slash and Duff didn't share a Montreal stage again until 2017. Twenty five years. At a park on an island, not the stadium. By most accounts, that one went fine. Nobody flipped anything.
Faith No More, the opening band who had to watch all of this, got kicked off the tour about six weeks later, mostly over friction with the Guns camp.
Metallica didn't play the Olympic Stadium again until August 2023. Thirty one years.
And then the blame game. Doug Goldstein, who managed Guns N' Roses, has pinned the Montreal disaster on, of all people, Lars. The way Goldstein tells it, Lars had been messing with the pyro cues before the show.
And who stepped in to say that's not what happened? Pyro Pete. The guy Lars called a traitor. The guy who'd been on the Guns payroll that night. Defending Lars. Against Guns's own manager.
[FILL: The traitor shows up with an alibi for the guy who called him a traitor. That's the most rock and roll thing in this whole story.]

## THE SERIOUS PART
Back to James, straight, because this wasn't a punchline.
James Hetfield suffered second and third degree burns. Reports at the time described deep second degree burns on the back of his left hand, and second and third degree burns up his arm. Jason Newsted has said if James had been breathing in at that moment, he would have been dead.
Metallica postponed about six shows and canceled one, in Vancouver. And seventeen days later, August 25th, they were back. Outside Phoenix. James on stage with his arm wrapped from his elbow to his fingers. Singing. Not playing.
And who's playing rhythm guitar?
John Marshall. The guitar tech. Again. He finished the tour.
James made a full recovery, and he's still out there playing. And about the pyro guy nobody names? James wrote in "Messengers" that he doesn't hate him. Doesn't hate anyone or anything. If the guy who got burned is letting it go, I'm letting it go too.

## THAT GUY
[CLIP: That Guy verdict]
Okay. Time to name him. That Guy.
Suspect one: the pyro side. Whatever version you believe, the fire went off with a person standing on it, and that is the one thing pyro exists to never do. But the guy is unnamed, the stories don't agree, and the man who got burned has forgiven him. He's off the board.
Suspect two: Lars Ulrich. It was his idea, by the Louder account. He looked at Axl Rose, a man with an active warrant for starting a riot, and said, "I've always wanted to play with these guys." His me-or-them phone call sent Pyro Pete to the other band. When Guns demanded to close every night, Metallica said fine. That's a lot of fingerprints for a drummer.
Suspect three: Axl Rose.
And the verdict.
For James's arm? Nobody. Too many versions, every one of them a guy doing a hard job half a second off, and James himself let it go.
For how this got made? Lars gets an assist. He wanted the tour so bad he signed off on letting the guy who walks off stages close every show.
But the riot? That one has a name on it.
He had a stadium full of people who had just watched a man catch fire. He had the easiest hero moment in rock history sitting right in front of him. Go out, say "James is going to be okay, we're playing our asses off for him," and play. Instead: two hours and change of nothing, "there ain't no rules, Montreal," a short set, and a walk-off, while the local promoter begs managers and agents to get him back out there. And he'd already watched a building riot over a walk-off one year earlier. He'd seen this movie. He was in it.
That Guy is Axl Rose.
[THAT GUY STING]
Axl gets a doctor's note for the throat. He doesn't get a doctor's note for St. Louis. And he definitely doesn't get one for the two hours.
And the good guys in this one? John Marshall, who got handed the rhythm guitar for the biggest metal band in the world twice, both times because James got hurt, and both times just plugged in and did it. And Pyro Pete, who got called a traitor by Lars and still showed up years later to say, nope, it wasn't Lars.

The lesson, if there is one: always know where your mark is. And if you're the second band, show up.
