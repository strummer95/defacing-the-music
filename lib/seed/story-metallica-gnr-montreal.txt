## COLD OPEN
August 8th, 1992. Montreal. Olympic Stadium.
Tens of thousands of people. Two of the biggest bands on the planet on one bill. Metallica is up first, and they're cooking.
They go into "Fade to Black." The slow one. The intro.
And James Hetfield walks toward a spot on the stage where, at that exact second, there is not supposed to be a person.
[Pause.]
A column of fire goes up.
And by the end of the night, the singer of the first band is in a hospital, the singer of the second band has walked off with a sore throat, and the people of Montreal are flipping police cars over in the street.

## 1992
Okay. Context. Because if you were not alive and holding a cassette in 1992, you need to understand how big these two bands were.
In August 1991, Metallica put out the self-titled record. Everybody calls it the Black Album. "Enter Sandman." "Nothing Else Matters." Your mom knew "Enter Sandman." That's how big.
A month later, Guns N' Roses put out "Use Your Illusion I" and "Use Your Illusion II." Two albums. Same day. Because one wasn't enough for Axl.
So in 1992, somebody has the idea: put them together. One stadium tour. Metallica and Guns N' Roses, co-headlining, with Faith No More opening. The biggest metal band in the world and the biggest rock band in the world, every night, for the price of one ticket.
[RIFF: what that ticket meant if you were sixteen in 1992. Or use this:]
If you were sixteen in 1992 and you got a ticket to this, you were the most important person in your high school for about a week. You wore the shirt to school. You wore it until your mom made you take it off.
On paper, perfect. In practice, these two bands were about as compatible as a cat and a bathtub.
Metallica showed up on time. Played hard. Went home. Guns N' Roses insisted on closing every night, and they had a habit of going on late. Like, late. Accounts from the tour describe fans waiting two or three hours after Metallica finished before Guns came out. And by most accounts, Metallica didn't understand why Axl couldn't just show up.
[FILL: If you've ever been in a band with a guy who's always late to practice, multiply him by a stadium.]

## JAMES
Let me tell you about James Hetfield. Because the guy we're about to set on fire had a hell of a road to get here.
James grew up in Southern California. His parents were Christian Scientists. And if you don't know, that's a faith that leans hard on prayer instead of doctors.
When James was sixteen, his mother died of cancer. She had refused medical treatment because of her beliefs.
[Pause.]
He wrote about it later. The song "The God That Failed," on the Black Album, comes out of that. He's said it's less about blaming God and more about the fact that his mom's faith didn't save her.
I'm not going to make a joke there, and I'm telling you this for a reason: this is a guy who grew up in a house where you didn't go to the doctor. Keep that in your head for the hospital part.
A couple years later, in 1981, he answers an ad. A kid named Lars Ulrich, a Danish kid whose dad was a pro tennis player, is looking for people to start a band. Lars plays drums. Sort of. That's Metallica.
And here's a little piece of foreshadowing. In 1986, Metallica's opening for Ozzy Osbourne, and James breaks his wrist. Skateboarding. Can't play guitar. So the band hands his rhythm guitar parts to their guitar tech, a guy named John Marshall, and James just sings.
[Pause.]
Remember John Marshall. John Marshall is the guy who keeps getting the call.
[FILL: Imagine your whole job is tuning another guy's guitars, and every few years, the universe goes, "Hey. Get out there."]

## AXL
And then there's Axl.
Axl Rose was born William Bruce Rose Jr., in Lafayette, Indiana. A Midwestern kid. Not far from here, honestly. A couple hours down the road from Chicago. And in the early eighties he heads out to Los Angeles with his buddy Izzy Stradlin, and a few years later they're Guns N' Roses, and "Appetite for Destruction" is selling more copies than God.
And Axl, by 1992, has a reputation. For being brilliant. And for walking off.
Here's the big one. July 2nd, 1991. Riverport Amphitheatre, outside St. Louis. In the middle of "Rocket Queen," Axl spots a fan with a camera. Accounts differ on the exact order of events, but Axl goes after the guy, ends up in the crowd, and then the band leaves the stage.
And St. Louis riots. Dozens of people hurt. Damage in the hundreds of thousands. A judge issues a warrant for Axl for inciting a riot.
And here's my favorite part. A year later, July 1992, Axl flies back into New York. And they arrest him at the airport. At JFK. That same summer.
He ended up with probation and a fifty thousand dollar donation to local charities.
[RIFF: getting arrested at the airport. Or use this:]
You know how your worst fear at the airport is that they lose your bag? Axl's was a little different. Axl got off the plane and his bag was fine. Axl was what got lost.
So that's who's closing this show. A singer who had already walked off one stage and watched a city riot. A year earlier.

## THE BIG OWE
Now, the venue. Montreal's Olympic Stadium.
It was built for the 1976 Summer Olympics. And Montreal locals have a nickname for it. Officially it's "the Big O."
Unofficially? "The Big Owe."
[Pause.]
Because the thing cost so much money that Quebec didn't finish paying it off until 2006. Thirty years. And a big chunk of it was paid by a special tax on tobacco. Quebec basically paid for its stadium with cigarettes.
[FILL: I looked this up twice. The final bill, with interest and repairs, comes out to about one point six billion Canadian dollars. They were still paying it off when the iPod came out.]
And here's the kicker. In 2006, Quebec brought in an indoor smoking ban. Which cut into the tobacco tax. Which slowed down paying off the stadium. So Montreal's health got better, and its stadium payments got worse.
That's the room. A concrete UFO that the whole province chipped in for, one pack of smokes at a time. Remember that, because by the end of the night, people are going to be throwing parts of it.

## FADE TO BLACK
So. August 8th, 1992. Faith No More opens. Metallica comes on. And the show's going great.
And Metallica has a big production. Lots of pyro. And when they get to "Fade to Black," there's a pyro cue at the front of the stage.
James walks into it.
[Pause.]
Now. How. This is the part where the stories don't all line up, and I'm going to give you all of them, because I don't get to pick.
James's own version: he was confused about where he was supposed to stand. He walked forward. Walked back. And the guy running the pyro didn't see that he'd walked back out into that spot.
A pyro tech who worked that tour, Pete Cappadocia, has told it differently on a podcast years later: the stage monitors and a piece of the pyro had been moved, and James took about an extra half step and put his arm right over the cue just as it went.
And the general summary you'll see is "a miscommunication with the pyro crew."
[FILL: Honestly, all three of those can be true at the same time. That's what makes it scary. Nobody has to screw up very much.]
And the flame went up. Accounts describe it as around twelve feet. And it caught James on the left side. His arm. His hand.
[RIFF: the half step. Every musician knows the stage mark. Or use this:]
Every musician who's ever played a real stage knows the tape on the floor. You hit your mark. And most nights, if you miss it by half a step, the worst that happens is you're out of the light. On this night, half a step was the difference between a great show and a burn unit.

## THE WAIT
So James is hurt, he's on his way to the hospital, and Metallica is done for the night. I'm going to come back to James, properly, in a minute. But there's still a second band in the building.
Metallica's set is cut short. So now there's a giant hole in the night. And everybody's looking at Guns N' Roses to fill it.
And Guns N' Roses does what Guns N' Roses does.
They take their time.
[Pause.]
By the Montreal Gazette's account, it was more than two hours after Metallica left before Guns came on. Other accounts say longer. Sitting in a concrete stadium. In August. After you just watched the singer of the other band get hurt.
[RIFF: the longest you ever waited for a band. Or use this:]
I've waited for bands. I've waited for drummers who were "five minutes out" for an hour. But I've never waited two hours, in a stadium, right after watching the other singer catch fire. That's not a wait. That's a hostage situation with merch.

## NINE SONGS, MAYBE
So Guns comes out. And, look, Axl had been having throat trouble. They'd already called off shows that summer because of it. Slash had told the Montreal Gazette that Axl had a problem with a vocal cord. So this was not made up on the spot.
They also had trouble with the monitors. Slash said the PA and the monitors were feeding back the whole time. Duff and Gilby Clarke, according to Axl, said they couldn't hear.
So they start. And they don't finish.
How long did they play? Depends who you ask. The New York Times said about fifty five minutes. A Seattle paper said way less. UPI said five songs. The Gazette count was nine. I'm going with "not long."
And Axl walks off. And somewhere in there he tells the crowd, more or less, that this will be their last show for a long time. The exact words get quoted different ways, so I'm not going to pretend I know which ones.
[Pause.]
And the crowd had now seen two headliners. Neither one finished.

## THE RIOT
And Montreal goes off.
Fans pour out of the stadium and into the streets around it. Depending on whose estimate you believe, it's anywhere from a couple thousand people to ten thousand.
They set fires. They smash windows. They wreck stuff inside and outside the stadium. A police cruiser gets flipped over. A police spokesman said thirty police cars got damaged. Eight officers had minor injuries. Twelve people got arrested that night.
And the damage, depending on the source, was in the hundreds of thousands of dollars. One account puts it close to four hundred thousand.
[RIFF: rioting at the stadium your own province is still paying for. Or use this:]
Think about that. These are Quebec taxpayers. Trashing a stadium that Quebec taxpayers were still paying for. With cigarette money. They were punching themselves in the wallet.

## AFTERMATH
So, where did everybody land?
Guns N' Roses did not come back to Montreal with the original guys for a long time. They played there in 2010, in a later lineup. But Axl, Slash and Duff didn't share a Montreal stage again until 2017. Twenty five years. And by most accounts, that one went fine. Nobody flipped anything.
And Faith No More, the opening band who had to watch all of this? They got kicked off the tour about six weeks later. Mostly because of friction with the Guns camp.

## THE SERIOUS PART
Back to James. I want to be straight about what happened to him, because it's easy to turn this into a punchline and it wasn't one.
James Hetfield suffered second and third degree burns. Reports at the time described deep second degree burns on the back of his left hand, and second and third degree burns up his arm.
Jason Newsted, the bass player, has said if James had been breathing in at that moment, he would have been dead. Newsted also said, about seeing the arm, "That was his whole fucking arm."
[Pause.]
He was in shock. He was taken to the hospital. And remember, this is a guy who grew up in a house where you didn't see doctors. And that night, doctors were the whole thing.
Back on the night itself: Metallica stopped. Lars, Kirk Hammett and Jason went out and told the crowd they couldn't keep going. Lars promised the band would come back to Montreal to make it up.
Here's the part I respect. Metallica postponed about six shows. Canceled one, in Vancouver. And seventeen days later, August 25th, they were back. Outside Phoenix. James on stage with his arm wrapped from his elbow to his fingers. Singing. Not playing.
And who's playing rhythm guitar?
[Pause.]
John Marshall. The guitar tech. Again. He finished the tour.
And years later, Jason Newsted said that accident is what brought Metallica together. And James made a full recovery. He's still out there playing guitar, which, after what happened to that hand, is a big deal.

## THAT GUY
Okay. Time to name him. That Guy.
The suspects.
Suspect one: the pyro side. The cue, the layout, whoever hit the button. Whatever version you believe, the fire went off with a person standing on it, and that is the one thing pyro exists to never do.
Suspect two: Axl Rose. The guy who came on more than two hours late, played a short set to a crowd that had already been let down once, and walked off. With a man who'd already done this in St. Louis.
Suspect three: the tour itself. Whoever decided that two of the biggest, most chaotic bands in the world, with completely different ideas of what "showtime" means, should share a stage for an entire summer. With the less punctual band going on last.
[Pause.]
And the verdict.
I'm going to split it, and I'll tell you why.
For James? I'm not putting a name on it. Too many versions, and every one of them is a guy doing a hard job a half second off. I'm not hanging that on one person.
For the riot? That's Axl. I believe him about the throat. I believe Slash about the monitors. But you were more than two hours late, to a crowd that just watched a guy get burned, and when you walked off, you knew what happened in St. Louis. He'd seen this movie. He was in it.
But the real That Guy is whoever booked it. Whoever looked at Metallica, the band that shows up, and Guns N' Roses, the band that shows up eventually, and put the late band last. Every night. In stadiums.
That's a whole office full of guys.
[RIFF: your verdict. Do you let Axl off for the throat? Or use this:]
Axl gets a doctor's note for the throat. He doesn't get a doctor's note for St. Louis.
And the "that guy is usually awesome" part? John Marshall. The guitar tech who got handed the rhythm guitar for the biggest band in the world twice, both times because James got hurt, and both times just plugged in and did it. That's That Guy. The good one.

The lesson, if there is one: always know where your mark is. And if you're the second band, show up.
