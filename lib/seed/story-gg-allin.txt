## COLD OPEN
June 27th, 1993. New York City. The East Village.
A tiny punk club on Avenue B called the Gas Station. Because it used to be one.
The singer comes out. He gets about two songs in. He's already fighting with people in the crowd. And somebody pulls the power.
[Pause.]
And then the singer walks out the door, into the street, bleeding, filthy, mostly undressed, with something like eighty to a hundred punks following him down the avenue.
And at some point, in that condition, with that crowd behind him, he steps off the curb and tries to hail a cab.

## A BOY NAMED JESUS
Okay. If you don't know who GG Allin was: he was a punk singer, from the late seventies until 1993, and he's remembered as the most chaotic performer punk ever had. Shows that ended with the police, with ambulances, with the club closed. Not once. As a business model.
But let's start at the beginning, because the beginning is something.
He was born in 1956 in Lancaster, New Hampshire. Way up north. Mountains, woods, not a lot of anything else.
And his name, on his birth certificate, was Jesus Christ Allin.
[Pause.]
Here's how the story goes, and it's the version in most of the biographies: his father, Merle Allin Sr., told his wife that Jesus had visited him and said this baby would be a great man. So. Name the baby Jesus Christ.
[FILL: I need you to picture the guy at the county office in 1956. "Name of child?" "Jesus Christ." And he has to write it down. And he does.]
The family lived in a log cabin. By most accounts, no running water, no electricity. And his father's mental state, by every account, was getting worse. His mother filed for divorce in 1961, and in 1962 she legally changed the boy's name to Kevin Michael Allin. Which, honestly, good call. Hard to get through gym class as Jesus Christ.
And here's where "GG" comes from. His older brother Merle Jr. was a toddler who couldn't say "Jesus." It came out "Jeje." And that turned into GG. So the most notorious name in punk rock is a baby's mispronunciation of the Son of God.
[RIFF: what's in a name. Or use this:]
You can change your kid's name from Jesus Christ to Kevin Michael. You can do all the paperwork. And he's still going to go by the nickname. Every parent learns that eventually. Most of them don't learn it this hard.

## THE JABBERS
And the thing is, early on, GG was kind of a normal punk guy.
In the late seventies he's playing in New Hampshire with a band called the Jabbers. Drums sometimes, singing. His brother Merle on bass. And in 1980 they put out a record on a little New York label called Orange. And those early records are a lot closer to regular punk and rock and roll than to what came later. Just a guy in a band.
[Pause.]
And then, over the eighties, he goes somewhere else. The shows get more violent. More extreme. He's fighting the crowd. He's hurting himself on stage. He's hurting other people. He's doing things on stage with his own bodily functions that I am not going to describe, because you can look it up and I'd rather you didn't do it while eating.
And here's the part I want you to sit with. It worked. In the sense that people came. Word got around. "You have to see this guy." Not because the songs were good. Because something terrible might happen. And usually it did.
[FILL: There's a word for a show people go to hoping for a disaster. It's not "concert." It's "car crash with a cover charge."]

## THE BOOKINGS
So now let me get to the question in the title. Because this is a guy whose shows famously got shut down. Over and over.
And somebody kept booking him.
[Pause.]
I'm going to give you one example, because it's perfect. February 18th, 1992. Austin, Texas. A club called the Cavity Club. Small, grimy, beloved.
GG plays. People run for the exits. And the show ends, according to one Austin radio history, with about a dozen cop cars outside.
And the Austin Chronicle, the city's alternative paper, ran a review of the show. And the review was the arrest report.
[Pause.]
They didn't write a review. They printed the police report as the review. Which, honestly, is the most accurate review anyone's ever written of anything.
[RIFF: what would the arrest report say about your worst gig? Or use this:]
I've played some bad shows. But none of them were reviewed by a cop. If a cop reviewed my worst gig, it'd say "subject played 'Brown Eyed Girl' twice. No charges filed."
And here's my favorite booking detail. When GG got out of prison in Michigan in 1991, he was on parole. And he skipped it. To go on tour. In his own words in an interview later, he wasn't supposed to leave Michigan after he got out.
[Pause.]
So think about that tour from the other side. You're a club owner in some other state. You're booking a guy who, legally, is not supposed to be in your state. Who is, technically, on the run. And you go, "What's he draw?"
[FILL: Most bands, the rider says "two cases of beer and a vegetable tray." His should've said "please do not mention my parole officer."]
And here's the thing. A club owner knew who GG Allin was before he booked him. Everybody knew. That was the pitch. Nobody booked GG Allin expecting a nice quiet evening and a tight forty minutes.

## THE TALK SHOWS
And it wasn't just tiny punk clubs. Because the early nineties had a whole other industry that ran on exactly this.
Daytime talk shows.
GG went on Geraldo. He went on "The Jerry Springer Show." He went on "The Jane Whitney Show."
[Pause.]
And I want you to think about the producers. Somebody at those shows had a phone, and a budget, and a booking sheet. And they went: "Who can we get who'll make the audience scream?" And they called GG Allin.
That's a booker. That's a professional. With a desk. Reading his file. Going "perfect."
[FILL: It's the same thing the club owners were doing. The talk shows just had better lighting.]
And the man said exactly what you'd think he'd say on those shows. That was the point. He'd been saying for years that he was going to kill himself on stage. And the talk shows let him say it to a national audience at two in the afternoon, between commercials for detergent.
[RIFF: early nineties daytime TV. Or use this:]
If you were home sick from school in the early nineties, daytime TV was a parade of people screaming at each other with their names in yellow letters at the bottom of the screen. Somewhere in that parade, between the paternity tests, was GG Allin. That was normal TV.

## THE PROMISE
So about that promise. Because this is where the absurdity really kicks in.
For years, GG said he was going to kill himself on stage. And he picked a date. In 1989 he wrote to a punk zine, Maximum RocknRoll, and said he'd do it on Halloween.
And Halloween came. And he didn't.
Why? Because he was in jail.
[Pause.]
And the story goes that it kept working out that way: whenever the big date came around, he was locked up somewhere. Which is the one thing in this story I'm grateful to the legal system for.

## THE LAST SHOW
Now, the last show itself. Because that cab ride I opened with is real, and it's the most perfect picture of the whole thing.
The Gas Station, Avenue B. June 27th, 1993. A tiny club. And GG gets about two songs in, according to the reports at the time, and he's already fighting with people in the audience. And the sound gets cut. Show's over.
And instead of it ending, it moves.
He walks out into the street. And a writer who was there told the New York Daily News that GG led something like eighty to a hundred punks down the street. And then he tried to hail a cab.
[Pause.]
Covered in blood. And other stuff. Leading a mob. Trying to flag down a yellow cab. In Manhattan.
[RIFF: the cab driver. Or use this:]
I need you to picture the cab driver. It's a Sunday night. He's been driving for ten hours. He sees a guy with his arm up on Avenue B. And then his headlights hit him. And behind him, a hundred punks. Did a cab stop? Nobody wrote that part down. I can't prove it either way. But I'd bet my guitar it did not.

## HATED
One more side character, because he's the one that blew my mind.
In the early nineties, a film student at NYU decided to make a documentary about GG Allin for his senior project. He followed GG and his band, the Murder Junkies, around. He shot the shows. He cut in the talk show footage.
It's called "Hated: GG Allin and the Murder Junkies." He finished it just after GG died, in 1993.
And the film student's name was Todd Phillips.
[Pause.]
Todd Phillips went on to direct "Old School." And "The Hangover." And "Joker."
So the guy who made the biggest bachelor party comedy of all time got his start following GG Allin around with a camera.
[FILL: And honestly? If you've seen "The Hangover," a bunch of guys wake up and have no idea what they did last night. Todd Phillips had seen that movie before. He'd seen it on Avenue B.]
And just days before GG died, there was a screening of "Hated." GG showed up, very drunk, and according to the accounts of that night he threw beer bottles at the screen, and one of them hurt a woman in the audience. The screening got stopped. He took off before police got there.
That's the guy. Even at a movie about himself, somebody in the seats got hurt.

## THE SERIOUS PART
Okay. I need to stop and say a couple things plainly.
In 1989, in Ann Arbor, Michigan, a woman he knew was beaten and burned. GG was charged with assault with intent to do great bodily harm. That charge was dropped when he pleaded no contest to felonious assault. And he went to prison in Michigan for more than a year.
That's not a bit. There was a real person on the other end of that, and she doesn't get a punchline in this episode or any other.
And at the shows, it wasn't just him getting hurt. People in his audiences got hurt. Hit, attacked, injured. The people who came to watch were not always in on the joke, because there wasn't a joke.
And then there's how it ended.
On June 28th, 1993, hours after that last show on Avenue B, GG Allin died of an accidental heroin overdose in New York City. He was thirty six years old.
[Pause.]
After the show, he ended up at an apartment with a group of people, partying and using drugs into the night. And around two in the morning, people at that party posed for pictures with him. They didn't realize he was dying.
He wasn't passed out. He was dying, and nobody in that room knew.
His funeral was a few days later, July 3rd, back in New Hampshire.
For a guy who spent years promising a spectacle of a death, it wasn't one. It was a drug overdose in an apartment, and a room full of people who didn't notice. That's what it was. I'm not going to dress it up.

## THAT GUY
Okay. Time to name him. That Guy.
The suspects.
Suspect one: GG Allin. Obviously. He did it. He did all of it. Nobody made him do any of it. I'm not letting him off because he was "a character."
Suspect two: the bookers. The club owners who put him on a bill knowing exactly how it would end. Every one of them made a little money off the chaos and let the cops, the neighborhood and the hospital clean up after.
Suspect three: the talk shows. Geraldo, Springer, Jane Whitney. Professional TV producers who put him in front of the country because he'd get a reaction. He got a bigger stage from them than any punk club ever gave him.
And honorary mention, the audience. Because every booking only happened because people bought tickets.
[Pause.]
And the verdict.
This is a whole office full of guys. Maybe a whole building.
GG's responsible for what GG did. Full stop. But the title asks who kept booking him, and the answer is: everybody who figured out you could make money off of a guy falling apart in public. The club owner who wanted a packed room. The talk show that wanted ratings. The labels that kept putting out the records. A whole little economy built on one man getting worse.
[RIFF: is the guy who books the disaster as guilty as the disaster? Or use this:]
If you sell tickets to a train wreck, you're not a promoter anymore. You're part of the train.
And look, this episode doesn't have a "that guy is usually awesome" guy. Not really. The closest I've got is a cab driver who may not even exist. The one I'm choosing to believe in, who saw that coming down Avenue B and kept driving. In my head, that guy made the only good decision in this whole story.
