## A BOY NAMED JESUS
Okay. If you don't know who GG Allin was: he was a punk singer, from the late seventies until 1993, and he's remembered as the most chaotic performer punk ever had. Shows that ended with the police, with ambulances, with the club closed. Not once. As a business model.
But let's start at the beginning, because the beginning is something.
He was born in 1956 in Lancaster, New Hampshire. Way up north. Mountains, woods, not a lot of anything else.
[CLIP: Born Jesus Christ Allin]
And his name, on his birth certificate, was Jesus Christ Allin.
[Pause.]
Here's how the story goes, and it's the version in most of the biographies: his father, Merle Allin Sr., told his wife that Jesus had visited him and said this baby would be a great man. So. Name the baby Jesus Christ.
[FILL: Think about the guy at the county office in 1956. "Name of child?" "Jesus Christ." And he has to write it down. And he does.]
The family lived in a log cabin. By most accounts, no running water, no electricity. And his father's mental state, by every account, was getting worse. His mother filed for divorce in 1961, and in 1962 she legally changed the boy's name to Kevin Michael Allin. Which, honestly, good call. Hard to get through gym class as Jesus Christ.
And here's where "GG" comes from. His older brother Merle Jr. was a toddler who couldn't say "Jesus." It came out "Jeje." And that turned into GG. So the most notorious name in punk rock is a baby's mispronunciation of the Son of God.
[RIFF: what's in a name. Or use this:]
You can change your kid's name from Jesus Christ to Kevin Michael. You can do all the paperwork. And he's still going to go by the nickname. Every parent learns that eventually. Most of them don't learn it this hard.

## THE JABBERS
And the thing is, early on, GG was kind of a normal punk guy.
In the late seventies he's playing in New Hampshire with a band called the Jabbers. Drums sometimes, singing. His brother Merle on bass. And in 1980 they put out a record on a little New York label called Orange. And those early records are a lot closer to regular punk and rock and roll than to what came later. Just a guy in a band.
And then, over the eighties, he goes somewhere else. The shows get more violent. More extreme. He's fighting the crowd. He's hurting himself on stage. He's hurting other people. He's doing things on stage with his own bodily functions that I am not going to describe, because you can look it up and I'd rather you didn't do it while eating.
And here's the part I want you to sit with. It worked. In the sense that people came. Word got around. "You have to see this guy." Not because the songs were good. Because something terrible might happen. And usually it did.
And here's the business side, because this episode is about the business side. Back in the eighties, there were ads in Maximum RocknRoll, the big punk zine, bragging about GG's guarantee. A hundred bucks. That's what it cost to book GG Allin. And the damage he did to clubs regularly ran several times that.
[FILL: There's a word for a show people go to hoping for a disaster. It's not "concert." It's "car crash with a cover charge."]

## THE BOOKINGS
So now let me get to the question in the title. Because this is a guy whose shows famously got shut down. Over and over.
And somebody kept booking him.
And I wanted a name. Not "the clubs." A guy. So here's one.
[CLIP: The club that GG-proofed itself]
February 18th, 1992. Austin, Texas. A club called the Cavity Club, on Red River. Small, grimy, beloved. A guy named Staryn Wagner co-founded it with a couple of partners, turning an old building into a punk club in 1991.
And Staryn Wagner was a fan. He'd met GG a couple of years earlier, because a roommate of his fixed GG's tour van. That's how you meet GG Allin. He breaks down in your town and your roommate's the mechanic. And Wagner told the Austin Chronicle he still sings some of GG's old songs to himself. To this day.
And here's my favorite detail in this whole episode. Wagner says they spent weeks getting the club ready for that show. Weeks. Going through the room and making sure there was nothing in there that anybody, on stage or in the crowd, could throw that would shatter and hurt somebody.
[Pause.]
That's not a club that got surprised. That's a club that GG-proofed itself. They did the math. They knew exactly what was coming, and they baby-proofed the bar for it.
And the money? Wagner says the most they ever charged at the door was five bucks, and they never paid guarantees. So this was not a get-rich scheme. This was a fan with a club going, "I want to see it."
[RIFF: GG-proofing a club. What do you take down first? Or use this:]
I've played clubs where they wouldn't move a bar stool for you. These guys spent weeks GG-proofing the place. Pull the glass, pull the bottles, pull anything with an edge. Imagine the to-do list on the fridge. "Milk. Eggs. Remove everything throwable before Tuesday."
So how'd it go? GG plays. People run for the exits. And the show ends, according to one Austin radio history, with about a dozen cop cars outside.
And the Austin Chronicle, the city's alternative paper, ran a review of the show. And the review was the arrest report.
They didn't write a review. They printed the police report as the review. Which, honestly, is the most accurate review anyone's ever written of anything.
And the report is full of little moments. Somebody in the crowd had sprayed Mace. And GG, getting arrested, asks the officer why the police Maced him. And the officer writes down that he told GG it wasn't the police. It was the crowd. Austin police, he explained, don't even carry Mace.
So GG Allin got Maced by his own audience. At his own show. And the cop had to break it to him.
And then GG tells the officer, and this is in the report, that what he does on stage is part of his act, and that the club manager knew that when he booked the band.
That's GG Allin, in handcuffs, making the case for this episode. "Don't look at me, officer. They booked me."
[RIFF: what would the arrest report say about your worst gig? Or use this:]
I've played some bad shows. But none of them were reviewed by a cop. If a cop reviewed my worst gig, it'd say "subject played 'Brown Eyed Girl' twice. No charges filed."

## ON THE RUN
And here's the booking detail that ties Austin together. When GG got out of prison in Michigan in 1991, he was on parole. And he skipped it. To go on tour. In his own words in an interview later, he wasn't supposed to leave Michigan after he got out.
So think about that tour from the other side. You're a club owner in some other state. You're booking a guy who, legally, is not supposed to be in your state. Who is, technically, on the run. And you go, "What's he draw?"
[FILL: Most bands, the rider says "two cases of beer and a vegetable tray." His should've said "please do not mention my parole officer."]
And that's how the Austin night ends. When the police run his name, there's a warrant out of Flint, Michigan, to revoke his probation. So the Cavity Club bust was the end of the tour. He went back to Michigan.
And the Cavity Club? It closed about a year later, February 1993. The last band on that stage was called Pork. Which is a fine name for a band. Just not a GG-level ending.
And here's the thing. A club owner knew who GG Allin was before he booked him. Everybody knew. That was the pitch. Nobody booked GG Allin expecting a nice quiet evening and a tight forty minutes.

## THE TALK SHOWS
And it wasn't just tiny punk clubs. Because the early nineties had a whole other industry that ran on exactly this.
Daytime talk shows.
GG went on Geraldo, in 1992. One listing of that episode files it under "obscene art." He went on "The Jerry Springer Show," in May 1993. And the way that Springer episode was set up is perfect: it was a dad trying to keep his teenage daughter away from GG Allin's music. So they solved that by putting GG Allin on national television. In front of the daughter. And everybody else's daughter.
[Pause.]
And then he went on "The Jane Whitney Show," in 1993. That's the last big TV interview he ever did. And on that show, he said out loud, to the studio audience, that when he killed himself on stage he'd take his fans with him.
Now. I went looking for the producers. The person at each of these shows who picked up the phone and booked him. Their names are not in anything I could find. So I'll give you the names that are on the shows. Geraldo Rivera. Jerry Springer. Jane Whitney. It was their names in the title. Somebody on their staff had a phone, and a budget, and a booking sheet. And they went: "Who can we get who'll make the audience scream?" And they called GG Allin.
That's a booker. That's a professional. With a desk. Reading his file. Going "perfect."
[FILL: It's the same thing the club owners were doing. The talk shows just had better lighting.]
And the man said exactly what you'd think he'd say on those shows. That was the point. He'd been saying for years that he was going to kill himself on stage. And the talk shows let him say it to a national audience at two in the afternoon, between commercials for detergent.
[RIFF: early nineties daytime TV. Or use this:]
If you were home sick from school in the early nineties, daytime TV was a parade of people screaming at each other with their names in yellow letters at the bottom of the screen. Somewhere in that parade, between the paternity tests, was GG Allin. That was normal TV.

## THE PROMISE
So about that promise. Because this is where the absurdity really kicks in.
For years, GG said he was going to kill himself on stage. And he picked a date. In 1989 he wrote to Maximum RocknRoll, the same zine with the guarantee ads, and said he'd do it on Halloween.
And Halloween came. And he didn't.
Why? Because he was in jail.
And the story goes that it kept working out that way: whenever the big date came around, he was locked up somewhere. Which is the one thing in this story I'm grateful to the legal system for.

## THE LAST SHOW
Now, the last show itself. Because that cab ride I opened with is real, and it's the most perfect picture of the whole thing.
First, the room. The Gas Station, Avenue B and East Second Street. And it really was an old gas station. In the eighties it got turned into an art space. The guy who ran it for most of its life was a sculptor named Linus Coraggio, and the place was a junk-metal sculpture yard with a wall of scrap metal around it, studios, welding gear, and a performance space. Years later, when the place was getting evicted, Coraggio told the New York Times it was one of the last venues for unusual art work, alternative parties and experimentation.
So, yes. GG Allin played his last show in a scrap-metal sculpture garden. With welding equipment on site. I'm not saying that was a good idea. I'm saying it was very on brand for everybody involved.
June 27th, 1993. A Sunday. GG gets about two songs in, according to the reports at the time, and he's already fighting with people in the audience. And the sound gets cut. Show's over.
And instead of it ending, it moves.
He walks out into the street. And a writer who was there, Tom DiNardo, told the New York Daily News that GG led something like eighty to a hundred punks down the street. And then he tried to hail a cab.
[Pause.]
Covered in blood. And other stuff. Leading a mob. Trying to flag down a yellow cab. In Manhattan.
[RIFF: the cab driver. Or use this:]
Put yourself in that cab. It's a Sunday night. You've been driving for ten hours. You see a guy with his arm up on Avenue B. And then your headlights hit him. And behind him, a hundred punks. Did a cab stop? Nobody wrote that part down. I can't prove it either way. But I'd bet my guitar it did not.

## HATED
One more side character, because he's the one that blew my mind.
[CLIP: The Hangover director's student film]
In the early nineties, a film student at NYU decided to make a documentary about GG Allin for his senior project. He followed GG and his band, the Murder Junkies, around. He shot the shows. He cut in the talk show footage.
It's called "Hated: GG Allin and the Murder Junkies." He finished it just after GG died, in 1993.
And the film student's name was Todd Phillips.
Todd Phillips went on to direct "Old School." And "The Hangover." And "Joker."
So the guy who made the biggest bachelor party comedy of all time got his start following GG Allin around with a camera.
[FILL: And honestly? If you've seen "The Hangover," a bunch of guys wake up and have no idea what they did last night. Todd Phillips had seen that movie before. He'd seen it on Avenue B.]
And just days before GG died, there was a screening of "Hated." GG showed up, very drunk, and according to the accounts of that night he threw beer bottles at the screen, and one of them hurt a woman in the audience. The screening got stopped. He took off before police got there.
That's the guy. Even at a movie about himself, somebody in the seats got hurt.

## THE SERIOUS PART
Okay. I need to stop and say a couple things plainly.
In 1989, in Ann Arbor, Michigan, a woman he knew was beaten and burned. GG was charged with assault with intent to do great bodily harm. That charge was dropped when he pleaded no contest to felonious assault. And he went to prison in Michigan for more than a year.
That's not a bit. There was a real person on the other end of that, and she doesn't get a punchline in this episode or any other.
And at the shows, it wasn't just him getting hurt. People in his audiences got hurt. Hit, attacked, injured. The people who came to watch were not always in on the joke, because there wasn't a joke.
And then there's how it ended.
On June 28th, 1993, hours after that last show on Avenue B, GG Allin died of an accidental heroin overdose in New York City. He was thirty six years old.
[Pause.]
After the show, he ended up at an apartment with a group of people, partying and using drugs into the night. And around two in the morning, people at that party posed for pictures with him. They didn't realize he was dying.
He wasn't passed out. He was dying, and nobody in that room knew.
His funeral was a few days later, July 3rd, back in New Hampshire. By the reports, he was buried with a bottle of Jim Beam and a microphone. And one account says his brother Merle, the same brother who couldn't say "Jesus" as a toddler, put a pair of headphones on him at the end, plugged into a tape of his own songs.
For a guy who spent years promising a spectacle of a death, it wasn't one. It was a drug overdose in an apartment, and a room full of people who didn't notice. That's what it was. I'm not going to dress it up.

## THAT GUY
[CLIP: That Guy verdict]
Okay. Time to name him. That Guy.
The suspects.
Suspect one: the bookers. And now they've got faces. Staryn Wagner at the Cavity Club, who liked GG enough to spend weeks GG-proofing his own room, and then booked him anyway. Every club like it, all the way to the sculpture yard on Avenue B. Every one of them made a little money, or just got a great story, off the chaos, and let the cops, the neighborhood and the hospital clean up after.
And to be fair to Wagner, of everybody in this story, he's the one who actually did homework. He took the glass out. That's more planning than most of the people on this list.
Suspect two: the talk shows. Geraldo, Springer, Jane Whitney. Professional TV operations that put him in front of the country because he'd get a reaction. Springer's show booked him for an episode about a dad trying to protect his daughter from GG Allin. And then gave GG the hour. He got a bigger stage from them than any punk club ever gave him.
Suspect three: the audience. Because every booking only happened because people bought tickets. Including the ones who brought Mace.
And suspect four. The guy in the middle of all of it.
[Pause.]
And the verdict.
The title asks who kept booking him. And the honest answer is a long list, and I just read you some of it. But who was every one of those bookers saying yes to? Who skipped parole to go play their rooms? Who got arrested in Austin and told the cop, more or less, "the club knew"? Who sat on daytime TV and promised to take his own fans with him?
Every one of those clubs and every one of those talk shows was somebody saying yes. Nobody made him do any of it. He's responsible for what he did, full stop, and I'm not letting him off because he was "a character."
That Guy is GG Allin.
