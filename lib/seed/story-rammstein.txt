## SIX GUYS FROM EAST BERLIN
Everybody knows Rammstein. Even if you think you don't, you do. Six huge German guys, "Du Hast," and so much fire that the people in the back row get a tan.
What almost nobody knows is that two of them spent a night in a Massachusetts jail over a song called "Bück dich." Which means "bend over." We'll get there.
All six grew up in East Germany, behind the Berlin Wall. They only became Rammstein in 1994, after the Wall came down.
The singer is Till Lindemann. Born 1963 in Leipzig. Before he was a rock singer, he was a serious swimmer, shortlisted for the 1980 Olympics in Moscow until an injury ended it. So the scariest frontman in metal was once a kid in a Speedo doing the fifteen hundred meter freestyle.
And the keyboard player is Christian Lorenz. Everybody calls him Flake. Born 1966 in East Berlin. Rolling Stone Germany ran a little profile of him, and it reads like a list of reasons he should never have joined this band. He stuttered. Kids at school stuffed him in dumpsters. He wanted to be a surgeon. He ended up a failed car salesman, a big fan of long walks, and a hypochondriac.
[CLIP: Flake's two jail stays]
And that same profile says Flake has been to jail twice in his life. The first time was in East Germany, for "attempted fleeing of the republic." Because he went hiking with a friend too close to the border.
He went for a walk. In the wrong direction. That's jail number one. Jail number two is in Worcester, Massachusetts, and it's dumber.
When Flake first joined, the story goes, he just sat around at rehearsals not knowing what to play, until one day he made a noise on his keyboard that sounded, in Rolling Stone Germany's words, translated, like a "dying dinosaur." And that became the Rammstein sound.
[FILL: Every great band has a guy whose whole job is one weird noise. Flake found his noise and got a career out of it.]

## WHY TILL IS ON FIRE
So why the fire? This is the part I didn't know, and I love it.
Till Lindemann was shy. Here's Till, to Rolling Stone, in 2014:
"I wasn't really happy on stage. I wore sunglasses, as I couldn't stand the stares. I had stage fright and thought: 'What am I doing here at all?' I used to be a drummer, and then I always had something to do. Now, I was standing there, up front, and everyone was staring at me. It was uncomfortable for me, so I wanted to compensate for that somehow. That's where the fire came into play."
[Pause.]
Most people with stage fright picture the audience in their underwear. Till pictured himself in flames.
And it started small. Flake told the Deseret News, the Salt Lake City paper, in June 1999, how it began: "The pyrotechnics actually emerged during our second concert back in 1993. We had a small flash device that the kids really liked. And when we played the next show, we didn't have it. Everyone wondered where it was."
One flash pot. Six years later they're flying to America with rockets, flaming coats and, according to that same article, "spark-spewing shoes and exploding telephones."
I don't know what an exploding telephone does in a concert. I just know I want one.
Early on, they did a lot of it themselves, and it shows. Zak Tell, the singer of Clawfinger, a Swedish band Rammstein opened for in the mid nineties, told Metal Hammer: "They had pyros, flamethrowers and in Austria, they actually set the lighting rig on fire." And then, very calmly: "Thankfully, nothing was destroyed."
Till burned his ears, his hair and his arms in those early years.
Then it stopped being funny for a minute. September 27th, 1996, a big hometown show at Arena Berlin. Metal Hammer reported that a beam loaded with fireworks fell and injured five people. That's the night they stopped doing it themselves. They brought in a production manager named Nicolai Sabottka, who went to Dresden's school of explosives. His pyro company now has its own factory in Montana. And Till went and got certified as a pyrotechnician himself.
Flake's version, to the Deseret News: "Till had to go to school to get a certificate to handle the fireworks everyday. Otherwise we could only do the show on New Year's Eve."
And Till says that license comes with rules. He told Rolling Stone in 2015 he can't even drink before a show: "I'm even not allowed to drink because if something happens, I go to jail forever."
That's the one thing this man is careful about. Going to jail over the show.
And just showing the fire marshal costs a fortune. Till again: "Everything is twice as expensive because you have to show them the whole fucking thing, the whole show in advance. You blow probably €7,000 to €10,000 a night just for the fire marshal."
Seven to ten thousand euros a night. In today's money, that's about eleven to fifteen thousand bucks. Every night. Before a single note.

## FLAKE'S JOB DESCRIPTION
Now, if Till is the guy on fire, Flake is the guy things happen to. Here's a quick tour of what that band has done to its own keyboard player.
For years, during the song "Seemann," Flake crowd surfed in an inflatable rubber dinghy. Rolling Stone Germany notes he rarely got carried back to the stage. The way Flake tells it, he handed the boat off to the bass player in 2002 because he got hurt too often.
Then there's the pot. During the song "Mein Teil," Till comes out in a bloody apron and a chef's hat, pushing a giant metal cooking pot. Flake is in it. Playing keyboard. And Till cooks him with a flamethrower.
And here's the detail I love. The guy who designs their stage, LeRoy Bennett, told Metal Hammer they could have moved that pot with machinery. "It would have been easier to put that thing on some kind of a lift and get it to the stage, but no, it was Till pushing it up the ramp himself. That thing is heavy, but Till's a big guy."
So it's not enough to cook your keyboard player. You have to push him up the ramp yourself, like a man moving a fridge.
There's also a bathtub where Till pours sparks on him. And in Gothenburg, Sweden, in 2005, Flake finally got revenge, by accident. He ran into Till with the Segway he rides during "Amerika," hurt his knee, and the band had to cancel shows in Asia.
Twenty years of fire, and the thing that took Till out was a Segway.
[CLIP: Till sets himself on fire and the crowd thinks he died]
And Till on fire. LeRoy Bennett says in 2001 Till was wearing a flaming chain mail coat, accidentally set his pant leg on fire, and liked it so much that he decided to set his whole body on fire at future shows. Fireproof suit, a mask that looks like his face, and Flake comes out with a fire extinguisher full of flammable powder.
Here's LeRoy: "And so Till turns into a big ball of fire and eventually he falls down and the crew come out, put him out, and drag him offstage. And there was dead silence. I went back to see Till and he goes, 'I guess I better get up at the end of that, because I think they thought I died!'"
[FILL: The fire extinguisher is full of fire. That's the whole Rammstein philosophy in one prop. The safety equipment is also on fire.]
[TALK: Which of Flake's jobs would you take: the dinghy, the pot or the bathtub?]

## RAMMSTEIN MEETS AMERICA
So how does a band singing entirely in German end up in front of American teenagers?
In 1997 David Lynch put them in his movie "Lost Highway." Then MTV started playing "Du Hast." Their American label guy, Wayne Pighini, saw them in Las Vegas that December and told Metal Hammer: "Till took out the flamethrower and started blowing flame 20 feet over the crowd's heads, and the crowd was going crazy. I'd never seen anything like this."
Then in 1998 they get booked on Korn's Family Values Tour. Korn, Ice Cube, Orgy, and, yes, Limp Bizkit. Fred Durst. That Fred Durst. So Rammstein's big American break was opening the door for a whole lot of backwards red hats.
Korn's guitar player, Munky, said in the documentary "Rammstein in Amerika": "I don't think the audience knew what hit them over the head."
And Jay Gordon, the singer of Orgy, the opening band, remembered: "I would walk in their dressing room, and a Heineken bottle would go at 120mph past my head."
Their album "Sehnsucht" eventually went platinum in America. In German. Nobody does that.
And here's a detail from that Deseret News piece that turns out to be very important. When they played Salt Lake City on the Family Values Tour, they toned it down. The paper says instead of the simulated sex act, "Lindemann ate an apple."
So the band knew some American towns would not love "Bück dich." And at least once, they had a backup plan. A piece of fruit.
[Pause.]
On that same tour, they played the Worcester Centrum in Massachusetts. Metal Hammer says that was nine months before the night we're talking about. Nobody got arrested. So Rammstein had been to Worcester. Worcester had seen Rammstein. Everybody seemed fine.
[TALK: Would you have bought a Rammstein ticket in 1998 without understanding a word of it?]

## THE NIGHT IN WORCESTER
Saturday, June 5th, 1999. Rammstein is headlining their own tour now, and they play the Worcester Palladium. A theater that holds a little under three thousand people. The night before, they played New York. The night after, Montreal. Skunk Anansie and Soulfly open.
And the tour is already going badly. There's a German write-up of it from that summer, with Flake's own account of the arrest in it, and it starts with the injuries. Flake fell out of the boat and cut his head open. Then Till jumped off the stage during "Weisses Fleisch" and twisted his knee, and now he's limping around on crutches. And then, translated: "But the show goes on like every night."
So near the end of the set, they do "Bück dich." And I'll describe this the way a gentleman would.
Flake comes out on all fours, on a leash. Till walks him around the stage like a dog. Then Till pulls out a very large, very fake prop, and it sprays liquid. On Flake. On the crowd. On Till's own face. Both of them keep their clothes on the entire time. And it all happens on a well lit riser at the front of the stage.
Oh, and the liquid. Metal Hammer says Till started out using milk, then switched to Pernod and water. Pernod is a French licorice liqueur. He switched because, and this is how the magazine put it, it "eliminated the malodorous challenges of dousing oneself in dairy each night."
So somewhere in this band's history, there was a meeting. And the agenda item was: "The milk smells."
[Pause.]
The show ends. And then, here's Flake. Translated from German:
"After the concert in Worcester, Nicolai, our tour manager, told me that Till and I were going to be arrested. At first I thought it was a joke. But then I saw about eight grim-looking police officers come backstage, and we really had to beg to be allowed a quick shower, under supervision."
Under supervision. Two German rock stars, covered in licorice liqueur, negotiating shower rights with the Worcester police.
"Then they put handcuffs on us, stuck us in a kind of dogcatcher's van and drove us to jail."
The guy who spent the last hour on a leash goes to jail in a dogcatcher's van. You couldn't write that. Well, Flake did.

## A NIGHT IN THE CELL
[CLIP: The blanket rule]
At the station they take everything, Flake says, even his glasses and his shoes. Photos. Fingerprints. A holding cell that was, his words, completely empty apart from a drain. Then a single cell with a bare wooden bunk.
And then, the best line in his whole account: "That was not what I had pictured as an after-show party."
His neighbors that night? Flake: "The other prisoners, some of whom had come straight from a shooting, were drunk and on drugs, and they made a hell of a racket, yelling, banging on the walls and groaning, so sleep was out of the question."
And then the part that makes me laugh every time. Flake: "Later we found out it was lucky we didn't get a blanket, because the people with blankets had to wait until Monday for the judge."
The blanket was the tell. If you got a blanket, you were staying the weekend. Somewhere in Worcester there's a jail where a blanket is bad news.
Meanwhile, the police had a problem. Their two prisoners didn't speak much English. So, according to Metal Hammer, the Worcester police called Holy Cross, the local college, to see if any of their professors could help. And a German professor volunteered to sit with them until the lawyers showed up.
Think about her night. Saturday. Phone rings. "Hi, it's the Worcester police. Do you speak German? Can you come down? It's about a leash."
[TALK: You're the Holy Cross professor. Do you take that call?]
And the police statement. Worcester Police Sergeant Thomas Radula said Till was simulating sex with Flake on stage using, as MTV News quoted it, a "fake phallic object that shot water at the crowd."
Water. Sergeant, it was Pernod. Get it right.
The charge: lewd and lascivious behavior. Two days later, MTV News ran the headline "Rammstein's Act Lands Two Members In Jail." And the band put out a statement. The Deseret News printed it word for word:
"While they strongly deny any criminal wrongdoing, they have retained local counsel and intend to respect the laws of the city in question by seeing this matter through to its logical conclusion."
"The city in question." They won't even say Worcester. Worcester is the city in question now.

## "PER PIECE"
Sunday morning, they get out. MTV reported they were released on twenty five dollars bail. In today's money, that's about fifty. Flake says they had to sign a promise to come back for court on their own, and then they drove to Canada, very late, for the Montreal show.
And then they had to come back. Flake: "So three days later we flew back to Worcester for the bail hearing. The prosecutor demanded 10,000 dollars per 'piece,' as he put it."
Flake wrote all of this in German, but that one word he left in English, in quote marks. "Piece." It stuck with him.
Ten thousand dollars. In today's money, that's about twenty thousand. Per piece. And if they didn't pay, Flake says, they'd sit in jail until the trial. Their lawyer got the bail dropped, and they got to leave Worcester.
[CLIP: Till gets arrested in "Maine"]
So how did it end? Even that's a mess. Billboard reported that summer, according to the fan archive RammWiki, that it came down to a hundred dollar fine and six months' probation. A hundred bucks. In today's money, that's about two hundred. Other retellings say twenty five dollars, a hundred and twenty five, or two hundred each. Nobody agrees on the fine. Everybody agrees on the leash.
And Till did not take it well. In "Rammstein in Amerika," he said: "If I get out a plastic dildo and squirt some milk around, I don't expect to be locked up because of it."
But the best part is in 2015. Rolling Stone brings up the act, and Till says: "Yeah, yeah. I got arrested for that in Maine."
[Pause.]
Maine. Wrong state, Till. The man went to jail in Massachusetts, flew back for a bail hearing, got probation, and he still thinks it was Maine.
And it never went away. Same interview: "I still have to do interviews and explain myself for why I got arrested and why I have probation. It never goes away. So for the rest of my life, as soon as I enter American ground, I have to go to the interview. I tried to get this thing out of my file with some lawyers but it's impossible."
So every time the scariest man in metal lands in America, he sits in a little room at the airport and explains the leash.
[TALK: How do you explain "Bück dich" to a customs officer?]

## WHERE ARE THEY NOW
Did they stop doing it? Of course not. "Bück dich" stayed in the set.
After 2001 they basically stopped touring America for about ten years. When they came back, they sold out Madison Square Garden in December 2010.
And on April 29th, 2012, they went back to Worcester. Not a three thousand seat theater this time. The DCU Center. Loudwire said they rolled in with more than thirty semi trucks. They still played "Bück dich." The Boston Globe review opened, "Some bands play rock 'n' roll; Rammstein plays phallus 'n' flames."
And before the final bow, Till spoke to the crowd in English: "Worcester, thank you very much. I hope we don't get arrested like last time."
They left as free men.
Today they play stadiums all over the world. Flake writes books. And the pyro guy, Nicolai Sabottka, told Metal Hammer in 2015, about setting Till on fire: "We actually haven't set fire to him for the past couple tours, but we've been developing new ways of attaching flames to him."
New ways of attaching flames to him. That's a job.

## THAT GUY
[CLIP: That Guy verdict: the apple]
Okay. Who let Rammstein into Massachusetts? Time to name That Guy.
Suspect one: Flake. He agreed to the leash. Every night. Fell out of a boat, got cooked in a pot, rode a dinghy into a crowd, and when somebody said "get on all fours," he said "sure." He's the most cooperative man in heavy metal. But you don't arrest the dog.
Suspect two: the Worcester police. Same band, same city, nine months earlier at the Centrum, and nobody blinked. Then a smaller room, a well lit riser, and suddenly eight officers backstage. Nobody has ever said who made that call. But the police didn't write the act. They just watched it.
Suspect three: whoever switched the milk to Pernod. Not guilty. That person improved the show for everybody.
And the verdict.
It's Till.
[THAT GUY STING]
Here's why. This is a man who is careful about exactly one thing: going to jail over the show. He got a pyro license so he wouldn't. He doesn't drink before shows because if something happens, he goes to jail forever. And he knew some American towns would not like "Bück dich." The proof is in Salt Lake City. He had a backup. He ate an apple.
Worcester did not get the apple.
The man had a fruit-based exit plan in his pocket, and in Massachusetts he picked the leash. So That Guy is Till Lindemann. The guy who had an apple and didn't use it.
