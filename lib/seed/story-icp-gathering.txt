## TWO JOES FROM DETROIT
Every summer, for more than twenty five years, thousands of people have driven across the country to a campground to see a band that a music magazine once voted the worst band of any genre. They spray soda on each other. They watch wrestling at three in the morning. They call each other family. And the band itself built the whole thing, because almost nobody else would host it.
This is how the Gathering of the Juggalos happened. And how, one year, it went really wrong for one guest.
So. Two guys named Joe. Joseph Bruce and Joseph Utsler. They meet as kids in the Detroit area, backyard wrestling in rings they built themselves. Bruce becomes Violent J. Utsler becomes Shaggy 2 Dope. And at first they're a rap group called Inner City Posse.
By late 1991 they've spent more money on recording than they have, and they need something nobody else is doing. And the way Violent J tells it, he has a dream about a clown running around Delray, his old neighborhood in southwest Detroit. Shaggy points out they can keep the initials. I.C.P. Inner City Posse becomes Insane Clown Posse. They paint their faces, and they build a whole mythology around it, a series of albums called the Joker's Cards.
And then the soda. Faygo is the cheap Detroit pop. Violent J told The Stranger, "We always drank Faygo." And then, "Faygo Cola was only 69 cents for a two-liter." In today's money, that's still under two bucks. Somewhere around 1993, the story goes, a row of guys at a show is flipping them off, and one of the clowns throws an open bottle of Faygo at them. The crowd loves it. So now they throw Faygo every show. That's a whole tradition started by somebody being mad at a heckler.
[CLIP: Disney pulls the clowns in a few hours]
And then Disney happens. In 1997, Hollywood Records, which is owned by Disney, puts out their album "The Great Milenko." Release day. It sells eighteen thousand copies. And within hours, Disney pulls it off the shelves. Disney's explanation was that the album "did not fit the Disney image."
They found that out after printing it.
Another label picks it up, and that album ends up going platinum. So the lesson the clowns take from the nineties is simple. The big guys will not let us in. Fine. We'll build our own house.

## THE FIRST GATHERING
[CLIP: The convention center that did baseball cards]
So in 2000, the band's fans, who call themselves Juggalos, get an invitation. Violent J said it plain to Spin, years later: "To be totally honest, my brother put it together." His brother is Rob Bruce, who also raps as Jumpsteady. The plan: everybody come meet up, one weekend, one place.
The place is the Novi Expo Center, outside Detroit. July 21st and 22nd, 2000. Admission was sixty bucks. In today's money, that's about a hundred and fifteen.
And it's not just a concert. There are seminars, autograph sessions, a haunted house, and a wrestling show from the band's own wrestling company. Shaggy remembered, "We had a dunk tank where you could dunk a hillbilly." He also admitted, "The first one, we really didn't have no idea what we were doing."
And people came from everywhere. Sonicnet covered it. Here's their reporter, Adam Graham: "Jesse Satterfield, 25, of Los Angeles, traveled 53 hours with seven of his friends by bus to attend the Gathering." Fifty three hours. On a bus. To Novi.
At the memorabilia auction, one guy, Bill Syckle, "paid $1,750 for a pair of all-access passes." In today's money, that's about thirty four hundred bucks.
And my favorite person at the first Gathering isn't even a fan. Twenty years later, the Detroit News talked to a guy named Alex Christodoulou, who was twelve that weekend. His dad drove him. Here's Alex: "My dad, for the most part, dropped me off and he hung out in the car." His dad didn't understand why a bunch of kids were dumping soda on each other's heads. But Alex says, "So he would show up every hour or two and he would have the whole car filled with Faygo." And, "he would bring cheeseburgers for people and hand out a bunch of food."
And then Alex gave him a title. "So he was the first official Jugga-dad, so to speak."
[Pause.]
That guy's a hero. No idea what's happening, full support, cheeseburgers.
Violent J told the crowd that weekend what he thought the money was for: "The money we're spending on the Gathering Of The Juggalos, we could be spending on some gigantic video." And he didn't want the video. He wanted the party.
But the Expo Center was not ready for the party. Violent J told Spin, "They're a convention center, they're used to collectible baseball cards or something." At the wrestling show, two hundred fans climb into the ring at once, and the whole thing collapses. At the closing concert, Violent J invites the crowd up, and about three hundred people get on the stage. The show is shut down at 10:40 at night. Two small fires in the parking lot. And here's the amazing part: no arrests.
And Sonicnet had one more line. The next year's Gathering was tentatively planned for Chicago.
It didn't end up in Chicago. Hold that thought.

## BANNED
Here's the chain of decisions that made the Gathering what it is. Every single step is somebody saying no.
2001. Toledo, Ohio. The SeaGate Convention Centre, plus a big hotel full of Juggalos. Shaggy said the hotel and the convention center both got trashed, and, his words, "somebody punched a police horse in the face or something like that." Fans rushed the stage, the band had to get off before it collapsed, and the festival ended early.
And Violent J: "We got kicked out of Toledo. Banned from Toledo. We didn't even play Toledo for ten years after that."
And then they learn the convention center business is a small world. Shaggy: "Basically, the convention centers all know each other." So for 2002, they had to talk their way into the Peoria Civic Center, here in Illinois. Shaggy's own words: "We had to fucking bullshit our way into Peoria, saying that we'd banned all stickers and shit."
They promised Peoria no stickers. That was the pitch.
Peoria ended with police firing tear gas and pepper balls into the crowd. The rapper Bubba Sparxxx got booed off stage so hard that the Gathering named an award after him, for the next act who got it the same way.
So in 2003 they give up on buildings. They rent Nelson Ledges Quarry Park in Garrettsville, Ohio. Outdoors. Twenty four hours a day. And Violent J talks about that year like he found religion: "It was like the key fit in the lock, finally."
And they learn something else in the woods. Juggalos chant "whoop whoop." And Violent J explained the problem: if you don't know what it is, "woop woop" sounds a lot like "boo." So the band starts sitting down with every act before they go on. Shaggy: "We always put it in their contracts, too. If they feel they're in any kind of danger, get the fuck out of there." So by 2004 they have a system. Warn the act. Explain the crowd. Tell them they can leave.
Keep that system in mind. It's going to come back.
And then the woods kick them out too. After 2005, the quarry park's owner says no more. In 2006 they get booked at a Michigan racetrack, and the local township denies the permits. Violent J: "And they kicked us out two weeks before." They land at a ranch in Ohio, and he calls it "the only year in my opinion that has a black eye on it."
Convention centers, no. Toledo, no. The quarry, no. Michigan, no. They need somebody, anybody, who doesn't care.
[TALK: How many times do you get kicked out of places before you go, okay, maybe it's us?]

## CAVE-IN-ROCK
[CLIP: Clowns, river pirates and a biker camp]
Cave-In-Rock, Illinois. I'm from Chicagoland, and I'll be honest, I had to look at a map. It's at the very bottom of the state, on the Ohio River, about six hours south of Chicago. Across the water is Kentucky, and you get there by ferry.
And the town is named after an actual cave in the riverbank. Around 1800, that cave was a hideout for river pirates. A guy named Samuel Mason ran a tavern out of it to lure in travelers coming down the river. Hollywood shot part of "How the West Was Won" there in 1962, with the cave playing a pirate hideout.
So the town's whole history is "people show up by water and things get weird." The clowns fit right in.
Just outside town is a place called Hogrock. It's a campground that hosts a biker rally every year, owned by a guy named Tim York, who goes by Hog Daddy. And in 2007, Hog Daddy says yes. Shaggy told Spin what that felt like: "After the year before, it was just such a relief." And then, "it's private grounds, not even police allowed on it for shit." Violent J said the owners were "so good to us, they were like, we don't give a fuck about the controversy, you guys are the shit."
Thousands of Juggalos, and the town is a few hundred people.
The town did not all agree. Vice sent Thomas Morton that first year, and he wrote that a local minister had led a campaign to ban the Juggalos from Cave-In-Rock, which "ended up doing little more than providing fodder for the local paper." It turned the paper's message board into, his words, "a hilarious shouting match between residents." Two restaurants put up signs saying they'd be closed for the exact dates of the Gathering.
But not everybody was mad. The next year, a shopkeeper told the Southern Illinoisan, "They were nice kids from all over the world." And, "They spent a lot of money here, and we can use all the business we can get."
And what did they spend it on? Vice listed the schedule, running from one in the afternoon to six in the morning. Wrestling, autograph sessions, movie screenings, a magic show, a talent show, poker tournaments, carnival rides, helicopter rides. The Village Voice reported Psychopathic Records bought Faygo "by the truckload." There were Faygo sno-cones. There was a dunk tank with, quote, "a mean clown, of course" inside.
In 2009, for the tenth one, Violent J said, "we went farther and booked more bands than we ever had." Ice Cube. Scarface. Over twenty thousand people, the biggest Gathering ever, in a town you need a ferry to leave. Their infomercial for it, with helicopter rides and guys on stilts, got so famous that Saturday Night Live did a parody.
[FILL: Cave-In-Rock got its money and its traffic, and the Gathering got the one thing nobody else would give it: a yes.]
And now the straight part, because it's real. A festival that size, with that much partying, means people got hurt. In 2013, a Hardin County deputy reported ten overdoses in the first two full days. People have died at Gatherings. They had families, and none of that is a joke.
[Pause.]

## MIRACLES
So by 2010 the Gathering is huge, but the rest of the world still mostly doesn't know or care. And then the clowns put out a video.
It's called "Miracles." It came out in April 2010. It's a slow, sincere song where they list off the everyday stuff that blows their minds. Giraffes. Rainbows. Water, fire, air and dirt. And then the famous one: magnets. They want to know how magnets work, and they make it real clear they don't want a scientist explaining it to them.
The internet lost its mind. On April 17th, 2010, Saturday Night Live did a parody of it. And the clowns' response? They liked it, and asked to be on the show.
That fall, Violent J told the Village Voice the joke was on the people laughing: "They know we don't really not know how magnets work!"
I believe him. I think.

But here's why "Miracles" matters for this story. In the summer of 2010, the whole internet is suddenly paying attention to the clowns. Reporters from New York are driving to southern Illinois. So whatever happens at that summer's Gathering, everybody's going to hear about it.

## JUGGALETTES' REVENGE
[CLIP: The bag of chicken tenderloin]
August 2010. The eleventh Gathering. And the label wants to try something new.
The Village Voice's Camille Dodero explained it. Here's her reporting, word for word: "To counter the criticism that Insane Clown Posse's catalog and attitude encourage misogyny, Psychopathic Records wanted, finally after a decade, to establish a female face. So they promoted the Tila Tequila lineup, which Lil' Kim was supposed to headline, as the "Juggalettes' Revenge.""
Juggalettes are the women in the Juggalo world. So the idea is a night for them. Nice idea.
And the name they put on it is Tila Tequila. She was a reality TV star, famous for a MySpace page and a dating show on MTV. And the crowd was not in her corner. Dodero again: "A fever pitch of promises had already been made, via Twitter and Juggalo forums, to heckle her, to spray her with Faygo."
So it's public. It's on the forums. Everybody knows.
And the band knew. Violent J told Pollstar, "We told her about the rumor and she didn't care." The label's statement afterward said she "was made aware, in advance, of her unpopularity with many Juggalos at the Gathering." It said she was "paid in full before her performance, and was told that she did not have to perform." And it said the "Gathering's head of security told her that it was unsafe to go on stage."
Read that again. The head of security, the guy whose whole job is keeping people safe, told her the stage was unsafe. And the stage stayed on the schedule.
That night, a rapper named Ill E. Gal goes on right before her and gets a warm reception. Then Tila comes out. Nathan Rabin from the A.V. Club was there. He wrote that she "came on several hours late surrounded by security guards with linebacker physiques." And as she performed, she "ducked behind an ever-increasing ring of security guards."
Because stuff was flying. According to the Village Voice, the crowd threw "rocks, feces, bottles, cans, sex toys, and a bag of chicken tenderloin."
A bag of chicken tenderloin. Somebody packed that for a four day campout and decided this was its best use.
Now let me be straight. She got hurt. Here's how she described it to TMZ: "dudes were throwing huge stone rocks in my face, beer bottles that slit my eye open." Rabin, from where he was standing, said it looked like surface cuts and bruises. Either way, she was bleeding on a stage, and nobody deserves that. Security finally pulled her off and drove her off the grounds.
[Pause.]
And the people who threw rocks are on the people who threw rocks. Thousands of people were at that show, and Violent J said the same thing about Method Man, who got cut by a can the next night: "It was one asshole." I'll believe that most of that crowd booed and went back to their Faygo.
She called it disgusting and said she was going to sue. She never did.

## THAT GUY
[CLIP: That Guy verdict]
Okay. Who booked Tila Tequila at the Gathering? Time to name That Guy.
Suspect one: whoever threw the rocks. Guilty of throwing rocks, obviously. But they didn't book anybody.
Suspect two: the convention centers, Toledo, the quarry, Michigan. Everybody who said no, and pushed this whole thing into the woods, past the last ferry, onto private ground where nobody's in charge but the clowns. They didn't throw anything. But they made sure the clowns had nobody to answer to but themselves.
And suspect three: the clowns. Insane Clown Posse and their label, Psychopathic Records.
Here's the thing. These guys had a system. Since the woods, they'd been sitting down with every act and warning them about the crowd. They put it in the contracts. They had a whole award named after a guy who got booed off. They knew exactly what this crowd does to an act it doesn't want, better than anybody alive.
And they still booked her. They read the forums. They told her the rumor. Their own head of security said it was unsafe. And their answer was to pay her early and tell her she could leave whenever she wanted. Violent J told the Village Voice afterward, "We thought Tila Tequila would turn it out." In the same breath, he said, "we didn't know that was gonna happen."
They did know. They told her.
So That Guy is Insane Clown Posse.

## WHERE ARE THEY NOW
The Gathering stayed at Hogrock through 2013. Then it left. The Hardin County sheriff, Jerry Fricker, told the Associated Press the "downfall" was them "not filling their contract with Tim York and their contractors out there." The label didn't explain.
Then a town in Missouri said yes for 2014, and after the locals pushed back, it said no. Classic Gathering. They ended up back in Ohio for years.
And this August, 2026, the Gathering went to Macks Creek, Missouri, in the Ozarks. Still going. More than twenty five years after a twelve year old's dad filled his car with Faygo in a parking lot in Novi.
Violent J revealed at the 2021 Gathering that he has heart failure. They called the next tour the farewell tour. They're still playing shows.
And in Cave-In-Rock, the cave is still there. So is Hogrock. The bikers still come every summer.
