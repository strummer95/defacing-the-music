## COLD OPEN
July 21st, 1989. Lake Compounce, an amusement park in Bristol, Connecticut. A summer night. MTV is filming.
The hottest pop act in America is on stage. Two guys with long braids and the best dance moves in the business. The crowd is screaming every word of their big hit, "Girl You Know It's True."
And then the song hiccups.
"Girl, you know it's..." "Girl, you know it's..." "Girl, you know it's..."
[Pause.]
Over and over. Like a CD in a car going over a pothole.
And the two guys on stage are standing there with microphones in their hands. And their mouths aren't moving.
## TODAY
Today: a German producer with a secret, two dancers with a dream, three number one singles, a Grammy, a backing track with a mind of its own, a press conference where everybody finally sang, and the only Grammy ever taken back.
Also, refunds. America got refunds. For feelings.
And somewhere in this story, there's a guy. There always is. And I'll warn you now, it's not who the jokes were about.
I'm Duck, and this is Defacing the Music.

## WHO IS FRANK FARIAN
To understand Milli Vanilli, you have to start with a guy who isn't in Milli Vanilli.
Frank Farian. German record producer. And in the seventies, Frank Farian builds a group called Boney M. You know Boney M even if you think you don't. "Rasputin." "Daddy Cool." Every wedding DJ in Europe still owes this man a fruit basket.
Boney M had a guy named Bobby Farrell. Great dancer. Tall, skinny, all knees and elbows, the face of the group on TV. And here's the thing. On the records, the deep male voice wasn't Bobby. It was Frank. Farian sang the male parts in the studio, and Bobby performed them on camera.
[Pause.]
And this was not a big secret in the business. People knew.
[FILL: So Frank had already run this play once. It worked. It worked for years. When something works, you don't stop. That's how you end up with a fourth Fast and Furious.]
So file that away. Frank Farian has a template. A great voice in the studio, and a great-looking performer in front of the camera. And he does not see a problem with that.

## WHO ARE ROB AND FAB
Now let's meet the guys in front of the camera.
Rob Pilatus. His mother was a German dancer, his father an African American serviceman. And Rob spent his first four years in an orphanage in Bavaria, in Oberammergau, before a family in Munich adopted him. Growing up Black in Germany in the sixties and seventies, he said he got bullied, called names, made to feel like he didn't belong.
Fab Morvan. Fabrice. From Paris. Moved to Germany at eighteen.
And the two of them meet in the Munich club scene. The stories differ on exactly where. One version is a dance class at a club. Another says they first crossed paths in Los Angeles and reconnected in Munich. But everybody agrees on what they were. Dancers. Models. Two young guys who looked like stars before anybody knew their names.
And they want to be a group. They want to sing.
[RIFF: being twenty-something and knowing you're going to be famous. Or use this:]
Everybody in music has been that guy. You're twenty-two, you've got the hair, you've got the outfit, you've got the moves, and you know, you just know, that somebody important is going to walk through that door. And for most of us, nobody does. For Rob and Fab, somebody did.

## THE DEAL
That somebody is Frank Farian.
Around 1988, Farian has a batch of songs recorded. Studio singers. Good ones. A rapper named Charles Shaw, a singer named Brad Howell, plus others. What Farian wants is a face. Somebody who looks like the music sounds.
So he signs Rob and Fab.
And here's a detail that matters later. By their own account, they signed without taking any legal advice. Two young guys, a famous producer, a stack of paper. Sign here.
And at some point, it becomes clear. They're not going to be singing on the record. They're going to be the record.
[Pause.]
Now, where'd the name come from? The story goes that it came from a trip to Turkey, where they saw a slogan that sounded like "Milli Vanilli." I can't nail down the details, so that one goes in the "the story goes" pile. Which, honestly, is the right pile for this whole band.
[FILL: It's a great name, though. You can't say "Milli Vanilli" angry. Try it. You can't. It comes out like a kid's game.]

## AMERICA
The record comes out in Europe first. Then it crosses over to America on Arista Records, under the title "Girl You Know It's True."
And it explodes.
"Girl You Know It's True" goes to number two on the Billboard Hot 100. Then "Baby Don't Forget My Number" goes to number one. "Girl I'm Gonna Miss You," number one. "Blame It on the Rain," number one. Every single from that album made the top five.
By January 1990, the album is certified six times platinum. Six million copies in America.
And the videos are everywhere. The braids. The outfits. The dancing. Rob and Fab are on MTV every hour. They are the look of 1989.
[RIFF: what 1989 looked like. Or use this:]
If you were alive in 1989, you saw those two guys more than you saw your own family. Somebody in your house had a cassette. Somebody's older sister had the poster. Your gym teacher played it during the dodgeball unit.

## THE SKIP
So now they're huge. And when you're huge, you tour. Which, if you don't sing, is a problem.
July 21st, 1989. They're on MTV's Club MTV tour, playing Lake Compounce, an amusement park in Bristol, Connecticut.
And they're doing what pop acts did a lot back then, honestly. Dancing hard and performing to a track.
And then the track skips.
The line "Girl, you know it's" starts looping. Over and over. Stuck.
[Pause.]
Rob described it to MTV later. He said he couldn't keep repeating it, fifteen times, so he panicked, stopped, and went off stage.
And here's the side character. MTV's "Downtown" Julie Brown, the VJ, who's hosting this tour. According to her, she chased Rob off stage and talked him into going back out to finish the show.
[FILL: That's the "according to her" pile. It's her story from a TV special years later. I believe it, I just can't prove it.]
And the wild part? That night didn't end Milli Vanilli. The show went on. The records kept selling. The truth didn't fully come out for more than a year.
But Rob knew. He said later that he knew right then and there, it was the beginning of the end.
[RIFF: a gear failure on stage, the moment your stomach drops. Or use this:]
Every musician has had the moment. The amp cuts out. The click track goes. Something fails, and for one second, the whole room can see exactly how the trick works. For most of us, that's a bad night. For Rob, it was the trick.

## THE MOUTH
Now, here's where it gets hard to watch.
Because after the skip, Rob and Fab didn't get quieter. They got louder.
In an interview with Time magazine, Rob, twenty-four at the time, said this, and I'm quoting Time: "Musically, we are more talented than any Bob Dylan. Musically, we are more talented than Paul McCartney."
[Pause.]
And then he said Mick Jagger's lines are not clear.
[RIFF: a guy who didn't sing on his own record dissing Paul McCartney. Or use this:]
That's a guy who didn't sing on his own album telling a national magazine that he's better than Paul McCartney. Paul McCartney wrote "Yesterday." Rob didn't sing "Blame It on the Rain." I need the scoreboard on this one.
But think about it from where Rob's sitting. Kid from an orphanage, bullied, finally the most famous guy in the room. Holding a secret that could take it all away. Some people get quiet when they're scared. Some people start yelling about Bob Dylan.

## THE GRAMMY
February 21st, 1990. The Grammys. Milli Vanilli wins Best New Artist.
They go up. They take the trophy. They thank people.
Best New Artist. For two guys who, as the whole world would find out, were not the artist.
[Pause.]
And some people already had questions. Because back in December 1989, one of those studio guys, Charles Shaw, the rapper, told Newsday that he was one of the real voices on the album, and that the guys on the cover were impostors.
And then he took it back.
Now, the widely repeated version is that Farian paid Shaw a hundred fifty thousand dollars to retract it. That number gets repeated everywhere, usually with an "allegedly" next to it, and I've never seen hard proof. So it goes in the "reportedly" pile.
[FILL: A guy tells the newspaper the truth, takes it back, and the lie keeps going for almost another year. That's how big the money was.]

## THE CONFESSION
So what finally breaks it?
The usual version goes like this. Rob and Fab want to sing on the next album. For real. With their own voices. And Frank Farian isn't having it.
In November 1990, Farian goes public. He admits that Rob and Fab didn't sing on the record.
[Pause.]
And then the whole world turns on Rob and Fab. Not on the producer. Not on the label. On the two guys with the braids.
Within days, the Grammy organization takes the award back. Best New Artist, revoked. And to this day, it's the only time the Grammys have ever done that. One time. Ever. Thousands of awards, and these are the guys.
[FILL: Reportedly, the duo had already said they'd give it back on their own. And they asked that it go to the singers who actually sang on the album. That didn't happen.]
Then Rob and Fab do a press conference in Los Angeles. More than two hundred reporters show up. And at one point, the two of them sing. Live. A cappella. A few bars of "Girl You Know It's True."
[Pause.]
The first time a lot of people ever heard their real voices was the day they got caught.
[RIFF: the press conference. Two guys singing for a room full of reporters who came to watch them die. Or use this:]
Imagine having to audition for the whole world, on the worst day of your life, in a room full of people who already decided you're a punchline.

## THE REFUNDS
And then America did the most American thing possible. America sued.
Class action lawsuits. People wanted their money back. For an album they listened to, liked, and danced to, for over a year.
The settlement came out of a court in Chicago, our town, and an estimated ten million buyers were eligible. And the numbers in the settlement, as reported, were about a buck back for a single, two for a cassette or record, three for a CD.
[Pause.]
And you got to keep the album.
[FILL: So the song still sounds exactly the same. You just know who's singing it now. And for that, three dollars.]
Arista dropped them and pulled the album out of print. One of the biggest-selling records of its time, just gone from the catalog.
[RIFF: what's the album you'd want a refund for. Or use this:]
I've bought records that made me feel lied to. Not one of them ever sent me three dollars.

## WHAT HAPPENED NEXT
Fab kept going. He kept singing. Live, with his own voice, for decades. He ended up with the rights to the Milli Vanilli name. In 2023 there was a documentary, "Milli Vanilli," on Paramount Plus, that let the two of them tell their side.
And then, and I love this, Fab wrote a memoir called "You Know It's True." He read the audiobook himself. And for the 2026 Grammys, that audiobook got nominated.
[Pause.]
Thirty-five years after they took the Grammy away, the Grammys nominated Fab Morvan for reading his own words, out loud, in his own voice.
He lost. To the Dalai Lama.
[FILL: That's fine. You can lose to the Dalai Lama. Nobody's mad at you for losing to the Dalai Lama.]
Frank Farian died in January 2024 at his home in Miami. He was eighty-two.

## THE SERIOUS PART
Rob Pilatus died in April 1998, in a hotel near Frankfurt, from an accidental overdose of alcohol and prescription drugs. He was in his early thirties.
[Pause.]
[RIFF: a few words for Rob. Keep it straight. Or use this:]
He was a kid who wanted to sing, and when he finally got to, nobody was listening anymore.

## THAT GUY
Okay. Time to name him. That Guy.
The suspects.
Suspect one: Rob and Fab. They knew. They took the deal, took the stage, took the Grammy, and Rob told Time he was better than Paul McCartney. They're not innocent.
Suspect two: the record business. Arista, the label, pushing a record to six million people. MTV, putting them in every living room. And the Grammys, who handed them the award and then took it back like none of them had ever heard of a studio singer. That's a whole office full of guys, and every one of them made money.
Suspect three: Frank Farian. The guy who'd already done this with Boney M. The guy who had the singers and the dancers, put them together, and kept the secret until the dancers wanted to sing.
[Pause.]
And the verdict.
Frank Farian. Rob and Fab carried the blame for thirty years, but Farian built the machine, and he'd built it before. And when it came out, two young guys who signed without a lawyer took the hit, while the guy with the plan went on making records.
The "that guy is usually awesome" part: the songs were good. They still are. "Blame It on the Rain" comes on, you know every word. Farian knew how to make a hit. He just didn't think the people singing it mattered.
Now, to be fair, this is disputed. Plenty of people will tell you Rob and Fab were grown men who knew exactly what they signed up for. And that's not wrong.
[RIFF: your verdict. Farian, the duo, or the whole business? Or use this:]
Rob and Fab lip-synced. Fine. Frank Farian lip-synced an entire group, twice, and nobody took his trophies away.
