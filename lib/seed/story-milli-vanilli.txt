## WHO IS FRANK FARIAN
[CLIP: Frank Farian already did this with Boney M]
Before Milli Vanilli, the same producer had already put a dancer in front of his own voice, and it worked for years.
Frank Farian. German record producer. In the seventies, Frank Farian builds a group called Boney M. You know Boney M even if you think you don't. "Rasputin." "Daddy Cool." Every wedding DJ in Europe still owes this man a fruit basket.
Boney M had a guy named Bobby Farrell. Great dancer. Tall, skinny, all knees and elbows, the face of the group on TV. And on the records, the deep male voice wasn't Bobby. It was Frank. Farian sang the male parts in the studio, and Bobby performed them on camera.
And this was not a big secret in the business. People knew.
[FILL: So Frank had already run this play once. It worked. When something works, you don't stop. That's how you end up with a fourth Fast and Furious.]
So Frank Farian has a template. A great voice in the studio, and a great-looking performer in front of the camera. And he does not see a problem with that.

## WHO ARE ROB AND FAB
The two faces of Milli Vanilli were Rob and Fab.
Rob Pilatus. His mother was a German dancer, his father an American serviceman. Rob spent his first four years in an orphanage in Bavaria, in Oberammergau, before a family in Munich adopted him. He said he got bullied as a kid and made to feel like he didn't belong.
Fab Morvan. Fabrice. From Paris. Moved to Germany at eighteen.
The two of them meet in the Munich club scene. The stories differ on exactly where. But everybody agrees on what they were. Dancers. Models. Two young guys who looked like stars before anybody knew their names.
And they want to be a group. They want to sing.
[RIFF: being twenty-something and knowing you're going to be famous. Or use this:]
Everybody in music has been that guy. You're twenty-two, you've got the hair, you've got the outfit, you've got the moves, and you know, you just know, that somebody important is going to walk through that door. And for most of us, nobody does. For Rob and Fab, somebody did.

## THE DEAL
That somebody is Frank Farian.
Around 1988, Farian has a batch of songs recorded. Studio singers. Good ones. A rapper named Charles Shaw, a singer named Brad Howell, another singer named John Davis, plus others. What Farian wants is a face. Somebody who looks like the music sounds.
So he signs Rob and Fab. And by their own account, they signed without taking any legal advice. Two young guys, a famous producer, a stack of paper. Sign here.
Fab's version, years later, to VladTV: "We walked into a trap, not knowing it was a trap." He says the first time Farian played them "Girl You Know It's True," he played it as an instrumental. The vocals were already recorded, by somebody else. Fab says by the time they understood, they owed Farian money and couldn't walk away until they paid him back.
And Farian's version of how big he thought this was going to be, to the Los Angeles Times: "It was a crazy idea. I thought, OK, it's just for discotheques and clubs."
Now, where'd the name come from? Early on, the story was that "Milli Vanilli" meant "positive energy" in Turkish. Other accounts say it came from a nightclub in Berlin. So the name goes in the "the story goes" pile. Which, honestly, is the right pile for this whole band.
[FILL: It's a great name, though. You can't say "Milli Vanilli" angry. Try it. You can't. It comes out like a kid's game.]

## AMERICA
The Milli Vanilli record comes out in Europe first. Then it crosses over to America on Arista Records, under the title "Girl You Know It's True."
And it explodes.
"Girl You Know It's True" goes to number two on the Billboard Hot 100. Then "Baby Don't Forget My Number" goes to number one. "Girl I'm Gonna Miss You," number one. "Blame It on the Rain," number one. Every single from that album made the top five.
By January 1990, the album is certified six times platinum. Six million copies in America.
And the videos are everywhere. The braids. The outfits. The dancing. Rob and Fab are on MTV every hour. They are the look of 1989.
[RIFF: what 1989 looked like. Or use this:]
If you were alive in 1989, you saw those two guys more than you saw your own family. Somebody in your house had a cassette. Somebody's older sister had the poster. Your gym teacher played it during the dodgeball unit.

## THE SKIP
So now they're huge. And when you're huge, you tour. Which, if you don't sing, is a problem.
July 21st, 1989. Milli Vanilli is on MTV's Club MTV tour, playing Lake Compounce, an amusement park in Bristol, Connecticut.
And they're doing what pop acts did a lot back then, honestly. Dancing hard and performing to a track.
And then the track skips.
The line "Girl, you know it's" starts looping. Over and over. Stuck. Like a CD in a car going over a pothole, except the pothole never ends.
And Rob and Fab are standing up there with microphones in their hands, in front of a crowd that was singing every word along with them about ten seconds ago.
[Pause.]
Here's Rob, later: "When my voice got stuck in the computer, and it just kept repeating and repeating, I panicked. I didn't know what to do. I just ran off the stage."
My voice. Stuck in the computer. He called it his voice.
And MTV's "Downtown" Julie Brown, the VJ hosting the tour, said years later on VH1's Behind the Music that she chased Rob off stage and talked him into going back out to finish the show.
And the wild part? That night didn't end Milli Vanilli. Most of the crowd barely noticed. The show went on. The records kept selling. The truth didn't fully come out for more than a year.
But Rob knew. "I knew right then and there, it was the beginning of the end for Milli Vanilli."
[RIFF: a gear failure on stage, the moment your stomach drops. Or use this:]
Every musician has had the moment. The amp cuts out. The click track goes. Something fails, and for one second, the whole room can see exactly how the trick works. For most of us, that's a bad night. For Rob, it was the trick.

## THE MOUTH
[CLIP: More talented than Paul McCartney]
A guy who didn't sing a note on his own album told Time magazine he was more talented than Paul McCartney.
After the skip, Rob and Fab didn't get quieter. They got louder. Here's Rob, twenty-four, in Time: "Musically, we are more talented than any Bob Dylan. Musically, we are more talented than Paul McCartney."
[Pause.]
And then he said Mick Jagger's lines are not clear.
Paul McCartney wrote "Yesterday." Rob didn't sing "Blame It on the Rain." I need the scoreboard on this one.
But think about it from where Rob's sitting. Kid from an orphanage, bullied, finally the most famous guy in the room, holding a secret that could take it all away. Some people get quiet when they're scared. Some people start yelling about Bob Dylan.

## THE GRAMMY
February 21st, 1990. The Grammys. Best New Artist.
[CLIP: Praying not to win the Grammy]
Milli Vanilli rode to the Grammys praying they would lose. Fab told Yahoo, in 2013, that in the limo on the way to the Shrine Auditorium, they were both praying for somebody else. His words: "Let's hope that it's Indigo Girls or Tone Lōc, or Soul II Soul, but let it not be us."
"But of course: 'And the winner is… Milli Vanilli!' And that was shocking. Because suddenly, the world was watching closer."
[Pause.]
They lost the one award they were rooting to lose.
And people already had questions. Back in December 1989, one of the studio guys, Charles Shaw, the rapper, told Newsday he was one of the real voices on the album, and the guys on the cover were impostors.
And then he took it back.
The widely repeated version is that Farian paid Shaw a hundred fifty thousand dollars to retract it. In today's money, that's about three hundred ninety thousand. That number always comes with an "allegedly," and I've never seen hard proof. So it goes in the "reportedly" pile.
[FILL: A guy tells the newspaper the truth, takes it back, and the lie keeps going for almost another year. That's how big the money was.]

## THE CONFESSION
So what finally breaks Milli Vanilli?
Rob and Fab want to sing on the next album. For real. With their own voices. And they push. Frank Farian isn't having it.
In November 1990, Farian goes public. He fires them and admits that Rob and Fab didn't sing on the record. And he doesn't stop there. To the Los Angeles Times, about Rob: "I've never heard such a bad singer. They wanted to sing. They wanted to write songs. It never happened. They went instead to discos 'til 4 a.m. and slept all day. All they ever really did was party. Someone who lives like that can't make good music."
That's the guy who hired them to dance, mad that they danced.
And Rob, to the LA Times reporter Chuck Phillips: "I feel like a mosquito being squeezed. The last two years of our lives have been a total nightmare. We've had to lie to everybody. We are true singers, but that maniac Frank Farian would never allow us to express ourselves."
And then the whole world turns on Rob and Fab. Not on the producer. Not on the label. On the two guys with the braids.
Within days, the Grammy gets taken back. Best New Artist, revoked. To this day, it's the only time the Grammys have ever done that. Thousands of awards, and these are the guys.
And Fab says the giving-back part was their idea. "The Grammy was at our house. It could have stayed at our house. But we made the choice to give it back."
Then Rob and Fab do a press conference in Los Angeles. More than a hundred reporters show up. And at one point, the two of them sing. Live. To prove they can.
[Pause.]
The first time a lot of people ever heard their real voices was the day they got caught.
Fab, on that room: "All those journalists were just out for blood. They were really there to hang us dry."
[TALK: Pat, worst room you've ever had to walk into and perform for. Does it come close?]

## THE REFUNDS
[CLIP: America sued for refunds]
Milli Vanilli fans sued to get their money back for an album they'd been dancing to for over a year. And they won.
At least twenty-six lawsuits. And the settlement came out of a court in Cook County, right here, our town. About ten million buyers were eligible. A buck back for a single. Two for a cassette or a record. Three for a CD. In today's money, that's about seven bucks. And if you could prove you went to a concert, five percent of the ticket price.
[Pause.]
And you got to keep the album.
And the judge, signing off on it, said he hoped to get the case out of the way so everybody could "devote their time to the resolution of controversies of a more significant social and economic nature."
That's a Cook County judge going, "Please. I have real cases."
Arista dropped them and pulled the album out of print. One of the biggest-selling records of its time, just gone from the catalog.
[RIFF: what's the album you'd want a refund for. Or use this:]
I've bought records that made me feel lied to. Not one of them ever sent me three dollars.

## AFTER THE FALL
And here's how embarrassing it got.
Farian put out another album with the real singers, under the name The Real Milli Vanilli. And according to Grunge's history of the band, one of the guys in that group was dressed and made up to look like Rob.
[Pause.]
He fired Rob, and then he hired a Rob.
Rob and Fab went the other way. They did a gum commercial, playing themselves, lip-syncing to opera until the tape skipped. They got in on the joke. Good for them.
Then they made a real album, with their own voices. Called it "Rob and Fab." 1993. It sold about two thousand copies in the U.S.
Six million copies when somebody else sang. Two thousand when they did.
And Rob took it the hardest. Fab told the BBC that they both fell into drugs after the scandal. In 1991, police had to talk Rob down from a ninth-floor balcony. He was in and out of rehab, and served time in jail. By 1997, the two of them barely spoke.

## THE SERIOUS PART
And then they made up. Rob and Fab got back in the studio together for a comeback album. It was called "Back and in Attack."
On the eve of the promotional tour, in April 1998, Rob Pilatus was found dead in a hotel near Frankfurt, from an accidental overdose of alcohol and prescription drugs. He was in his early thirties.
"Back and in Attack" was never released.
Fab: "When I heard the news of Rob's death, everything went blank at that moment. Silence. It was like losing a part of myself."
[Pause.]
[RIFF: a few words for Rob. Keep it straight. Or use this:]
He was a kid who wanted to sing, and when he finally got to, nobody was listening anymore.

## WHERE ARE THEY NOW
Fab kept going. He kept singing. Live, with his own voice, for decades. He ended up with the rights to the Milli Vanilli name. In 2023 there was a documentary, "Milli Vanilli," on Paramount Plus, that let him tell their side.
And then, and I love this, Fab wrote a memoir called "You Know It's True." He read the audiobook himself. And for the 2026 Grammys, that audiobook got nominated.
Thirty-five years after they gave the Grammy back, the Grammys nominated Fab Morvan for reading his own words, out loud, in his own voice.
He lost. To the Dalai Lama.
[FILL: That's fine. You can lose to the Dalai Lama. Nobody's mad at you for losing to the Dalai Lama.]
And Frank Farian? He kept right on making hits. In the nineties he was behind acts like La Bouche and No Mercy. Frank Farian died in January 2024 at his home in Miami. He was eighty-two.

## THAT GUY
[CLIP: That Guy verdict]
Okay. Time to name him. The Milli Vanilli That Guy.
The suspects.
Suspect one: Rob and Fab. They knew. They took the deal, took the stage, took the Grammy, and Rob told Time he was better than Paul McCartney. They're not innocent.
Suspect two: the record business. Arista, pushing a record to six million people. MTV, putting them in every living room. And the Grammys, who handed them the award and then took it back like none of them had ever heard of a studio singer.
Suspect three: Frank Farian. The guy who'd already done this with Boney M. The guy who had the singers and the dancers, put them together, and kept the secret until the dancers wanted to sing.
And the verdict.
Frank Farian. Rob and Fab carried the blame for thirty years, but Farian built the machine, and he'd built it before. When it came out, two young guys who signed without a lawyer took the hit, while the guy with the plan went on making records. With a Rob lookalike.
